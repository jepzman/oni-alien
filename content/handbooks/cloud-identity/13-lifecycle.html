Joiners, movers and leavers

Access is easy to grant and easy to forget. People join and need the right tools on day one; they change teams and quietly keep their old access; they leave and, in too many companies, stay in a few systems for weeks. This chapter connects the HR system, the identity provider and every app so that access follows the person automatically, and sets up the reviews that catch whatever automation misses.

By the end you can

  • Describe the joiner–mover–leaver lifecycle, with HR as the source of truth.
  • Design birthright access from department and role, and requests for everything else.
  • Explain SCIM provisioning and connect an identity provider to IAM Identity Center.
  • Run a leaver process that ends sessions, not just accounts.
  • Run access reviews that people actually complete.

At Larder

In May 2027, Lena, Larder's data protection officer, reviews who can see customer data. Most of the answers are reassuring. One isn't: Pim, a customer support agent who left on 30 April, could still sign in to the helpdesk and to the billing tool, where support agents issue refunds, on 21 May. Three weeks, with access to customers' order histories and the power to send money.

AWS wasn't the problem: since January, Larder's engineers sign in through Identity Center (Chapter 12), and Eli removes leavers the same day. But Larder has 14 staff tools, and each has its own accounts, created and deleted by whoever remembers. Pim's manager had told IT. IT had closed the email account. Nobody owned the other twelve. Section 13.9 shows what Larder changed.

13.1The lifecycle

Every person's access goes through three kinds of event: they join, they move (change team, role, location or manager), and they leave. The aim of lifecycle management is that each event changes access everywhere, automatically, from one place. That place should be where the event is first recorded: the HR system, which knows about a new hire before IT does, and about a resignation before anyone touches an account.

Two different protocols are involved, and both are needed. SAML or OpenID Connect handles sign-in: the app trusts the IdP to say who's there (Chapter 4, Chapter 5). SCIM handles provisioning: the IdP tells the app who exists, which groups they're in, and when they're gone (13.5). Sign-in alone stops a leaver logging in again; it doesn't remove their account, their group memberships or their existing sessions.

13.2Joiners: birthright access, and requests for the rest

Split access into two kinds:

  • Birthright access: what everyone in a department or role needs from day one, derived automatically from HR attributes. Every support agent gets the helpdesk and the billing tool's refund role; every engineer gets ViewOnly in production and Developer in staging (Chapter 12). Rules, not decisions.
  • Requested access: anything beyond that, asked for by the person, approved by an owner, and ideally time-limited (Chapter 14).

Keep birthright access small. Every item on it is given to everyone, forever, without anyone deciding. Day-one readiness then comes from HR entering the start date early: the account exists, with the right groups, before the person arrives.

13.3Movers: the forgotten event

Joiners get attention because they can't work without access. Leavers get attention because auditors ask. Movers get neither, so they're where privilege creep comes from (Chapter 6): someone moves from support to operations, gains the stock tool, and keeps the refund role. Nobody complains, because extra access never stops anyone working.

With birthright access derived from attributes, a move is just an attribute change: the old department's groups go, the new one's arrive. Allow a short, deliberate overlap if handovers need it (a week, with an end date), never an open one. Requested access should be re-confirmed on a move, by the new manager.

13.4Leavers: ending sessions, not just accounts

Disabling someone at the identity provider stops new sign-ins everywhere it's used. It does not immediately stop everything they already have:

  • Sessions already open in each app last until they expire: a browser session, a refresh token on a phone (Chapter 3), an Identity Center role session of up to its permission set's duration.
  • Accounts in apps without SCIM stay active. Pim's helpdesk and billing accounts were exactly this.
  • Secrets the person knew or made: shared passwords, API keys they created, keys on their laptop.
  • Things they owned: scheduled jobs, resources, the only copy of a runbook.
  1. HR records the leave date; the IdP disables the account at the end of the last day, automatically.
  2. SCIM deactivates the person in every connected app within minutes.
  3. Revoke active sessions where it matters: in the IdP, in apps that support it, and in Identity Center, whose console lists a user's sessions and can end them. That stops the access portal from issuing new credentials; role credentials it already issued still work until they expire. For an urgent case, AWS's documented method is a deny-everything statement in your permission sets (or an SCP) with a condition on the person's identitystore:UserId, which takes effect for sessions already running.
  4. Tickets for the rest: each app without SCIM gets a task, owned by that app's owner, due the same day.
  5. Rotate what they knew, transfer what they owned, recover devices.
  6. Verify the next day: a report of the person's status in every app. Anything still active is an incident.

Watch out: a hostile departure

When someone leaves on bad terms, the order flips: disable and revoke sessions first, at the moment they're told, then do the rest. Short session durations for powerful access (Chapter 12) are what make this fast.

13.5SCIM

SCIM (System for Cross-domain Identity Management, RFC 7643 and 7644) is a small REST API that apps expose so an identity provider can manage their users and groups. The IdP calls it whenever something changes:

  • POST /Users creates a user; POST /Groups a group.
  • PATCH /Users/{id} changes attributes, including deactivation; PATCH /Groups/{id} adds or removes members.
  • DELETE removes a user or group entirely; many IdPs deactivate first and delete later.

To connect an IdP to IAM Identity Center, you change Identity Center's identity source to external identity provider, exchange SAML metadata (Chapter 5), and turn on automatic provisioning. Identity Center then gives you a SCIM endpoint and an access token for the IdP to use. Three things to know:

  • The SCIM token expires after a year. When it does, provisioning stops silently: new joiners get nothing and, worse, leavers aren't removed. Put the date in a calendar and rotate it early.
  • Provision groups, not just users, and keep assignments group-based (Chapter 12): then HR changes flow all the way through to AWS accounts.
  • Usernames must match when switching from Identity Center's own directory, so existing users are taken over rather than duplicated.

13.6Access reviews

Automation handles the rules. Reviews catch what the rules don't: requested access that outlived its reason, an assignment made by hand, a group that grew. An access review asks the person who knows, usually the manager or the resource owner, to confirm or remove each item, and records the answer.

Key idea

Automate what follows from HR; review what doesn't. A review that lists everyone's birthright access teaches people to click “approve all”.

Reviews people actually complete are short and pointed:

  • Review exceptions, not rules. Show requested and hand-made access, not what every support agent has.
  • Privileged access more often: monthly for production admin, quarterly for the rest.
  • Show usage. “Last used 140 days ago” makes the decision for the reviewer. IAM Access Analyzer's unused access findings and Identity Center's sign-in records give this for AWS (Chapter 23).
  • Default to removal when a reviewer doesn't answer, after a reminder. Unanswered reviews that keep access teach everyone that reviews don't matter.
Machines leave tooService accounts, API keys and roles have human owners. When an owner leaves or moves, ownership must move with the team, or the identity becomes an orphan nobody dares delete. Tag every workload identity with an owning team, not a person.

13.7Partners' people

The same lifecycle applies to people from customers and suppliers who use your systems. You can't see their HR system, so let their identity provider drive: federation for sign-in (Chapter 5) and, where they support it, SCIM from their IdP into yours, so their leavers disappear from your app when they leave their company. Where they don't, keep their sessions short and run an access review with the partner's own contact.

On Azure

Entra ID can take hires and leavers straight from HR systems (inbound provisioning), run lifecycle workflows (send a welcome, add to groups before day one, remove access on the last day) and schedule access reviews of groups, apps and privileged roles, with automatic removal when reviewers don't respond. These features are part of Entra ID Governance licensing.

On Google Cloud

If your source of truth is elsewhere (Entra ID, Okta, an LDAP directory), users and groups reach Cloud Identity by SCIM from the IdP or by Google Cloud Directory Sync; suspended users lose access to the console and APIs. The IAM role recommender suggests removing permissions a principal hasn't used in 90 days, which is good evidence for reviews.

13.9At Larder: one identity per person

At Larder: solved

Lena's finding became ADR 0021, accepted on 24 May 2027: one identity per person. Larder's company directory, which until then had mostly run email, became its identity provider for every staff tool, fed by the HR system. Departments and roles in HR now decide birthright groups. Eleven of the 14 tools support SAML or OpenID Connect plus SCIM, and are connected; the other three get an automatic leaver ticket, owned by each tool's owner, due the same day. Pim's two accounts were closed the afternoon Lena found them, and the billing tool's log showed they hadn't been used.

On 7 June, Eli switched Identity Center's identity source from its own directory to the IdP, with SCIM; usernames matched, so nobody noticed except the new joiners, who had AWS access on their first morning. The SCIM token's expiry is in the platform team's calendar. Managers now review requested and privileged access every quarter, and production admin monthly. Two more changes followed from the same decision: business customers' staff are federated from their own IdPs (as Korenwolf's office managers have been since December, Chapter 5), and pickers get personal badges by July instead of shared logins on store tablets.

13.10Lab: reconcile apps with HR

Lab 13 · What SCIM provisioning would do

You'll compare what HR says with what four apps contain, and produce the SCIM requests that would fix every difference: a joiner, a leaver, a mover and a group granted by hand. You need Python 3. Cost: nothing.

jml.pyPython 3.8+

# jml.py · joiners, movers and leavers: reconcile apps with HR, the way SCIM provisioning does
from datetime import date

TODAY = date(2027, 5, 21)

HR = {   # the source of truth: who works here, in which department, until when
    "ben":   {"dept": "engineering", "left": None},
    "noor":  {"dept": "support",     "left": None},                 # joined 17 May
    "pim":   {"dept": "support",     "left": date(2027, 4, 30)},    # left
    "tomas": {"dept": "operations",  "left": None},
    "yara":  {"dept": "operations",  "left": None},                 # moved from support on 3 May
}

RULES = {  # department → the groups each app should give them
    "engineering": {"aws": {"engineers"}},
    "support":     {"helpdesk": {"agents"}, "billing": {"refunds"}},
    "operations":  {"stock-tool": {"store-staff"}},
}

APPS = {   # what each app has today: user → groups
    "aws":        {"ben": {"engineers"}},
    "helpdesk":   {"pim": {"agents"}, "yara": {"agents"}},
    "billing":    {"pim": {"refunds"}, "yara": {"refunds"}},
    "stock-tool": {"tomas": {"store-staff", "rota-admin"}},     # rota-admin: granted by hand in March
}

def desired(app):
    want = {}
    for user, rec in HR.items():
        if rec["left"] is None or rec["left"] > TODAY:
            groups = RULES.get(rec["dept"], {}).get(app)
            if groups:
                want[user] = groups
    return want

def reconcile(app):
    have, want = APPS[app], desired(app)
    for user in sorted(want.keys() - have.keys()):
        yield f"POST   /Users                  {app}: create {user}@larder.example, groups {sorted(want[user])}"
    for user in sorted(have.keys() - want.keys()):
        rec = HR.get(user)
        why = (f"left {rec['left']}, {(TODAY - rec['left']).days} days ago" if rec and rec["left"]
               else "moved" if rec else "not in HR")
        yield f"PATCH  /Users/{user:<17} {app}: active=false  ({why})"
    for user in sorted(want.keys() & have.keys()):
        for g in sorted(want[user] - have[user]):
            yield f"PATCH  /Groups/{g:<16} {app}: add {user}"
        for g in sorted(have[user] - want[user]):
            yield f"PATCH  /Groups/{g:<16} {app}: remove {user}"

for app in APPS:
    for action in reconcile(app):
        print(action)

✓Chapter summary

  • Access changes at three events: join, move, leave. HR, where they're first recorded, should drive them.
  • Birthright access follows from department and role; everything else is requested, approved and time-limited.
  • Movers are where privilege creep comes from; derive groups from attributes so a move removes old access.
  • A leaver process must end sessions and handle apps without SCIM, secrets and ownership, then verify.
  • SCIM lets the IdP create, change and deactivate users and groups in apps, including IAM Identity Center; its token expires.
  • Access reviews catch what rules don't; keep them short, show usage, default to removal.

Key terms

Joiner, mover, leaver
The three lifecycle events that change a person's access.
Source of truth
The system whose record decides; for people, usually HR.
Birthright access
Access granted automatically from attributes such as department.
Provisioning
Creating, updating and removing accounts and group memberships in apps.
SCIM
A standard REST API for provisioning users and groups.
Deprovisioning
Deactivating or deleting a person's accounts and access.
Access review
A periodic confirmation of who has access, by someone who knows.

Check your understanding

  1. What's the difference between what SAML does and what SCIM does for an app? recall

    Show answer

    SAML (or OIDC) signs people in: the app trusts the IdP to say who's there. SCIM provisions: the IdP creates, updates and deactivates the person's account and group memberships in the app.

  2. An engineer is disabled in the IdP at 17:00. They have an Identity Center PlatformAdmin session from 16:45, with a one-hour duration. When does their AWS access end, and how could you end it sooner? apply

    Show answer

    The role session's credentials work until about 17:45. Sooner: end the user's sessions in Identity Center (so no new credentials are issued), and add a deny statement conditioned on their identitystore:UserId to the permission sets or an SCP, which blocks the credentials already issued. The AWSReservedSSO_ role itself can't be edited.

  3. Why are movers more dangerous than joiners for least privilege? explain

    Show answer

    Joiners complain when access is missing; movers never complain about access they no longer need. Without automatic removal on a move, old access accumulates silently.

  4. Your access review shows 400 items per manager and everyone clicks “approve all”. Redesign it. design

    Show answer

    Remove birthright access from the review (it's governed by rules); show only requested, hand-made and privileged access; add last-used dates; review privileged access monthly and the rest quarterly; remove access that isn't confirmed after a reminder.

  5. Identity Center provisioning stopped a month ago and nobody noticed. What probably happened, and what would have caught it? apply

    Show answer

    The SCIM access token expired after a year. A calendar reminder before expiry, alerts on provisioning errors in the IdP, and a daily comparison of HR leavers with Identity Center's active users.

  6. Compare how a leaver loses access to Azure and to AWS when the company's IdP is Entra ID. compare

    Show answer

    Azure: disabling the Entra ID account stops sign-in to the portal and every subscription at once; refresh tokens can be revoked, and Continuous Access Evaluation cuts many sessions quickly. AWS: Entra ID's SCIM deactivates the user in Identity Center, which stops new sign-ins; existing role sessions run until they expire unless revoked.
