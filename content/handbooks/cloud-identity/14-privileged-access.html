Privileged access and break-glass

The most dangerous permissions in any company are also the ones its best people need, at the worst moments: during an outage, at night, in a hurry. This chapter shows how to give admin and production-change rights only when they're needed, for as long as they're needed, with someone else's say-so; and how to keep an emergency way in that still works when single sign-on itself is broken.

  • Design an elevation process: eligibility, approval, duration, incident rules and review.
  • Implement time-limited elevation on AWS with IAM Identity Center.
  • Design, store, monitor and test break-glass access.

At Larder

On Friday 18 December 2026, at 18:05, in the middle of the Christmas peak, the orders database failed over and checkout was down for 38 minutes. Cho was on call. He assumed prod-operator with an authenticator code from a phone at 4 % battery, and watched the failover complete on its own.

The post-incident review on 21 December found nothing wrong with access that night, and a lot that could have been. Had the database needed a change, only the db-change role could make one (Chapter 10), and both of its owners were unreachable: Ben on a plane, Eli at the office Christmas dinner without a laptop. Had Cho's phone died, nobody else on the rota was awake. And the break-glass-* roles in the guardrail's exception didn't exist yet. Section 14.8 shows what Larder built.

14.1Standing access and just-in-time access

Standing access is privilege you hold all the time, whether you're using it or not. Just-in-time (JIT) access is privilege you're eligible for, but activate only when needed, for a limited time, usually with a reason and an approval. The difference isn't about trusting people less. It's about exposure: how much of the year a powerful credential exists that a phishing page, a stolen laptop or a malicious browser extension could use.

Key idea

Nobody should hold production admin rights while they sleep. Be eligible, not permanently entitled.

14.2What counts as privileged

Privileged access is anything that could cause serious, hard-to-reverse harm, or that could be used to grant more access. In an AWS estate, that's at least:

  • Administrator access in any account, and anything at all in the management account.
  • Changing production: deployments outside the pipeline, database changes, network and firewall rules.
  • IAM and Identity Center: creating roles, editing policies, changing assignments, iam:PassRole on powerful roles (Chapter 8).
  • Reading sensitive data in bulk: customer exports, secrets, backups.
  • Security tooling and logs: anything that could switch off CloudTrail or an alarm.

Everything else, viewing configuration, working in development, reading logs, can usually be standing access through birthright groups (Chapter 13).

14.3Designing elevation

An elevation process answers five questions for each privileged permission set:

Table 14.1 · Larder's elevation rules
Permission setWho may askWho approvesLongest grantIn an incident

PlatformAdmin (dev-staging)any engineertheir tech lead4 hn/a

Principles behind the table:

  • Never approve your own request, except in a declared incident, and then always with review afterwards. An approval nobody can bypass at 3 a.m. becomes a reason to keep standing access.
  • A reason, every time. “INC-412: checkout errors” or a ticket number. It's what the reviewer reads, and what an auditor asks for.
  • Short by default. An hour, renewable. Long grants become standing access with extra steps.
  • Everyone on call can elevate. Larder's near miss came from too few people being able to act, not too many.

On AWS

Identity Center has no built-in “eligible” state, so elevation is built from what it has: account assignments can be created and deleted by API. An elevation tool creates the assignment when a request is approved and deletes it when the time is up, logging both. AWS publishes an open-source solution that does exactly this, TEAM (Temporary Elevated Access Management for IAM Identity Center); identity providers offer the same idea one level up, with time-limited group memberships that SCIM then pushes into Identity Center. Either way:

  • Keep the permission set's session duration no longer than the grant, so a session can't outlive its approval by much.
  • Remember that deleting the assignment stops new sessions; a session already started runs to its end (Chapter 13, 13.4).
  • Run the tool in a locked-down account of its own: whoever controls it can grant anything.

14.4Two people for the worst actions

A few actions are too dangerous even for a well-approved individual session: changing SCPs, editing Identity Center assignments wholesale, deleting a whole account, turning off organisation-wide logging. For these, require two people at the moment of action, not just at approval: changes made through code with a mandatory second reviewer (Chapter 24), and an explicit deny for everyone else (Chapter 10). Deleting backups, at Larder, is denied to everyone.

14.5Break-glass

Break-glass access is a way in that doesn't depend on the things that usually let people in: the identity provider, Identity Center, a phone, the approval tool. It exists for the day those fail, or are misconfigured, or are the very thing under attack. It should be strong, rare, loud and tested.

A design that works for most AWS estates:

  1. Two IAM users, break-glass-1 and break-glass-2, in the management account: console passwords, no access keys, each with two hardware security keys. These are the only IAM users for people in the whole organisation.
  2. A role in every account, break-glass-admin, with administrator permissions, trusting only those two users and only with MFA (Chapter 8). Guardrails list it as an exception (Chapter 10).
  3. Stored apart. Each password is sealed in an envelope in the office safe, and its security keys are in a second place, so using break-glass takes two people.
  4. Loud. Any sign-in by those users, and any assumption of break-glass-admin, pages every tech lead and posts to the incident channel (the lab builds this alarm).
  5. Tested every quarter. Two people follow the runbook, sign in, switch role into production, make a harmless change, check the alarm fired, then change the password and reseal it. An untested break-glass procedure fails on the day you need it.

Watch out: break-glass that depends on what broke

Break-glass accounts federated through the same IdP, protected by MFA on the same phones, or alerting through the same SSO-protected chat tool fail together with the everyday path. Check every dependency, including where the runbook itself is stored.

14.6Watching privileged sessions

  • Every elevation produces a record: who, which permission set, why, approved by whom, from when to when. Review self-approved incident elevations the next working day.
  • CloudTrail ties actions to the session (Chapter 12, 12.7), so “what did Cho do between 18:07 and 19:07?” is one query.
  • Shell access to servers goes through AWS Systems Manager Session Manager rather than SSH keys: sessions are authorised by IAM and can be logged, keystrokes and all, to S3 or CloudWatch Logs.
  • Alerts on the rare things: break-glass use, root sign-in (Chapter 7), changes to Identity Center assignments outside the elevation tool (Chapter 23).

On Azure

Privileged Identity Management is built in: assign people as eligible for a role (on Entra ID or on Azure resources), and they activate it for a set time, with MFA, a justification and optionally an approver. PIM for groups extends the same to group membership. PIM also schedules access reviews of who is eligible.

On Google Cloud

Privileged Access Manager defines entitlements: which roles, on which resource, for whom, for how long, with which approvers. Principals request a grant, it's approved or not, and the binding is added and later removed automatically, with every step in the audit logs.

14.8At Larder: eligible, not entitled

At Larder: solved

With single sign-on in place (Chapter 12), Eli spent February 2027 on the review's three actions. First, no more standing production privilege: ProdOperator, DbChange and production PlatformAdmin stopped being permanently assigned, and became requests through TEAM, deployed in a small tooling account, with the rules in Table 14.1. Everyone on the rota can now get DbChange during an incident with the incident lead's approval, which closes the gap from 18 December. Dee's leftover staging admin became a four-hour request.

Second, break-glass, as in 14.5: two IAM users in larder-management, a break-glass-admin role in all three accounts, envelopes in the office safe and security keys in a locked drawer in Marco's office, and an alarm that pages every tech lead.

The new process had its first real use on 18 February: a payments incident (INC-412) at 18:07, Cho on call again. He self-approved ProdOperator in one click, and Eli approved a DbChange request from his phone three minutes later; the review the next morning took two minutes. Third, practice: the first break-glass test, on 26 February, took 19 minutes and found one problem: the runbook lived in the wiki, which signs in through the IdP. A printed copy now lives in the safe with the envelopes.

14.9Lab: an elevation broker, and a break-glass alarm

Lab 14 · Just-in-time rules in code; an alarm in AWS

You'll run a small elevation broker with Larder's rules on the evening of INC-412, then, optionally, build the alarm that fires when a break-glass user signs in. You need Python 3; for the AWS part, the AWS CLI v2 as an administrator of a sandbox account that has a CloudTrail trail recording management events (Appendix C). Cost: the Python part is free; the alarm costs nothing at this volume beyond the trail's S3 storage, which is pennies. The broker ignores accounts to stay short.

jit.pyPython 3.8+

# jit.py · just-in-time elevation: request, approve, grant for a while, expire, and log it all
from datetime import datetime, timedelta

GROUPS = {"on-call": {"ben", "cho", "eli"}, "db-owners": {"ben", "eli"},
          "platform": {"eli"}, "tech-leads": {"ben", "cho", "dee", "eli"}}

RULES = {   # permission set → who may ask, who may approve, longest grant
    "ProdOperator":  {"eligible": "on-call",    "approvers": "tech-leads", "max_min": 60,  "on_call_self": True},
    "DbChange":      {"eligible": "on-call",    "approvers": "db-owners",  "max_min": 60,  "on_call_self": False},
    "PlatformAdmin": {"eligible": "tech-leads", "approvers": "platform",   "max_min": 120, "on_call_self": False},
}

LOG, GRANTS, PENDING = [], [], {}

def log(at, text):
    LOG.append(f"{at:%H:%M}  {text}")

def request(rid, who, pset, minutes, reason, at, incident=None):
    rule = RULES[pset]
    if who not in GROUPS[rule["eligible"]]:
        return log(at, f"{rid} REFUSED  {who} isn't eligible for {pset}")
    minutes = min(minutes, rule["max_min"])
    if incident and rule["on_call_self"]:
        return grant(rid, who, pset, minutes, at, f"self-approved during {incident}, review tomorrow")
    PENDING[rid] = (who, pset, minutes, reason)
    log(at, f"{rid} PENDING  {who} asks for {pset} {minutes} min: {reason}")

def approve(rid, approver, at):
    who, pset, minutes, _ = PENDING[rid]
    if approver == who:
        return log(at, f"{rid} REFUSED  {approver} can't approve their own request")
    if approver not in GROUPS[RULES[pset]["approvers"]]:
        return log(at, f"{rid} REFUSED  {approver} isn't an approver for {pset}")
    del PENDING[rid]
    grant(rid, who, pset, minutes, at, f"approved by {approver}")

def grant(rid, who, pset, minutes, at, how):
    GRANTS.append((who, pset, at, at + timedelta(minutes=minutes)))
    log(at, f"{rid} GRANTED  {who} → {pset} until {at + timedelta(minutes=minutes):%H:%M} ({how})")

def has(who, pset, at):
    return any(w == who and p == pset and start <= at < end for w, p, start, end in GRANTS)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    t = lambda hhmm: datetime(2027, 2, 18, *map(int, hhmm.split(":")))
    request("R1", "cho", "ProdOperator", 60, "checkout errors", t("18:07"), incident="INC-412")
    request("R2", "cho", "DbChange", 90, "raise max_connections", t("18:12"))
    approve("R2", "cho", t("18:13"))
    approve("R2", "eli", t("18:15"))
    request("R3", "dee", "PlatformAdmin", 240, "set up a sandbox", t("18:20"))
    request("R4", "marco", "DbChange", 30, "curious", t("18:21"))
    print("\n".join(LOG), "\n")
    for when in ("18:30", "19:20"):
        print(f"at {when}: cho ProdOperator {has('cho', 'ProdOperator', t(when))}, "
              f"cho DbChange {has('cho', 'DbChange', t(when))}")

  2. Change the rules. Let the incident lead approve DbChange: add an incident-leads group and accept approvers from either group. Then re-run 18 December 2026: remove Ben and Eli from every group (one on a plane, one at dinner) and see who could still have approved.
  3. Break it on purpose. Set "on_call_self": True for PlatformAdmin and add a request from Cho (a tech lead, so eligible) with incident="INC-999": anyone on call who is also a tech lead can now give themselves administrator rights by naming any incident. That's why self-approval is limited to the narrowest permission set, and reviewed.
  4. Optional: the break-glass alarm. Create an email topic and a rule that matches console sign-ins by IAM users whose names start with break-glass: 
    REGION=us-east-1      # console sign-ins usually arrive here; add the rule to your main region too
TOPIC=$(aws sns create-topic --name break-glass-alerts --region $REGION --query TopicArn --output text)
aws sns subscribe --topic-arn $TOPIC --protocol email --notification-endpoint you@example.com --region $REGION
cat > pattern.json <<'EOF'
{ "detail-type": ["AWS Console Sign In via CloudTrail"],
  "detail": { "userIdentity": { "type": ["IAMUser"], "userName": [{ "prefix": "break-glass" }] } } }
EOF
RULE=$(aws events put-rule --name break-glass-sign-in --event-pattern file://pattern.json \
  --region $REGION --query RuleArn --output text)
cat > topic-policy.json <<EOF
{ "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [{ "Effect": "Allow", "Principal": { "Service": "events.amazonaws.com" },
                  "Action": "sns:Publish", "Resource": "$TOPIC",
                  "Condition": { "ArnEquals": { "aws:SourceArn": "$RULE" } } }] }
EOF
aws sns set-topic-attributes --topic-arn $TOPIC --attribute-name Policy \
  --attribute-value file://topic-policy.json --region $REGION
aws events put-targets --rule break-glass-sign-in --targets "Id=email,Arn=$TOPIC" --region $REGION
    Confirm the subscription email. Then create an IAM user break-glass-lab with a console password, sign in as it in a private browser window, and wait a few minutes for the email. If nothing arrives, find the sign-in in CloudTrail and check which region recorded it.
  5. Clean up: delete the IAM user, aws events remove-targets --rule break-glass-sign-in --ids email, aws events delete-rule --name break-glass-sign-in, and aws sns delete-topic --topic-arn $TOPIC (all with --region $REGION).

✓Chapter summary

  • Standing privilege is exposed all the time; just-in-time privilege only while it's used.
  • Privileged means admin, production change, IAM, bulk sensitive data and security tooling, and anything in the management account.
  • Elevation rules say who may ask, who approves (never yourself, except in an incident, reviewed), for how long, and why.
  • On AWS, an elevation tool creates and deletes Identity Center assignments; keep session durations short.
  • The worst actions need two people at the moment of action, through code review and explicit denies.
  • Break-glass: separate IAM users with hardware keys, a role in every account, stored apart, alarmed, and tested quarterly.

Key terms

Privileged access
Permissions that can cause serious harm or grant more access.
Standing access
Privilege held permanently, whether used or not.
Just-in-time access
Privilege activated for a limited time when needed.
Eligibility
Being allowed to request a privilege, without holding it.
Elevation
Activating eligible privilege, with justification and often approval.
Break-glass access
Emergency access that works when normal sign-in doesn't.
Two-person rule
Requiring two people for an action, not just for its approval.

Check your understanding

  1. An administrator says, “I'm trustworthy, so I don't need just-in-time access.” What's the answer? explain

    Show answer

    It isn't about their trustworthiness: it's about how long a powerful credential exists that an attacker could steal or misuse through their browser, laptop or session. Just-in-time access shrinks that exposure from all the time to the few hours it's used.

  2. Why should a permission set used for elevation have a session duration no longer than the grant? apply

    Show answer

    Removing the assignment stops new sessions but not ones already started. A twelve-hour session started near the end of a one-hour grant would keep the privilege for eleven more hours.

  3. Larder's first break-glass test found the runbook was in the wiki. Why is that a problem, and what else would you check? apply

    Show answer

    The wiki signs in through the IdP, which might be the thing that's down. Also check: alarm delivery doesn't depend on SSO-protected tools, the security keys aren't registered only on IdP-managed devices, passwords haven't expired, and the people who know the safe's combination aren't all the same two people.

  4. Design elevation for a team of four engineers where everyone is on call and the CTO is usually unreachable. design

    Show answer

    All four eligible for an operator set, self-approved during incidents with next-day review; database and admin sets approved by any other engineer, never yourself; one-hour grants and sessions; break-glass with two people for when nobody else is reachable; the CTO reviews the weekly elevation log rather than approving each one.

  5. What makes an action deserve a two-person rule rather than just an approval? explain

    Show answer

    Approval happens before; the approved person then acts alone and could do more than approved. For actions that are catastrophic or could disable oversight (SCPs, logging, account deletion), the second person must see and accept the exact change, as in a mandatory code review, with direct changes denied to everyone.

  6. Compare just-in-time elevation on Azure and on AWS. compare

    Show answer

    Azure has it built in: Privileged Identity Management makes people eligible for roles, which they activate with MFA, justification and optional approval for a limited time. AWS Identity Center has no eligible state, so a tool (such as TEAM) or time-limited IdP groups create and remove account assignments.
