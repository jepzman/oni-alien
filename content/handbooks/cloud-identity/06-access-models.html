Access models: roles, attributes and relationships

Once you know who's asking, something has to decide what they may do. There are only a few ways to write that down: lists on each resource, roles, rules about attributes, and relationships between things. Each one fits some problems well and others badly. This chapter compares them on one company's real questions, and sets out the principles that keep any of them safe.

By the end you can

  • Describe access as a matrix, and explain ACLs and capabilities as its columns and rows.
  • Design roles, and recognise role explosion and privilege creep.
  • Write attribute-based rules, and say what they cost in auditability.
  • Model ownership and sharing as relationships.
  • Apply least privilege, separation of duties and object-level checks to any model.

At Larder

In December 2026, Joost's operations team starts planning the Utrecht store, and Eli is asked to set up its staff in the stock tool. The tool has 19 roles: a picker, stock clerk, store manager and operations role for each of the three stores, plus seven others that grew over two years. Roles are handed out from a spreadsheet. Adding Utrecht means four more, and the six-city plan means dozens.

While copying the spreadsheet, Eli notices that Tomás, who manages Noord, is also a store manager at Zuid. He covered there for two weeks in July while Zuid's manager was on holiday. Nobody removed the role, so for five months he could adjust any stock count at a store he didn't work in. Nothing bad happened. That's not the same as nothing being wrong. Section 6.9 shows what Larder changed.

6.1The access matrix

Every access model is a way of writing down one big table: principals down the side, resources across the top, and in each cell the actions that principal may perform on that resource. This is the access control matrix. Nobody stores it whole (it would be enormous and mostly empty), but every model is a compression of it.

6.2Access control lists

An access control list (ACL) stores the matrix by column: each resource carries a list of who may do what to it. File permissions are ACLs; so are sharing settings on a document (“Anna can edit, the team can comment”). ACLs are easy to understand for one resource, and answer “who can reach this file?” instantly.

They're hard to manage at scale. “What can Aylin reach?” means reading every ACL in the system, and when Aylin changes jobs, someone must find and edit each one. They suit cases where the owner of each resource genuinely decides who shares it, as in document sharing, and little else.

S3 ACLsS3 still supports per-object ACLs from its early days, but new buckets have them switched off (Object Ownership: “bucket owner enforced”). AWS's advice, and this book's, is to keep them off and use policies (Chapter 9).

6.3Roles

Role-based access control (RBAC) adds a layer: permissions are granted to roles named after jobs (“store manager”, “picker”), and people are assigned roles. When someone changes job, you change their roles, and every permission follows. RBAC is the most widely used model, and for good reason: roles match how organisations already think, and “who has the store manager role?” is an easy question to answer and review.

RBAC goes wrong in two familiar ways:

  • Role explosion. Roles start as jobs, then pick up a second dimension: a job at a store, in a team, for a customer. Every new store multiplies the roles. Larder's four jobs times three stores is 12 roles; times twelve stores it's 48, before any exceptions. Soon nobody knows what noord-lead-2 was for.
  • Privilege creep. People collect roles as they cover, move and help out, and roles are rarely taken away, because nobody notices extra access until it's misused. Tomás's Zuid role is a textbook case. Regular access reviews (Chapter 13) and assignments that expire are the cures.

Good role design keeps roles about what a job does, and moves where or for whom into something else: attributes or relationships.

6.4Attributes

Attribute-based access control (ABAC) writes rules over attributes of the principal, the resource, the action and the context:

allow if  action in permissions_of(principal.job)
     and  resource.store in principal.stores
     and  context.time is within the principal's shift

One rule replaces every “job at store” role. Add Utrecht, and nothing in the rules changes: Utrecht's staff get stores = {utrecht} from the HR system, and Utrecht's stock items carry store = utrecht. You've already seen ABAC in AWS: comparing a principal tag with a resource tag (Chapter 9, 9.6) is exactly this.

ABAC has its own costs:

  • Auditing gets harder. “Who can adjust Zuid's stock?” is no longer a list you can read; you have to evaluate the rule against every person's attributes.
  • Attributes become security data. Whoever can change someone's stores attribute, or a resource's tag, can change access. Attributes must come from a trustworthy source (the HR system, not a free-text field users can edit) and be protected like permissions.
  • Missing attributes need a safe default. A resource without a store attribute should be denied, not open to everyone.

6.5Relationships

Some access follows from how things relate. A Korenwolf office manager may see their office's orders; Korenwolf's Head of Workplace may see the orders of every office in her organisation; a document is visible to members of the folder it's in. Neither roles nor attributes say this naturally. Relationship-based access control (ReBAC) stores facts as relationships (“Rotterdam office has manager J. de Vries”, “order B-1042 belongs to the Rotterdam office”) and answers questions by following them.

ReBAC is how large sharing systems work: Google described its global authorisation system, Zanzibar, which stores trillions of such relationships for Drive, YouTube and Cloud; open-source systems such as OpenFGA and SpiceDB follow the same design. It suits multi-tenant products with organisations, teams, folders and sharing. It needs a place to store the relationships, kept in step with your application's data.

6.6Principles that apply to every model

  • Least privilege: each principal gets the access its job needs, and no more, for no longer than needed. Every model can express it; only discipline achieves it.
  • Default deny: no rule, no access (Chapter 1). Missing attributes and broken relationships deny.
  • Separation of duties: split powerful tasks so one person can't complete them alone. The person who adds a supplier shouldn't approve its payments; the engineer who writes a production change shouldn't be the only reviewer.
  • Deny overrides: a small number of explicit “never” rules (no one deletes backups; nothing leaves the EU) that win over any allow. AWS builds this into policy evaluation (Chapter 10).
  • Time limits: temporary needs get access that expires by itself. Tomás's July cover should have ended in July without anyone remembering.

Watch out: checking the action but not the object

The most common authorisation bug in web APIs isn't a wrong role; it's checking that a user may “view orders” and then returning whichever order ID they ask for. Change /orders/1042 to /orders/1043 and you see someone else's. OWASP ranks this, broken object-level authorisation, as the number one API security risk. Every request for a specific object must check that this principal may access this object, which is exactly the question ABAC and ReBAC ask.

Key idea

Roles for what a job does; attributes for where and for whom; relationships for who owns what. Keep each one small.

Wherever the decision is made, make it in one place. Authorisation rules scattered through application code (if user.role == "admin" or user.id == order.owner_id …) can't be reviewed, tested or changed safely. Put them in a policy layer: a module, a library, or a policy engine such as Open Policy Agent (Rego), Cedar or OpenFGA, which takes a request and returns allow or deny (the policy decision point of Chapter 1).

On Azure

Azure RBAC is role-based with a strong relationship flavour: a role assignment at a management group is inherited by every subscription, resource group and resource below it. Role assignment conditions add ABAC, for example letting a role read only blobs whose index tag project matches the principal's attribute.

On Google Cloud

Google Cloud's resource hierarchy makes inheritance central: a role granted on a folder applies to every project and resource in it. IAM Conditions narrow a binding by resource name, type, tags or time, which is how you grant temporary access that expires by itself.

6.9At Larder: four jobs, one rule

At Larder: solved

Eli and Joost rewrote the stock tool's access before Utrecht's staff were added. The 12 store roles became four jobs, and a person's job and home store now come from the HR system, which Joost's team already keeps up to date. One rule decides: the job's permissions, at the person's stores. Covering another store is a temporary store assignment with an end date, requested in the tool and approved by the store's manager; it disappears on the end date. The seven other roles shrank to three after nobody could explain the rest. Tomás's Zuid access went with the spreadsheet.

For Larder for Business, the portal that launches in spring 2027 checks orders by relationship from the start: an office manager sees their office's orders, and a customer's administrator, such as Korenwolf's Hanna, sees every office in their organisation. Every order request checks the order's office against the person, not just their role.

6.10Lab: one question, three models

Lab 6 · RBAC, ABAC and ReBAC side by side

You'll answer Larder's access questions with roles, then with an attribute rule, then answer the business-order question with relationships, count roles as stores grow, and see privilege creep in action. You need Python 3. Cost: nothing.

models.pyPython 3.8+

# models.py · the same access questions answered three ways: RBAC, ABAC, ReBAC
JOBS = {                                   # what each job may do, anywhere it works
    "picker":        {"stock:view"},
    "stock-clerk":   {"stock:view", "stock:count"},
    "store-manager": {"stock:view", "stock:count", "stock:adjust", "rota:edit"},
    "operations":    {"stock:view", "rota:edit"},
}

# ---------- RBAC: a role per job per store, assigned by hand
def rbac_roles(stores):
    return {f"{job}@{store}": perms for job, perms in JOBS.items() for store in stores}

ROLES = rbac_roles(["noord", "zuid", "rotterdam"])
ASSIGNED = {"tomas": {"store-manager@noord", "store-manager@zuid"},   # covered Zuid in July...
            "aylin": {"picker@zuid"},
            "joost": {f"operations@{s}" for s in ["noord", "zuid", "rotterdam"]}}

def rbac(user, action, store):
    return any(action in ROLES[r] and r.endswith("@" + store) for r in ASSIGNED.get(user, ()))

# ---------- ABAC: one rule over attributes of the person and the resource
PEOPLE = {"tomas": {"job": "store-manager", "stores": {"noord"}},   # from the HR system
          "aylin": {"job": "picker", "stores": {"zuid"}},
          "joost": {"job": "operations", "stores": {"*"}}}

def abac(user, action, store):
    p = PEOPLE.get(user)
    return bool(p) and action in JOBS[p["job"]] and (store in p["stores"] or "*" in p["stores"])

# ---------- ReBAC: who may see a business order follows relationships
TUPLES = {("office:kw-rotterdam", "manager", "j.devries"),
          ("office:kw-amsterdam", "manager", "m.bakker"),
          ("org:korenwolf", "admin", "hanna"),
          ("office:kw-rotterdam", "parent", "org:korenwolf"),
          ("office:kw-amsterdam", "parent", "org:korenwolf"),
          ("order:B-1042", "office", "office:kw-rotterdam")}

def related(obj, rel):
    return {s for (o, r, s) in TUPLES if o == obj and r == rel}

def can_view_order(user, order):
    for office in related(order, "office"):
        if user in related(office, "manager"):
            return True
        if any(user in related(org, "admin") for org in related(office, "parent")):
            return True
    return False

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"RBAC roles for 3 stores: {len(ROLES)}; for 12 stores: {len(rbac_roles(range(12)))}\n")
    questions = [("tomas", "stock:adjust", "noord"), ("tomas", "stock:adjust", "zuid"),
                 ("aylin", "stock:adjust", "zuid"), ("aylin", "stock:view", "zuid"),
                 ("joost", "rota:edit", "rotterdam"), ("joost", "stock:adjust", "rotterdam")]
    print(f"{'question':<34} RBAC   ABAC")
    for q in questions:
        print(f"{' '.join(q):<34} {str(rbac(*q)):<6} {abac(*q)}")
    print()
    for user in ["j.devries", "m.bakker", "hanna"]:
        print(f"{user:<10} may view order B-1042: {can_view_order(user, 'order:B-1042')}")

  3. Break it on purpose. In abac, change the store check to store in p.get("stores", {store}) and remove Aylin's "stores" key: a person with a missing attribute now gets access everywhere. Stretch goals. (1) Give ABAC a temporary cover: "cover": {"store": "zuid", "until": "2026-07-19"}, and a today argument, so Tomás's July access ends by itself. (2) Add a relationship type viewer on an order, so an office manager can share one order with a colleague, and extend can_view_order.

✓Chapter summary

  • Every model compresses the access matrix: ACLs store it by resource, capabilities by principal, roles and rules by pattern.
  • RBAC matches jobs and is easy to review, but suffers role explosion and privilege creep.
  • ABAC scales across stores, teams and tags with one rule, but makes “who can reach this?” harder and turns attributes into security data.
  • ReBAC follows relationships: ownership, hierarchies, sharing.
  • Combine them: roles for what, attributes for where, relationships for whose; decide in one policy layer.
  • Least privilege, default deny, separation of duties, deny overrides and time limits apply to every model. Always check the object, not just the action.

Key terms

Access control matrix
Principals × resources, with permitted actions in each cell.
ACL
Access control list: the permissions stored on one resource.
RBAC
Role-based access control: permissions granted to roles, roles assigned to principals.
ABAC
Attribute-based access control: rules over attributes of principal, resource, action and context.
ReBAC
Relationship-based access control: decisions follow relationships between objects.
Role explosion
Roles multiplying because they encode extra dimensions such as location.
Privilege creep
Access accumulating over time as people move and cover.
Least privilege
Only the access needed, for only as long as needed.
Separation of duties
Splitting sensitive tasks so no one person can complete them alone.
Policy engine
A component that evaluates authorisation rules and returns allow or deny.

Check your understanding

  1. Which model fits each: a shared slide deck; “store managers can adjust stock at their store”; “Korenwolf's admin sees all its offices' orders”? recall

    Show answer

    An ACL (owner-controlled sharing); ABAC (job plus a store attribute), or RBAC with attributes; ReBAC (organisation → office → order).

  2. Why did ABAC fix Tomás's leftover access, when RBAC didn't? explain

    Show answer

    ABAC reads his store from the HR system, which was updated when the cover ended; RBAC depended on someone remembering to remove a role assignment, which nobody did.

  3. An API checks that the caller has the orders:read scope, then returns /orders/{id} for any ID. What's wrong, and what's the fix? apply

    Show answer

    Broken object-level authorisation: any caller with the scope can read any order. Check that the caller is related to that order (owner, or manager of its office) before returning it.

  4. Give one example of separation of duties at Larder, and how you'd enforce it. design

    Show answer

    Refunds over €100: support staff can propose, a team lead must approve, and nobody can approve their own. Enforce it in the refund tool's policy: the approver must hold the lead role and differ from the requester.

  5. What new risks does ABAC introduce, and how do you control them? explain

    Show answer

    Whoever can change attributes or tags can change access, missing attributes can be mistaken for “match all”, and it's harder to list who can reach a resource. Source attributes from a trusted system, restrict who can change them, deny when they're missing, and evaluate rules regularly to produce access reports.

  6. Compare how inheritance works in Azure RBAC and Google Cloud IAM. compare

    Show answer

    Both inherit downwards: Azure from management groups to subscriptions, resource groups and resources; Google Cloud from organisation to folders, projects and resources. In both, a grant high up applies to everything below it, so grant at the lowest scope that works.
