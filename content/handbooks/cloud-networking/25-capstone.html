At Larder

In January 2028 Larder decides, in ADR 0026, to stay in one region but keep a pilot light in Paris: a minimal standby that can take over within four hours, for about €1,200 a month, instead of a full second region at around €300,000 a year. The ADR names four triggers for going further: 60,000 orders a day, a regional outage longer than an hour, a contract that requires it, or a city outside the EU. Ana asks Eli for two things: the network design for the pilot light, and one page that shows the whole network, for the review board and for whoever joins the team next.

25.1How to use this chapter

Each section starts with a “your turn” task. Do it on paper first, for Larder or for a system you know, then read Larder's answer and compare. Disagreeing with Larder's choice is fine, as long as you can say why: every decision in a network trades one thing for another, and the reasoning is the skill.

25.4The pilot light in Paris

Your turn

Design the network for a standby region that must be able to take over within four hours, while costing very little when idle. Decide what's built in advance, what's built during a failover, and how customers, stores and partners get moved.

A pilot light keeps the parts that are slow to create running, and builds the parts that are quick to create only when needed. For networks, that sorts nicely:

Table 25.3 · What exists in Paris, and when
ComponentAlways onBuilt during failoverWhy
Address plan, VPCs, subnets, route tables, security groups✓Free while idle; built from the same module as Frankfurt, so it can't drift
Transit gateway and peering with Frankfurt✓Carries database replication; small monthly cost
Database replica✓Data is the slowest thing to move; replication within the EU
Gateway endpoints, private zone associations✓Free
NAT gateways, egress firewall, interface endpoints, Resolver endpoints✓Charged by the hour; minutes to create from code
EKS nodes, load balancers✓Charged by the hour; scaled up from code and images
Store VPNs to Paris✓About $36 a month per connection would exceed the budget; configs pre-generated, created by runbook
CloudFront origin and DNS✓ (records ready)switchOrigin switched once Paris is serving; TTLs already short

The idle network costs well under €200 a month: the transit gateway and its attachments, the peering, and replication traffic between regions at about two cents per gigabyte (Chapter 23). The rest of the €1,200 goes to the database replica and stored images.

The failover, in network terms

  1. Decide. The ADR's trigger: Frankfurt down for more than an hour. Failover is a deliberate decision, not automatic; a pilot light can't take traffic until it's scaled up, and a false failover is expensive.
  2. Build the hourly parts from code: NAT gateways and egress firewall, interface and Resolver endpoints, load balancers, nodes. About 20–40 minutes.
  3. Promote the database replica in Paris.
  4. Reconnect stores. The runbook creates each store's Paris VPN connection from the pre-generated configs; store routers already hold the Paris tunnel settings, disabled, and are switched by the same management template as the MSS clamp. BGP does the rest (Chapter 13).
  5. Move customers and partners. Point CloudFront's origin at the Paris ALB; switch DNS for api.larder.example (TTL 60, Chapter 5); publish the Paris endpoint service to Korenwolf, who creates a new endpoint (agreed in advance, in their runbook too).
  6. Check from outside: synthetic checks for checkout, stores, partners; flow logs and load balancer metrics in Paris.

Watch out: a standby you never test doesn't work

Every untested step above hides a surprise: a quota that's lower in Paris, an endpoint service not yet allowed for a partner's account, a store router template with a typo. Rehearse the failover at least twice a year, end to end, during a quiet morning.

25.5A review checklist for any network

Every chapter's lessons, compressed into questions you can ask of any design, including your own:

Table 25.4 · Network design review
AreaQuestions
AddressesDo ranges come from a plan or IPAM? Do they overlap anything that might connect? Is there room for 2× growth and for pods?
ResilienceDoes losing any one zone leave a working system? Are NAT gateways, endpoints and subnets per zone? Are there two tunnels and two links where it matters?
RoutingAre subnets associated explicitly? Does every path have a route in both directions? Is the main table local-only? Is segmentation written as a from → to matrix?
FilteringAre rules written between roles? Do stateless rules allow return traffic? Is anything reachable from the internet that shouldn't be?
NamesDo private zones reach every VPC and site that needs them? Any split-horizon zones hiding public records? Short TTLs where changes are planned?
EdgeDoes every public request pass the edge? Is the origin locked? Is anything personal cacheable? Are WAF rules in count mode first?
EgressIs there an inventory of destinations? Can workloads bypass the checkpoint? Do AWS services go through endpoints?
TimeoutsAre app keep-alives longer than load balancer idle timeouts? Do pools close idle connections before NAT does? Are MSS values clamped on tunnels?
IdentityDo internal tools trust network location? Is access tied to HR through SSO? Are workload identities short-lived?
OperationsAre flow and query logs on? Are network metrics alarmed? Are critical paths checked after every change?
CostWhich paths charge per GB, and how much traffic takes them? Any public IPv4 addresses without a reason?
CodeIs everything in code, with plans reviewed? Are lessons in the module? Are absolute rules enforced as guardrails?

25.7Your capstone

Capstone · Design a network for a new company

The company: a chain of 25 physiotherapy clinics across Belgium and the Netherlands. Each clinic has a small office network, appointment tablets and a treatment-notes system. Patients book online and through an app. Health data must stay in the EU, and a partner insurer wants to submit claims through a private API. The team is six engineers. Cost: paper only; building parts of it costs what the matching labs cost.

Produce, in a few pages:

  1. Requirements and the network decisions each drives (Table 25.1's format).
  2. An address plan for clinics, the cloud, a possible second region and pods, tested with Lab 10's script.
  3. A one-page diagram like Figure 25.1.
  4. A layer-by-layer table like Table 25.2, with a chapter reference for each choice.
  5. A cost estimate for the network, using Lab 23's model.
  6. A review of your own design against Table 25.4, with at least three things you'd change after reviewing it.
  7. Optional build: combine Labs 8, 11, 12, 15 and 16 into a small version of your design in a sandbox account, check its critical paths with Reachability Analyzer, and destroy it.

If you can do this, and explain every choice to someone who disagrees, you're competent in cloud networking, which is what this book set out to make you.

✓Chapter summary

  • Start from requirements, and trace each to the network decisions it drives.
  • A whole network fits on one page, with a one-line decision per layer behind it.
  • A pilot light keeps slow-to-create parts running (network layout, peering, data) and builds hourly-charged parts on failover.
  • Failover is a rehearsed runbook: build, promote, reconnect sites, move customers and partners, check from outside.
  • Review every design against the checklist, and test it with game days.

Key terms

Pilot light
A minimal standby environment that can be scaled up to take over.
RTO
Recovery time objective: how quickly service must be restored.
Runbook
Written, tested steps for a specific operation, such as a failover.
Game day
A planned exercise that causes failures to test systems and people.
Design review
A structured check of a design against requirements and known pitfalls.

Check your understanding

  1. Why are store VPN connections to Paris created during failover rather than kept running? explain

    Show answer

    Each VPN connection is charged by the hour; one per store would exceed the pilot light's budget. With pre-generated configurations and router settings in place, creating them takes minutes, well within the four-hour RTO.

  2. Why isn't the failover to Paris automatic, with health-checked DNS? explain

    Show answer

    A pilot light can't serve traffic until it's scaled up and the database is promoted, and an unnecessary failover is costly and risky. The ADR sets a trigger (an outage longer than an hour), and people decide; automatic DNS failover suits warm standbys that are already serving.

  3. Pick three items from the review checklist that would have prevented incidents told in this book, and name the incidents. apply

    Show answer

    For example: explicit subnet associations (the reporting subnet on the main table, Ch. 8); routes in both directions (Rotterdam's missing return route, Ch. 4); rules between roles (the Dispatch outage, Ch. 9); keep-alives longer than idle timeouts (the 502s, Ch. 16); an inventory of egress destinations (the poisoned package, Ch. 18).

  4. In the game day where one zone is blocked, what would a failure tell you? apply

    Show answer

    That something is still single-zone: a NAT gateway or endpoint used by all zones, a subnet group or function config with one zone, uneven targets without cross-zone balancing, or a client pinned to one replica. Fix the design, then repeat the drill.

  5. Which of Larder's decisions would you revisit first if it hit the ADR's “60,000 orders a day” trigger, and why? design

    Show answer

    The pilot light itself: at that scale an outage's cost grows, so a warm standby or active-active second region may now be cheaper than the expected loss. Close behind: Direct Connect thresholds if a central warehouse appears, and service-to-service identity if service count grows past the mesh threshold.
