The cost of moving bytes

Compute and storage costs are easy to see: you choose an instance size or a disk. Network costs hide in the design: which zone talks to which, which path data takes out of the cloud, which gateway it passes through on the way. This chapter is a map of what moving data costs, where the surprises come from, and how to design networks that stay cheap as traffic grows.

By the end you can

  • Say which network paths are free and which are charged, and roughly how much.
  • Find the biggest network costs in a bill and trace them to traffic.
  • Apply the common fixes: endpoints, zone-local traffic, caching, peering.
  • Estimate a design's network cost before building it.

At Larder

Marco, the CFO, does his quarterly review of the cloud bill with Eli. Compute and databases have grown roughly in line with orders. The network lines have grown three times as fast and now come to about $9,000 a month across five usage types with names like NatGateway-Bytes and DataTransfer-Regional-Bytes. “I don't need to understand every line,” Marco says. “I need to know which ones we chose, and which ones just happened to us.”

Section 23.4 goes through them one by one.

23.1The big picture

Cloud providers charge for data by path. Some paths are free, some cost a cent or two per gigabyte, and a few cost much more. Data coming in from the internet is almost always free; data going out to the internet is the most expensive. In between, you pay for crossing zones, crossing regions, and for passing through certain managed gateways. Your design decides which paths your traffic takes.

Notice that some charges stack. A gigabyte from a server in zone b, through a NAT gateway in zone a, out to the internet pays three times: cross-zone, NAT processing, and internet egress. A gigabyte from a spoke VPC through a transit gateway to a firewall and NAT pays TGW processing, firewall processing, NAT processing and egress.

Key idea

Every hop that processes data charges for it. The cheapest byte is one that stays in its zone, or never travels at all.

23.3Finding where the money goes

A bill tells you which charge, not which traffic. To connect the two:

  1. Group the bill by usage type. In Cost Explorer (or the Cost and Usage Report), group by usage type and look for names containing DataTransfer, NatGateway-Bytes, TransitGateway-Bytes, VpcEndpoint-Bytes and PublicIPv4.
  2. Find the talkers. Query flow logs (Chapter 22) for the biggest byte counts by source and destination, especially through NAT gateways (the traffic-path field helps) and across zones.
  3. Name each flow. “Backups from the data subnets to S3 through NAT, 14 TB a month.” A named flow can be fixed; a usage type can't.
  4. Tag and allocate. Tag resources by team so each team sees its own share; costs that belong to nobody grow fastest.

At Larder: solved

Marco's question had a clear answer. Internet egress, the biggest line, was “chosen”: it's the cost of serving customers, and the CDN trimmed it and took load off the servers. Nearly everything else “just happened”: traffic that took an expensive path because nobody had decided otherwise. Eli's changes took four weeks and cut network costs by more than 40%. Two of them, endpoints and per-zone NAT, now go into every new VPC by default through the network module (Chapter 24), so they can't “just happen” again.

23.5Design rules for cheap networks

  • Gateway endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB in every VPC. Free, and they remove a big NAT line.
  • Keep chatty traffic in its zone. Zone-aware routing, same-zone replicas, one NAT gateway per zone.
  • Interface endpoints where the volume justifies them, and centralise them when you have many VPCs (Chapter 12).
  • Serve the public through a CDN. Lower origin load, fewer servers, and usually a slightly lower price per gigabyte.
  • Peer high-volume pairs alongside the transit gateway.
  • Don't send what you don't need to: compress responses, avoid chatty polling, ship logs in batches.
  • Release public IPv4 addresses you don't use; prefer private subnets and load balancers.
  • Estimate before you build. Traffic × path price is simple arithmetic; do it during design reviews.

Watch out: don't trade resilience for pennies

Putting everything in one zone makes cross-zone charges disappear, and makes a zone failure an outage (Chapter 7). Replication between zones is the point of having zones; pay for it. Cut the cross-zone traffic that has no resilience value, like a consumer reading from a far replica.

23.7Lab: price a design, then read your bill

Lab 23 · A network cost model in twenty lines

You'll model a design's monthly network cost in Python, test two changes, and pull your own account's network costs by usage type. You need Python 3 and, for step 4, the AWS CLI. Cost: the Cost Explorer API charges $0.01 per request; this lab makes one or two.

netcost.pyPython 3

PRICE = {                     # $ per GB, approximate; check your region's prices
    "cross_zone": 0.02,       # 0.01 out + 0.01 in
    "nat": 0.048, "internet": 0.09, "tgw": 0.02, "endpoint": 0.01,
}
HOURLY = {"nat": 0.052, "endpoint_az": 0.011}   # $ per hour
HOURS = 730

def monthly(flows, nat_gateways, endpoint_azs=0):
    data = sum(gb * sum(PRICE[p] for p in path) for gb, path in flows)
    fixed = (nat_gateways * HOURLY["nat"] + endpoint_azs * HOURLY["endpoint_az"]) * HOURS
    return round(data + fixed)

before = [(14_000, ["nat"]),                       # backups to S3 through NAT
          (3_000, ["cross_zone", "nat", "internet"]), # zone b and c out through zone a's NAT
          (40_000, ["tgw"])]                       # prod → analytics
after  = [(14_000, []),                             # S3 gateway endpoint: free
          (3_000, ["nat", "internet"]),             # NAT in each zone
          (40_000, [])]                             # peering, same zone pairs: free

print("before:", monthly(before, nat_gateways=1))
print("after: ", monthly(after, nat_gateways=3))
  1. Run it. python3 netcost.py. How much do the two changes save? Which single flow mattered most?
  2. Find the break-even. Adding two NAT gateways costs about $76 a month. Below how many GB of cross-zone NAT traffic is one NAT gateway cheaper, ignoring resilience? (Then remember why you'd still pay for three.)
  3. Model your own system. Replace the flows with three of yours, guessed from what you know. Rough numbers are fine; this is a design tool.
  4. Read your bill (if you have an account with some history): 
    aws ce get-cost-and-usage --time-period Start=2026-10-01,End=2026-11-01 \
  --granularity MONTHLY --metrics UnblendedCost --group-by Type=DIMENSION,Key=USAGE_TYPE \
  --query 'ResultsByTime[0].Groups[?contains(Keys[0], `Bytes`) || contains(Keys[0], `PublicIPv4`)].[Keys[0], Metrics.UnblendedCost.Amount]' \
  --output table
    Change the dates to last month. Which of your lines are “chosen” and which “just happened”?

✓Chapter summary

  • Data is charged by path: free within a zone, cents per GB between zones and regions, most to the internet; incoming is free.
  • Gateways that process data (NAT, transit gateway, interface endpoints, firewalls) charge per GB, and charges stack along a path.
  • Trace bill lines to named flows with usage types and flow logs.
  • Common fixes: gateway endpoints, zone-local traffic, per-zone NAT, CDNs, direct peering for heavy pairs, fewer public IPv4 addresses.
  • Estimate traffic × path price during design; don't cut resilience to save pennies.

Key terms

Data transfer out
Data leaving the provider to the internet; the most expensive path.
Regional data transfer
Data between zones in the same region.
Processing charge
A per-GB fee for data passing through a managed gateway.
Usage type
The bill's label for a kind of charge, such as NatGateway-Bytes.
Network tier
Google Cloud's choice between Premium and Standard internet routing.

Check your understanding

  1. Which of these are free on AWS: data in from the internet, S3 via a gateway endpoint, traffic between two zones, traffic inside one zone on private addresses? recall

    Show answer

    Free: data in from the internet, S3 via a gateway endpoint, and traffic within a zone on private addresses. Charged: traffic between zones, in each direction.

  2. A server in zone b sends 1 TB a month to the internet through a NAT gateway in zone a. List the charges. apply

    Show answer

    Cross-zone transfer (both directions, roughly $20 for 1 TB), NAT processing (about $45–50), and internet egress (about $90), plus the NAT gateway's hourly charge. A NAT gateway in zone b would remove the cross-zone part.

  3. Why might peering two VPCs that already share a transit gateway save money, and why does routing still work? explain

    Show answer

    Peering has no per-GB processing charge, unlike the TGW. With a summary route to the TGW and the peer's exact range to the peering, longest prefix match sends their traffic over the peering (Chapter 11).

  4. Why is putting everything in one zone a bad way to cut cross-zone costs? explain

    Show answer

    A zone failure would then take the whole service down. Replication and load balancing across zones are the reason zones exist; cut cross-zone traffic that adds no resilience instead.

  5. Your bill shows NatGateway-Bytes growing fast. How do you find out what's behind it? apply

    Show answer

    Query flow logs on the NAT gateways' interfaces (or with traffic-path) for the largest byte counts by source and destination, name the flows, and check whether they go to AWS services that endpoints could serve, or to destinations that shouldn't be contacted at all.
