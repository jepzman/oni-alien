What If the Signature Stalls?

TL;DR

When a reorganisation freezes your contract, don't wait and don't lobby around it. Find the new decision path (who decides now, when, on what papers), speak its language (a reorg about cost wants a cost case), keep your team together with a written interim arrangement, and answer every question fully, even from a rival advising the client. The people deciding are new and nervous; make yes easy.

  • Family: the paper
  • Lives in: contract/lodestar/horizon-case.pdf
  • Pairs with: working at risk, mapping the client

1The situation Wrenfield and MetroVolt, 18 May 2027

Twelve days after Wrenfield is named preferred bidder, Desmond calls: Project Horizon now requires every contract over £1m to be re-approved by a new Horizon Investment Board, chaired by Rupert Thorne. Vexmoor Consulting, Rupert's advisers, will review each business case first.

  • Vexmoor lost Lodestar three weeks ago. Now it reviews the winner's business case.
  • The board meets on 22 June. Papers are due on 11 June. Lodestar can't sign before then.
  • Wrenfield has six people waiting to start on 1 June. Graham asks whether to release them to other work.
  • Vivian wants to call Priya and “get this sorted”.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Priya and Elaine

Priya still wants Lodestar, but she's now defending her own department against a 15 % cut. A new board and new advisers are questioning every programme. She needs arguments Horizon will accept, and no supplier making her look like she can't control her own procurement.

“Help me make the case, and please don't go around me.”

Wrenfield Ines and Graham

Wrenfield needs to know who decides now and what they need, and it needs to keep its team from being reassigned. It must not look desperate, and it must not complain about Vexmoor, whatever it thinks.

“The deal isn't dead. It's in a different room.”

A reorg doesn't change whether the project is a good idea. It changes who has to be convinced, and in what words.

3Real-life analogy the house sale when the buyer's bank merges

You're selling a house, and the buyer's bank merges with another. The new bank wants its own valuation, by its own surveyor. You don't phone the bank's chairman. You find out what the surveyor needs, have the papers ready, keep your removal van booked on a flexible date, and answer every question politely, even if the surveyor once tried to buy the house himself.

4How it works the new path, its language, the team, and no shortcuts

Find the new path. Ines asks Desmond three questions in writing: who decides, when, and what the paper must contain. Answer: the Horizon Investment Board, 22 June, a four-page template with a section titled “cost reduction”.

Speak its language. Horizon is about cost. Lodestar's original case was about customers. Ines and Nkem rewrite it around one number: MAVIS's mainframe and support cost about £1.9m a year, and each wave of Lodestar shrinks it. Priya submits it as her paper, not Wrenfield's.

Keep the team. Graham asks MetroVolt for a letter of intent (next note) so the six can start preparation at limited risk instead of scattering to other work.

Don't go around. Vivian doesn't call Priya. When Vexmoor's analyst, Bea, sends 23 questions about Wrenfield's plan, Ines answers all 23 within three days, factually, with sources. On 22 June the board approves Lodestar with a condition: Vexmoor provides “independent assurance” at the steering committee. The contract is signed on 30 June.

  1. Ask who decides now

    In writing: who, when, on what papers.

  2. Learn the new language

    A cost reorg wants cost; a risk reorg wants risk.

  3. Help your sponsor make the case

    Their paper, your numbers.

  4. Hold the team with something written

    A letter of intent, not a promise on the phone.

  5. Answer every question fully and fast

    Including from a rival advising the client.

  6. Don't lobby around the process

    It embarrasses your sponsor and rarely works.

Vocabulary

Award limbo
The gap between being chosen and signing, when anything can happen.
Investment board
A group approving spending above a threshold.
Business case
The argument for spending money: costs, benefits, risks.
Independent assurance
A third party reviewing a programme for the sponsor.
Letter of intent
A written, limited commitment before the full contract.
Re-approval
Getting an already-made decision approved again under new rules.

5What the contract says what the preferred-bidder letter allowed

MetroVolt award letter · 6 May 202711:40
subject to

1 MetroVolt intends to award the Lodestar contract to Wrenfield Digital Ltd, subject to contract, to the standstill period, and to approval under Horizon governance.

the third “subject to” was the one that bit.

5 MetroVolt reserves the right not to proceed with the award, without liability for bidders' costs.

which is why we need a letter of intent before we spend.

In plain words: until a contract is signed, the client can stop without paying you anything. During a stall, anything you spend without a written interim agreement is your own money.

  • Short stall, small risk.
  • Keep the team informed.

Amber Lodestar now

  • New path, new language.
  • Letter of intent for the team.
  • Answer the rival's questions fully.

Red

the project may be cancelled

  • Prepare to release the team.
  • Leave gracefully; offer a smaller start.
  • Keep the relationship for next time.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Lobbying around the sponsor

    A call to the new boss behind your sponsor's back. Fix → help the sponsor make the case.

  • Speaking the old language

    A customer case to a cost board. Fix → rewrite for what the new board measures.

  • Letting the team scatter

    Your named people are reassigned. Fix → a letter of intent and a date.

  • Complaining about the rival adviser

    It sounds like sour grapes. Fix → answer fully; let the facts speak.

  • Spending unprotected

    If it's cancelled, it's your money. Fix → only within a written cap.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A client reorganises between award and signature.
  • A new approval step appears.
  • A rival gains influence over the decision.

Skip it when…

  • The delay is purely administrative and dated: just wait, politely.

11Self-check quiz

What three questions did Ines ask when the contract stalled?

Who decides now, when, and what the paper must contain.

Why rewrite the business case around cost?

Horizon was a cost-cutting programme; the board measured cost reduction.

Why answer Vexmoor's questions fully?

The board would hear how Wrenfield behaved; complaining would look like sour grapes.

Why not call Priya to “sort it”?

It would embarrass her and look like lobbying around the process.

What condition came with approval?

Vexmoor provides “independent assurance” at the Lodestar steering committee.

what would you do?Bea's questions include one that hints Wrenfield's plan is copied from Orion. How do you answer?

Factually: describe the plan's differences (waves, pilot gate, paid discovery) with references to the bid, without commenting on Orion or on the question's tone.

try it

Think of a decision you're waiting on that has moved to new people. Write down who decides now, when, and what they measure. How would you rewrite your case in their language?
