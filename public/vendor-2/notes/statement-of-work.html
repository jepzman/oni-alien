What Does the Contract Actually Say?

TL;DR

The statement of work is what you'll argue about for the next two years. Read it with the people who'll deliver it, not just with lawyers. Most of it is standard; five clauses decide almost every dispute: scope, assumptions, acceptance, change control and key personnel. Give each one a test question, and redline anything that fails, especially catch-all phrases like “all work reasonably necessary”.

1The situation The Grid, floor 19, 9 June 2027

From 10 May, Desmond, Anjali Rao (MetroVolt's legal counsel), Callum and Ines meet twice a week to turn the bid into a contract. Draft v0.3 arrives on Wednesday 9 June: 64 pages, 11 sections, 6 schedules.

  • Callum has read contracts. Hana, Bilal and Oskar, who'll live inside this one, never have.
  • Clause 2.4 says: “The Services include all work reasonably necessary to achieve the Objectives.” The Objectives include “a single view of every MetroVolt customer”.
  • The promises register flagged P-23: Ines's own words at the orals, “you won't see change requests for data”. It contradicts assumption A1-07.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Anjali drafts

MetroVolt's legal team writes for the worst day: if the supplier fails, the contract must make it possible to recover the money, the data and the time. A catch-all scope clause is how lawyers protect a client from gaps they can't foresee.

“If it's not in here, we can't make them do it.”

Wrenfield Ines, Hana and Callum read

For Wrenfield, every vague phrase is unpriced work. “All work reasonably necessary” to achieve a single view of 2.3 million customers is phase 2, 3 and 4, at a phase 1 price. Ines also needs to un-say P-23 before it becomes a clause.

“If it's in here vaguely, we'll do it for free.”

Both sides want the same thing, a contract that works on the worst day. They disagree about whose worst day.

3Real-life analogy the tenancy agreement

A tenancy agreement is twenty pages of standard text, and five clauses decide every argument: what's included, who fixes what, how the deposit comes back, how the agreement changes, and whom you call. Sensible tenants read those five properly and ask the landlord to change any that are vague, before signing, while everyone is still friendly.

4How it works five clauses, five test questions, and redlines

Ines books a two-hour reading session for the delivery team, with Callum explaining the legal words. They read the five clauses aloud, each with a test question:

  • §2 scope: could two people list the same work from this? Clause 2.4 fails. Redline: “The Services are those described in Schedule 2.”
  • §3 assumptions: is the consequence of a false assumption written? Yes, once “absorb” is struck. P-23 is fixed here: A1-07 stays, with its price, and Ines tells Elaine directly that her orals answer was wrong.
  • §5 acceptance: could we pass it, and what does silence mean? Draft says “to MetroVolt's satisfaction”. Redline (next note).
  • §7 change control: is it quick for small changes? No: every change needs steering approval. Redline: a small change pool.
  • §6 key personnel: what happens if someone is ill? Not covered. Added: replacement within 20 working days, with MetroVolt's approval.

The rest (liability, insurance, data protection, exit) is read once, checked by Wrenfield's lawyer and filed.

  1. Read it with the delivery team

    Two hours, the five clauses aloud.

  2. Give each clause a test question

    Could two people read it the same way?

  3. Hunt for catch-alls

    “Reasonably necessary”, “including but not limited to”, “to our satisfaction”.

  4. Check the bid made it in

    Assumptions, exclusions, the commercial model, the promises register.

  5. Fix promises you regret, openly

    In the contract and in person, before signing.

  6. Let the lawyers do the rest

    Liability, insurance, data protection, exit.

Vocabulary

Statement of work (SOW)
The part of a contract that says what will be done, how and for how much.
Schedule
An attachment to the contract with detail: solution, plan, rates, people.
Redline
A marked-up draft showing proposed deletions and additions.
Catch-all clause
Wording that sweeps unnamed work into scope.
Order of precedence
Which document wins when two disagree.
Entire agreement
A clause saying only what's in the contract counts.

5What the contract says the scope clause, before and after

Lodestar SOW · draft v0.3 · redlined9 June 2027
redlined

2.4 The Services include all work reasonably necessary to achieve the Objectives. The Services are those described in Schedule 2 (Solution) and Schedule 3 (Deliverables). The Objectives describe the purpose of the programme and do not of themselves create obligations.

Anjali accepted the second sentence on the third call.

1.3 In case of conflict, the order of precedence is: this SOW, its Schedules, the Tender Response, the Tender.

so the SOW wins over the bid. read it as if the bid didn't exist.

In plain words: a catch-all scope clause turns your client's ambitions into your obligations. Replace it with a reference to the schedules that describe the work, and say that objectives are purpose, not promises.

6Account health the same move, green, amber and red

Green

trust is high

  • Fewer redlines; a working session with legal.
  • Still read the five clauses as a team.
  • Keep a list of what you agreed and why.

Amber Lodestar now

burned before, careful lawyers

  • Five clauses, test questions.
  • Redline catch-alls with a clear alternative.
  • Fix regretted promises in person.

Red

take it or leave it

  • Price the vague clauses explicitly.
  • Escalate the few that could sink you.
  • Walk away if one of them can't move.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Lawyers only

    The people who'll live with it never read it. Fix → a reading session for the delivery team.

  • Missing catch-alls

    “All work reasonably necessary” slips through. Fix → hunt for the usual phrases.

  • Trusting the bid

    The SOW wins over the bid if they disagree. Fix → check the bid's protections made it in.

  • Hiding a regretted promise

    It becomes a clause. Fix → correct it openly, in writing and in person.

  • Redlines without alternatives

    “Delete” starts a fight. Fix → propose wording that protects both sides.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any contract you'll deliver under.
  • Before signing, while everyone is friendly.
  • When the bid had promises you regret.

Skip it when…

  • A standard order on your own terms you've used for years: a quick check is enough.
