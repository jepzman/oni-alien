a.k.a. “status reporting”: the first one sets the tone

What Goes in the First Status Report?

TL;DR

The first status report teaches the client how to read every report after it. Make the colour follow agreed definitions, not blame: if the project is behind, it's amber, whoever caused it. Put the bad news in the first line, facts before adjectives, owners next to every risk, and the decisions you need. Same format every week. A green report that turns red in month six is how Orion ended.

1The situation The Grid, Friday 30 July 2027

The first weekly status report is due on Friday 30 July. Discovery is nine working days behind: the laptops arrived on day 23, and MAVIS access hasn't.

  • Callum's draft is green: “Wrenfield is on track against its own activities. Delays are due to MetroVolt dependencies.”
  • His reasoning: amber looks like Wrenfield is failing, and it isn't Wrenfield's fault.
  • Elaine has read Orion's status reports. They were green for 22 months.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Elaine reads it

Elaine will read the first report for one thing: does this supplier tell the truth when it's inconvenient? A green report while her own team can't hand over laptops would tell her Wrenfield's colours mean nothing.

“If it's green now, I can't trust green later.”

Wrenfield Callum and Ines argue

Callum's worry is real: Graham, Priya and Vexmoor will all read the colour without the sentence. Ines's answer: the colour describes the project, not the fault. The sentence underneath says whose dependency it is, without blame. Amber now buys belief later.

“Our colours have to mean something from week one.”

A status report has one job: make the next decision easier. The colour only helps if everyone agrees what it means.

3Real-life analogy the weather forecast

A weather forecaster says “70 % chance of rain” because that's what the data says, not because rain would be someone's fault. A forecaster who said “sunny” to avoid upsetting people would be right some days, and never believed again after the first wedding washed out.

4How it works defined colours, bad news first, one page, every Friday

Ines and Callum agree colour definitions with Elaine first (they go in the governance schedule): green, on track for the next milestone; amber, at risk but recoverable with actions already planned; red, won't make the milestone without a decision from steering. By that definition, discovery is amber, whoever caused it.

The report is one page. Line one: AMBER. Discovery is 9 working days behind because the team cannot log in. Then four boxes: done (18 interviews, the RACI workshop, the kick-off), next (VPN, MAVIS access, the first design authority), risks and issues with owners (D-05 late, relief notice RN-01; D-09 at risk), and decisions needed (approve RN-01 at steering).

No adjectives without facts (“excellent progress” is deleted). No blame (“due to MetroVolt failures” becomes “D-05, delivered 27 July, promised 16 July”). Ines shows it to Elaine on Thursday, under the no-surprises pact. Elaine changes one word and says: “Amber. Good. Orion was green for 22 months.”

  1. Agree colour definitions first

    With the client, in writing.

  2. Colour the project, not the blame

    Whose fault goes in the sentence, not the colour.

  3. Bad news in line one

    The reader may read nothing else.

  4. Facts before adjectives

    Dates and numbers, not “excellent”.

  5. Owners and decisions

    Every risk has a name; every report asks for something.

  6. Same format every week

    So changes stand out.

5What the contract says what the contract says about reporting

Lodestar SOW · Schedule 7 (governance), §5v1.1 · 2 Aug 2027
defined

5.1 The Supplier shall provide a weekly status report by 17:00 each Friday in the format in Annex B.

promise P-12, now a clause.

5.2 Status colours have the meanings in Annex B and describe progress against the next Milestone, irrespective of cause.

“irrespective of cause”. Callum's sentence, in the end. he wrote it.

In plain words: if status colours are defined in the contract, “irrespective of cause”, nobody can argue the colour to avoid blame. The cause goes in the sentence underneath.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Colouring the blame

    Green, because it's not your fault. Fix → colours describe the project.

  • No definitions

    Everyone's amber means something different. Fix → define them, in writing.

  • Bad news at the bottom

    The reader stops at line two. Fix → line one.

  • Adjectives

    “Excellent progress”, nine days late. Fix → facts and dates.

  • Surprising the client lead

    They read it with their boss. Fix → show them first.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every regular report on a project.
  • The first one, especially.
  • A client burned by green reports.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip it; shrink it if the project is small.
