a.k.a. qualitative risk analysis: probability and impact

How Likely, and How Bad?

TL;DR

Qualitative analysis ranks risks quickly: score each one's probability and impact on the agreed scales, multiply, and plot them on a grid so the worst are visible. Add what the grid misses: how soon it could happen, how easy it is to spot, which risks share a cause. A score is only as good as the person who gave it, so score with people who know the work and the place, write down who scored, and re-score whenever someone better informed speaks up.

  • Lives in: the register's P, I and score columns, and a 5 × 5 grid

1The situation Antwerp, 10 February 2027

Two days after Maarten's review, Sanne phones Pieter, site manager of the Belgian builder. She asks him about R-07. “The permit is yours, not ours. With deliveries before seven it's the regular procedure, with a public inquiry. Four to six months is normal; longer if the neighbours object.” R-07 had been scored probability 1 in November, by a room of people who had opened Rotterdam and Zuid and had never applied for a permit in Belgium. The scale was fine. The scorers weren't the right ones.

  • Scores came from the wrong experience.
  • Nobody had written down who scored what.
  • The neighbours' risk and the permit risk shared a cause and were scored apart.

2Real-life analogy the local forecast

A couple planning a wedding on another country's coast guess from home: “It never rains there in June.” The local forecaster, who knows that coast, says 30 % chance of a storm in the afternoon, and when it comes it's heavy. Same scale, a better-informed score. They move the ceremony to the morning. The forecast didn't change the weather; it changed what they did.

3How it solves it the grid, the right scorers, and the re-score

Sanne re-scores with what Maarten and Pieter know. R-07, the permit: probability from 1 to 4 (50–70 % that it's not granted by early July, given four to six months and an application not yet started), impact from 3 to 4 (a 4–8 week slip, about €40k): 16, red, the top of the register. R-09, the neighbours, is merged into R-07 as one of its causes: their objections would happen in the permit's public inquiry. R-03, the builder, goes down from 9 to 6: Pieter has a second crew for the two weeks that matter. Three other columns are added to every row: who scored it, how soon it could strike (R-07: the application must go in by the end of March), and how we'd notice (the city's completeness letter; the inquiry's objections). Femke's Friday note that week opens with R-07. It goes over her threshold, so it's her decision how much to spend on it.

  1. Score with people who know the work and the place

    And write who scored.

  2. Use the agreed scales

    Words and numbers.

  3. Plot on the grid

    Red, amber, green.

  4. Add urgency and detectability

    How soon; how would we notice?

  5. Merge risks with a shared cause

    Score the cause once.

  6. Re-score when better information arrives

    The same day.

Vocabulary for the exam

Qualitative analysis
Ranking risks by scores on agreed scales, not by money or days.
Probability and impact matrix
A grid of probability against impact, coloured by score.
Risk score
Probability × impact on the agreed scales.
Urgency (proximity)
How soon a risk could happen, or a response must start.
Detectability
How easily you'd notice the risk starting to happen.
Data quality
How well the people scoring a risk know about it.

tools: the scales, a 5 × 5 grid, the people who know the place, and three extra columns.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • High, medium, low.
  • Sort the list.
  • Talk about the top three.

Standard

Antwerp

  • P × I on 1–5 scales, on a grid.
  • Who scored, how soon, how we'd notice.
  • Re-scored on new information.

Heavy

large programmes

  • Calibrated scales per objective.
  • Expert elicitation sessions.
  • Risk data quality assessments.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • The wrong scorers

    Confident and uninformed. Fix → score with people who know.

  • Anonymous scores

    Nobody can challenge them. Fix → write who scored.

  • Scored once

    New facts ignored. Fix → re-score the day they arrive.

  • Twins scored apart

    Shared causes hidden. Fix → merge risks with one cause.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • After identifying risks.
  • Whenever someone better informed speaks.
  • At each review for the top rows.
  • Before sizing the reserve.

Skip it when…

  • Never; on small work it's high, medium, low.

8Self-check quiz

What is a risk score?

Probability × impact on the agreed scales.

Why was R-07 scored 1 in November?

It was scored by people who knew Rotterdam, where a cold room needed no permit.

Why merge R-09 into R-07?

The neighbours' objections were a cause of the permit delay, through the public inquiry.

A supplier tells you a risk you scored 2 happens on “about half” of their jobs. What do you do?

Re-score it that day (probability 3–4), note who scored it, and check whether it crosses a threshold.

Two risks both score 12. One could strike next month, one in a year. Which first?

The near one: urgency decides between equal scores.

try it

For your top five risks, write who scored each one. For any scored without someone who has done this work in this place, ask such a person this week and re-score.

remember!!

Ask the forecaster who knows this coast. Then re-score.
