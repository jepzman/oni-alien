a.k.a. stakeholders abroad: regulators, local law, language and habits

Different Country, Different Rules

TL;DR

A project in a new country meets new regulators, new laws, a new language and new habits, inside a plan that looks just like the last one. Map the differences early, rule by rule, with someone who practises there (a lawyer, an agent, a local manager): what the rule is, who at your organisation owns it, by when it must be met, and how long the authority takes. Treat regulators as stakeholders with their own procedures and calendars. The differences you don't know are the ones that set the date.

1The situation Antwerp, 8 February 2027

Lena, Maarten (the Antwerp lawyer she has engaged) and Sanne walk through the launch plan rule by rule. Most of it Sanne knows: VAT, Bancontact, French and Dutch in the apps. Then Maarten reaches the cold room. “In Rotterdam your cold room needed nothing. Here, a cooling plant that size and lorries before seven need an environmental permit from the city, decided after a public inquiry. It can take months.” The plan has no line for it. The register has the permit office in the wrong box, and the risk register has the permit at a score of 3, with no owner.

  • The plan was Rotterdam's plan, translated.
  • The authority's calendar wasn't in the schedule.
  • The lawyer was asked about contracts, not about the plan.
meanwhile, in Eli’s & Grace’s notebook
  • Localisation for a New Country (Grace, vol. 6): Grace's localisation for Belgium: French and Dutch by native writers, Belgian VAT shown correctly, Bancontact first, tested with forty Antwerp households.
  • Operating in a Second Country (Eli, vol. 4): Eli's IT version of the same list: line lead time, two languages at the desk, Belgian holidays in the change calendar, Bancontact, AZERTY.

2Real-life analogy the hired car abroad

Hiring a car abroad, you drive the same way you always do, but the rules aren't the same: the other side of the road, different speed limits, a sticker for the motorway, priority from the right. The person at the hire desk can list them in five minutes. Those five minutes are the cheapest part of the holiday, and the only part most people skip.

3How it solves it one row per rule, an owner and a date, and the authority's calendar in the plan

The meeting ends with a map of nine differences, each with an owner and a date: the environmental permit (Sanne, then Hilde, a permit agent Maarten recommends, from 8 March); food-safety registration with FAVV-AFSCA before the first sale (Lena); VAT at 6 % and 21 % and Belgian invoices (Marco, March); Bancontact (Cho, July); staff documents in Dutch, as the law in Flanders requires (Joost); sector pay scales for store staff (Joost); courier contracts (Joost and Lena); beer and wine from 16, spirits from 18 (Grace); structured e-invoices for business customers (Ana, later). And three habits Maarten insists on: formal letters; an informal meeting with the city before applying, which is how things are done there; every decision confirmed in writing. The permit's calendar goes into the schedule the same week: an application, a completeness check, a 30-day public inquiry, a decision. Sanne re-places the permit office on the grid and takes the permit risk to the builder on Wednesday.

  1. Walk the plan rule by rule with a local expert

    Not only the contracts.

  2. Write each difference as a row

    The rule, the owner, the date.

  3. Put each authority's calendar in the schedule

    Steps, durations, inquiries.

  4. Ask about habits, not just law

    Letters, meetings, how decisions are confirmed.

  5. Update the register and the risks

    The same day.

  6. Hire local help where it pays

    An agent, a local manager.

Vocabulary for the exam

Regulator
An authority whose permit, registration or inspection the project needs.
Environmental permit
Permission from the authorities to run an installation or activity that may affect the surroundings.
Public inquiry
A period when the public can see an application and object to it.
Pre-application meeting
An informal meeting with an authority before applying.
Permit agent
A specialist who prepares and follows applications for you.
Enterprise environmental factors
Conditions outside the team's control (laws, culture, markets) that shape the project.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small job abroad

  • A call with a local partner.
  • A list of rules that differ.
  • Owners for each.

Standard

Antwerp

  • A rule-by-rule walk with a local lawyer.
  • A map with owners and dates.
  • Authorities' calendars in the schedule; a local agent.

Heavy

entering a regulated market

  • A formal regulatory assessment.
  • Local entity, local staff, local counsel.
  • A regulatory plan per authority.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • The plan translated

    The last country's assumptions. Fix → walk it rule by rule with a local.

  • Lawyer for contracts only

    The plan never gets checked. Fix → ask them about the plan.

  • Regulators as forms

    Their calendar sets your date. Fix → treat them as stakeholders, schedule their steps.

  • Law without habits

    Right papers, wrong way. Fix → ask how things are done there.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any project in a new country, region or sector.
  • When an authority's permission is needed.
  • Before the plan is baselined.
  • When local partners behave unexpectedly.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip it abroad; at home, a quick check for new rules.

8Self-check quiz

What did Maarten find that Sanne didn't know?

An environmental permit, with a public inquiry, for the cooling plant and deliveries before 07:00.

What goes in each row of the map?

The rule, what it means for the project, the owner, the date.

Name two of Maarten's habits.

Formal letters; meet the city informally before applying; confirm decisions in writing.

Your next project is in Germany. Your German colleague says “it's just like here.” What do you do?

Still walk the plan rule by rule with a local expert; similar isn't the same.

Why put the authority's steps into the schedule?

Their durations (inquiry, decision) can set the end date; outside the schedule they're invisible.

try it

Take your plan to someone who knows the place it will run in. Ask them to stop you at every line where the rules or habits differ. Put each into a table with an owner and a date.

remember!!

Same car, other rules. Ask at the hire desk.
