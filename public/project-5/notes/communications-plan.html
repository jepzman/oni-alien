Who Hears What, and When?

TL;DR

A communications plan says, for each audience: what they need to know, how (meeting, letter, email, a page), how often, in which language and tone, and who sends it. It's one table. A small team in one room can skip it; a project with authorities, neighbours, customers and teams in three languages can't. Write it from the stakeholder register, not from habit, and get outside texts checked by someone who writes the local language as it's spoken there.

1The situation Larder, 23 November 2026

For Rotterdam, Sanne tailored out the communications plan on 18 February: one team, one language, one building, a Thursday meeting and a Friday status were enough. Antwerp is different. The city, the landlord and the street work in Dutch, in its Flemish form; part of the customers want French; Larder works in English. The first letter to the landlord, drafted by Larder's marketing team in Amsterdam, comes back with a polite note from Maarten: correct, but blunt; in Belgium you'd open differently.

  • Thirty-one stakeholders, three languages, two countries' habits.
  • Nobody had said who speaks to whom.
  • Netherlands Dutch reads differently in Antwerp.

2Real-life analogy the airport

An airport tells one flight's news to four audiences. Passengers hear an announcement in two languages and read the gate screen. The crew get a briefing in English. The control tower gets an exact message in a fixed format. Families waiting outside read the arrivals board. Nobody gets everything; everybody gets what they need, in their language, through their channel, at the right moment.

3How it solves it one table, built from the register

Sanne writes the plan on 23 November, one row per audience from the register. For each: what they need (not what Larder wants to say), how, how often, the language, and one owner. Femke gets a short note every Friday and the steering group a page a month; the permit office gets formal letters, from March through Hilde, the permit agent; the landlord, letters and a monthly call from Femke, who signed the lease; the neighbours, a quarterly meeting and a letter in Dutch with a French summary; the waitlist, a monthly email in the language each person chose. One rule covers every outside text: Dutch texts are written or checked by a Flemish writer (Maarten's office until Grace's localisation writers start in the spring). The landlord's letter is rewritten that week and answered within two days.

  1. Start from the register

    One row per audience.

  2. Write what they need to know

    Not what you want to say.

  3. Choose the channel and rhythm

    Meeting, letter, email, page; how often.

  4. Fix the language and the tone

    As it's spoken there.

  5. Name one owner per audience

    One voice each.

  6. Review it when the register changes

    With the engagement plan.

Vocabulary for the exam

Communications plan
Who gets what information, how, how often, in which language, from whom.
Push communication
Sent to people: letters, emails, notes.
Pull communication
Put where people can fetch it: a page, a board.
Interactive communication
Two-way: meetings, calls, workshops.
Register (language)
The level of formality a text uses.
Localisation
Adapting text to how a language is used in a place, not just translating it.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

one team, one language

  • No written plan.
  • A weekly meeting and a status.
  • Say who speaks to the sponsor.

Standard

Antwerp

  • One table from the register.
  • Language and tone per audience.
  • One owner each.

Heavy

public or multinational programmes

  • A communications manager.
  • Approval steps for outside messages.
  • Translation and accessibility standards.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Same message for all

    Nobody gets what they need. Fix → write what each audience needs.

  • Translated, not localised

    Correct and wrong in tone. Fix → a local writer checks.

  • Many voices

    Mixed messages. Fix → one owner per audience.

  • Plan as paperwork

    Written, never used. Fix → keep it to one table, review it monthly.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Several audiences, especially outside the organisation.
  • More than one language or country.
  • Authorities with formal channels.
  • When people say “nobody told us”.

Skip it when…

  • One team, one language, one room: a meeting and a status are the plan.

8Self-check quiz

What five things does each row of the plan say?

What they need, how, how often, which language, who sends it.

Why did Rotterdam have no communications plan?

Tailored out: one team, one language, one building.

What was wrong with the first landlord letter?

Correct but blunt: Netherlands Dutch habits; rewritten with a Flemish writer.

Priya's team wants to post opening news to the neighbours themselves. What does Sanne say?

The neighbours have one owner (Sanne, then the store manager); the news goes through them, in the agreed letter.

A French-speaking customer complains the waitlist email is Dutch only. What's the fix?

The plan says French or Dutch by choice; check the sign-up field and resend in French.
