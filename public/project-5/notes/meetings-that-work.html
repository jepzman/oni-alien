a.k.a. managing communications: making sure the message arrived and was understood

Are We Being Understood?

TL;DR

Managing communications means checking that a message was received and understood, not only sent. In meetings: an agenda naming the decisions needed, sent ahead in the other side's language; the right voice in the room; every decision read back before moving on; a short written summary within a day, which the other side confirms. Nodding means “I follow you”, not “I agree”. Across languages and cultures, assume nothing was agreed until it's written and confirmed.

1The situation Antwerp, 3 February 2027

This is Sanne's mistake for this collection. She meets Koen, sales manager of a produce wholesaler at Antwerp's early market, for ninety minutes, in English, with no agenda. Koen is friendly and nods a lot. Her notes say: deliveries at 07:00, crates returned free. Nine days later his proposal arrives: deliveries at 05:30, as for all his shops, and a €2 deposit on every crate. Neither of them was wrong about what they'd heard. Nothing had been agreed. Two weeks are lost, and Koen, who thinks Larder is changing its mind, is now resistant.

  • No agenda: nobody knew which decisions the meeting was for.
  • English was Sanne's comfort, not Koen's.
  • Nodding was taken for agreement; nothing was written down.

2Real-life analogy the read-back

When a control tower tells a pilot “climb to flight level two hundred”, the instruction doesn't count until the pilot reads it back: “climbing to flight level two hundred.” If the read-back is wrong, the tower corrects it at once. Aviation learned the hard way that a message sent is not a message understood, even between two professionals speaking the same language every day.

3How it solves it agenda, voice, read-back, summary, confirmation

Sanne re-runs the meeting on Wednesday 17 February, in five steps. An agenda in Dutch goes out two days ahead with the three decisions needed: delivery time, crates, the weekly order cut-off. Larder's operations lead, a Flemish speaker, leads in Dutch; Sanne listens. Each decision is read back before the next one starts. A summary in Dutch goes out within 24 hours, and Koen confirms it by email the next morning. Four decisions: deliveries at 05:30 at the back door, Monday to Saturday; €2 deposit a crate, credited on return; orders by 14:00 the day before; a trial week before opening. Koen is supportive by the end of the month. Sanne notes one consequence: 05:30 deliveries need the store's early hours to be allowed, and writes it against the permit risk, R-07.

  1. Send an agenda that names the decisions

    In their language, ahead of time.

  2. Bring the right voice

    Someone who speaks it as they do.

  3. Read back each decision

    Before the next one starts.

  4. Write a summary within a day

    Decisions, owners, dates.

  5. Ask them to confirm it

    Silence isn't agreement.

  6. Note what the decisions touch

    Risks, plan, contracts.

Vocabulary for the exam

Manage communications
Making sure information is sent, received and understood.
Read-back
Repeating a decision to confirm both sides mean the same.
Agenda
The purpose and the decisions a meeting is for.
Meeting summary
Decisions, owners and dates, sent after the meeting.
Noise
Anything that distorts a message: language, jargon, assumptions.
High-context culture
Where much of the meaning is left unsaid and read from the situation.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

  • Say the decision aloud at the end.
  • One line in chat after.
  • Done.

Standard

Antwerp

  • Agenda with decisions, in their language.
  • Read-back in the room.

contracts, regulators

  • Formal minutes, signed.
  • Interpreters for key meetings.
  • Decisions only in writing.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • No agenda

    Nobody knows what to decide. Fix → name the decisions needed.

  • Your language, not theirs

    They follow; they don't agree. Fix → meet in their language, or with a voice who speaks it.

  • Nodding taken as yes

    Two versions of the meeting. Fix → read each decision back.

  • No summary

    Memory decides. Fix → write it within a day, ask them to confirm.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any meeting that should end in a decision.
  • Across languages or companies.
  • With suppliers and authorities.
  • When people later remember a meeting differently.

Skip it when…

  • A daily stand-up within a team: a board already records it.

8Self-check quiz

What does nodding mean in a meeting?

Often “I follow you”, not “I agree”.

Name the five steps of the second meeting.

Agenda in Dutch, the right voice, read-backs, summary in 24 h, confirmation.

What did decision D1 touch?

The store's permitted hours: deliveries before 07:00 → the permit risk R-07.

A supplier's summary says something different from yours. What do you do?

Call the same day, agree the wording, send one corrected summary both confirm.

Your sponsor wants to skip the agenda “to keep it informal” with a regulator. Answer them.

Informal is fine; unclear isn't. An agenda with the questions to answer respects their time and gives a record.

try it

After your next meeting, write the decisions in three lines and send them to everyone who was there. Count how many replies correct something.

remember!!

Sent is not understood. Read it back.
