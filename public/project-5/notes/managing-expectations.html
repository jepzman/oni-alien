How Do We Keep Them With Us?

TL;DR

Managing engagement is the work of keeping stakeholders with the project: meeting them early, before decisions are fixed; listening to what worries them; making only promises you can keep, and keeping them visibly; and saying plainly what you can't promise yet, with a date for the answer. Expectations are set by the first thing people hear, so make it true. Link every concern to the plan: a worry is often a risk in disguise.

  • Family: who's involved
  • Lives in: meetings, a log of concerns and commitments
  • Pairs with: the engagement plan, the communications plan, the risk register

1The situation Larder, January 2027

Rotterdam's neighbours got a letter on 2 March 2026, eight weeks before opening, and complained about e-bikes on the pavement in the first week. For Antwerp, Sanne wants to meet the street before anything is fixed. At the same time Femke is preparing an investor update that says “Antwerp opens in September.” Two groups are about to form their expectations from the first thing they hear. One is a street that hasn't been asked; the other is a board that would hear a planning date as a promise.

  • People who hear late feel decided upon.
  • A planning date said as a promise becomes a promise.
  • Concerns heard in a meeting often go nowhere.

2Real-life analogy the extension next door

A family planning an extension knocks on the neighbours' doors before the skip arrives. They show the drawing, say the builders start in June and work eight till five, and leave a phone number. They don't promise it will be quiet; they promise to tell them before the noisy week, and they do. The neighbours put up with months of dust. The family down the road who didn't knock got a complaint to the council in week one.

3How it solves it meet early, promise little, keep every promise, link concerns to risks

On Thursday 21 January at 19:00, at the community centre, twenty-eight people from the forty-six households come, with the head of the school. Sanne shows the store layout and the routes, then mostly listens. Twelve raise lorries before 07:00 (Koen's wholesaler delivers at 05:30), nine e-bikes on the pavement, five condenser noise at night, two litter. She promises four things she controls: e-bikes leave by the back lane, not past the school; a named contact; a meeting every quarter; a daily litter walk. Lorry times she doesn't promise: “we'll answer by the end of March.” With Femke she agrees the investor wording: “planning date September 2027, confirmed when the store's permits are in.” One thing she misses. The lorry concern goes into the stakeholder log, but not into the risk register, where the permit risk sits rated low. The twelve people who raised it are the people who can object when the permit goes to a public inquiry.

  1. Meet people before decisions are fixed

    Early enough that you can still change something.

  2. Listen more than you present

    Write every concern down.

  3. Promise only what you control

    And keep it visibly.

  4. Say what you can't promise, with a date

    “We'll answer by…”.

  5. Link each concern to the plan

    Is it a risk, an issue, a change?

  6. Set dates as ranges or planning dates

    Until they're safe to promise.

Vocabulary for the exam

Engagement
Working with stakeholders so they understand and support the project, or at least aren't surprised.
Expectation
What a stakeholder believes the project will deliver, and when.
Commitment log
The promises made to stakeholders, with owners and dates.
Planning date
A date to plan towards, not yet promised.
Concern
A worry a stakeholder raises; often a risk in disguise.
Under-promise
Commit to less than you expect to achieve, so you can keep it.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

internal change

  • Talk to the affected people early.
  • Write down what you promised.
  • Follow up.

Standard

Antwerp

  • A meeting before decisions are fixed.
  • A commitment log with owners.
  • Concerns linked to risks; planning dates, not promises.

Heavy

public works

  • Formal consultation phases.
  • Published responses to every concern.
  • Community liaison officers.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Telling instead of asking

    People feel decided upon. Fix → meet before decisions are fixed.

  • Promising to please

    Broken later. Fix → promise only what you control.

  • Planning dates said as promises

    The board remembers the promise. Fix → say “planning date” until it's safe.

  • Concerns left in the minutes

    A worry is often a risk. Fix → link each to the register.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Before decisions that affect people are fixed.
  • Whenever a date is said outside the team.
  • When stakeholders change mood.
  • Through the whole project, not just the start.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip it; on small work it's a conversation.

8Self-check quiz

Why did Sanne meet the street in January?

Before anything was fixed, so concerns could still change the plan; Rotterdam's letter came too late.

What did she not promise, and why?

Lorry times: she didn't control them yet; an answer by the end of March.

What was the investor wording?

“Planning date September 2027, confirmed when the store's permits are in.”

At the meeting a neighbour asks Sanne to promise no noise at all. What does she say?

She can't promise that; she promises what Larder controls (routes, contact, meetings) and to warn them before noisy work.

Which link did Sanne miss, and why did it matter?

The lorry concern wasn't linked to the permit risk; those neighbours could object in the permit's public inquiry.

try it

List the promises your project has made to people outside the team. Which ones do you control? For each concern you've heard, write whether it's a risk, an issue or a change, and put it there.

remember!!

Knock before the skip. Promise only what you control.
