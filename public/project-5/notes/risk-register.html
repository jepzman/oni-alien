a.k.a. identifying risks: the risk register

What Could Happen?

TL;DR

Identify risks with the people who know the work and the place: walk the plan, the categories, past lessons, assumptions and the experts. Write each one as cause, event, effect (“because X, Y may happen, which would Z”), so you know what to watch and what it's worth. Give each a score, an owner inside the organisation, a response and a review date, in a register. Include opportunities: good things that might happen if you help them. A register is only as good as its least-checked row.

1The situation Larder, 30 November 2026

This is Sanne's mistake for this collection. The leads' workshop produces 23 risks and Sanne writes them up on Monday 30 November. Row R-07 reads: permit delayed. Probability 1, impact 3, score 3, green. Owner: the builder. Response: “builder handles permits.” In Rotterdam that was true. The row breaks two rules of the page Femke signed two weeks earlier: the owner isn't at Larder, and the score comes from another country. Nobody notices, because a green row near the bottom is the last thing anyone reads.

  • The risk was written as a worry (“permit delayed”), not as cause, event and effect.
  • Its owner had no reason, or contract, to watch it for Larder.
  • The neighbours' risk, R-09, had no owner at all.
meanwhile, in Ana’s & Grace’s notebook
  • Risk Register (Ana, vol. 6): Ana's architecture risk register (from April 2028) writes each risk as cause, event and effect, with an owner and a response, and reviews it with the teams.
  • Pre-mortem (Grace, vol. 3): Grace's pre-mortem imagines the launch has already failed and asks the team why: a quick way to surface risks nobody wants to say.

2Real-life analogy the harbour chart

A pilot bringing a ship into a harbour uses a chart that marks every known hazard: the sandbank, the wreck, the strong tide at the entrance, each with what to do: keep to the buoys, stay 200 metres clear, enter at slack water. A hazard written as “water” helps nobody. A chart of another harbour is worse than none: it gives confidence and the wrong depths.

3How it solves it cause, event, effect; an owner inside Larder; opportunities too

The workshop on Thursday 26 November uses four sources: the categories from the risk approach (site, people, systems, partners, Belgium, market), Rotterdam's lessons, the plan's assumptions (“the builder handles permits” was one), and a pre-mortem, like Grace's: “It's October 2027 and Antwerp opened late. Why?” It finds 19 threats and 4 opportunities. Sanne writes each as cause, event, effect, scores it on the agreed scales, and gives it an owner, a response and a review date. The top three are hiring forty people on time, the builder, and the courier contracts. Two opportunities get owners at once: reuse Rotterdam's shelving design (Tomás) and ask the landlord to pay for roof insulation (Femke). What the room lacks is anyone who knows Belgian permits. R-07 is fixed on 10 February, after Maarten and Pieter, and rewritten as a sentence (see “going deeper”).

  1. Gather people who know the work and the place

    Including a local expert.

  2. Use several sources

    Categories, lessons, assumptions, a pre-mortem.

  3. Write each as cause, event, effect

    So it can be watched and valued.

  4. Score it on the agreed scales

    And note who scored it.

  5. Give it an owner inside the organisation

    And a response and a date.

  6. Add opportunities

    With owners too.

Vocabulary for the exam

Risk register
The list of risks with their scores, owners, responses and status.
Threat
A risk that would hurt the project if it happened.
Opportunity
A risk that would help the project if it happened.
Risk statement
“Because [cause], [event] may happen, which would [effect].”
Assumption
Something taken as true without proof; each one hides a risk.
Pre-mortem
Imagine the project has failed and ask why.

＋Going deeper writing a risk, where risks come from, opportunities, and keeping a register alive

Most risk registers start as a good workshop and end as a forgotten spreadsheet. This chapter shows how to write a risk so it can be acted on, where to look for the ones a workshop misses, why opportunities belong in the same table, and the five columns that keep a register alive.

1Writing a risk: cause, event, effect

“Permit delayed” is a worry. A risk statement has three parts: because of a cause (a fact, true today), an event may happen (uncertain), which would have an effect on an objective (dates, money, quality). The cause tells you what to watch and where a response can act. The effect tells you what it's worth, so you can score it. The event in the middle is what you're actually uncertain about.

Rewritten on 10 February, R-07 told Sanne three things the November row hadn't: the cause was a Belgian rule (so a Belgian expert should own the paperwork), the event had a date (granted by early July), and the effect had a size (4–8 weeks, about €40k).

2Where risks come from, and where workshops miss them

A workshop finds the risks the people in the room know about. To find the rest, use sources that don't depend on memory:

  • Categories (a risk breakdown structure): site, people, systems, partners, rules, market. Walk each one.
  • Assumptions: every “we assume” in the plan is a risk with its probability hidden. “The builder handles permits” was one.
  • Lessons from earlier projects, and their issue logs: Rotterdam's internet line.
  • Experts outside the room, especially on a new place: Maarten found in an hour what the workshop missed.
  • A pre-mortem: imagining failure makes it safe to say the awkward thing.

3Opportunities belong in the same table

A risk can be good: something that might happen and would help. Antwerp had four. Writing them down gives them an owner, and owned opportunities happen more often. O-01, reusing Rotterdam's shelving design, saved about €6k because Tomás sent the drawings to Pieter in January instead of waiting to be asked. O-02, the landlord paying for roof insulation, didn't happen; Femke asked, and the landlord said no. Both cost nothing to try.

Opportunities also balance the numbers: the reserve is sized from threats minus opportunities (two notes on).

4Keeping it alive: five columns and one habit

A register stays useful when every row has five things: a score on agreed scales, an owner inside the organisation, a response (not “monitor”), a trigger (the sign that it's starting to happen) and a next review date. Rows missing any of them are where surprises come from: R-07 and R-09 were both missing an owner.

The habit: review the bottom of the list too. A green row scored by the wrong person is more dangerous than a red one everyone watches. From February Sanne reads three low-scoring rows aloud at every review and asks: who scored this, and would they still?

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • Five risks in a list.
  • An owner each.
  • Reviewed at check-ins.

Standard

Antwerp

  • A register of cause, event, effect.
  • Score, owner, response, trigger, date.
  • Opportunities too; reviewed fortnightly.

Heavy

large programmes

  • A risk breakdown structure.
  • Risk workshops per phase.
  • Registers rolled up to programme and portfolio.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Worries, not statements

    Nothing to watch or value. Fix → cause, event, effect.

  • Owner outside the organisation

    Nobody watches it for you. Fix → a Larder owner, always.

  • Only threats

    Good luck goes unclaimed. Fix → add opportunities with owners.

  • Unread bottom rows

    Wrong scores hide there. Fix → read a few low rows aloud each review.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • At the start, with the plan.
  • At each phase and each new place.
  • When an assumption turns out false.
  • When an expert joins.

Skip it when…

  • Never; on small work it's a list of five.

8Self-check quiz

What three parts make a risk statement?

Cause, event, effect: “because X, Y may happen, which would Z.”

Which two rules did R-07 break?

Its owner wasn't at Larder; it was scored by Rotterdam's rules, by nobody who knew Belgium.

Why put opportunities in the register?

Owned opportunities happen more often, and they balance the reserve.

A lead says “we assume the landlord hands over the keys on 1 June.” What do you do?

Write it as a risk: because the lease date depends on the landlord's works, keys may come late, which would delay the fit-out; score it, owner Femke.

At a review, a green row's score was set by a supplier. What do you ask?

Who at Larder owns it, and would someone who knows the subject still score it that low?
