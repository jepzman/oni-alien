a.k.a. “the engagement matrix”: planning stakeholder engagement with power and interest

Who Matters Most, and How Much?

TL;DR

Not every stakeholder needs the same amount of you. Place each on a power and interest grid: high power and high interest, manage closely; high power, low interest, keep satisfied; low power, high interest, keep informed; the rest, monitor. Then mark on an engagement matrix where each one is now (unaware, resistant, neutral, supportive, leading) and where the project needs them. The gaps are the plan. Place them by this project's rules, not the last one's: power is what they can do to you here.

1The situation Larder, 9 November 2026

This is Sanne's mistake for this collection. She places the thirty-one stakeholders on a power and interest grid. The city's permit office goes low on power: in Rotterdam the cold room needed only a notice, so she assumes the Antwerp permit is a form the builder fills in. Its box says keep informed: one letter, sent by the builder. In Antwerp, a cooling plant that size and lorries before 07:00 need an environmental permit, with a public inquiry the neighbours can object to. Without it there is no opening. Nobody finds out until 8 February.

  • Power was judged by habit, from another country's rules.
  • “Keep informed” meant nobody owned the relationship.
  • Current and desired attitudes looked the same, so there was nothing to do.

2Real-life analogy the street party

Planning a street party, you give most of your time to the neighbours who'll bake and set up: they care, and the day depends on them. The council officer who issues the road-closure permit doesn't care about your party, but can cancel it, so you visit early and hand in a tidy form. The shopkeeper at the end of the road is interested, so you keep them informed. The dog walker gets a flyer. Get the council officer wrong and the bakers have nowhere to put the cakes.

3How it solves it the grid, the matrix, and the move of 8 February

Sanne's November grid had the permit office in the wrong box. The correction comes on 8 February 2027, when Maarten walks through the Belgian rules with Lena and her: “In Rotterdam your cold room needed nothing. Here it's a permit, with a public inquiry.” The permit office moves to manage closely, beside Femke, Joost, the landlord, the builder, the food-safety agency and the neighbours. On the engagement matrix it is unaware of Larder now and needs to be supportive, meaning confident in a complete application: a gap of two columns, and a named owner (Sanne, until Hilde, the permit agent, starts in March). Two other gaps get actions the same day: the food-safety agency (unaware → neutral: Lena books the registration) and Koen, the wholesaler (resistant → supportive: see “Are we being understood?”). Three months had gone by. That cost is told in the risk notes.

  1. Place each stakeholder by power and interest

    Power: what they can do to the project here.

  2. Check the high-power ones against local rules

    Ask someone who knows.

  3. Choose an approach per box

    Manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor.

  4. Mark current and desired attitude

    Unaware, resistant, neutral, supportive, leading.

  5. Turn every gap into an action

    With an owner and a date.

  6. Re-place them when things change

    Monthly, and after any surprise.

＋Going deeper the grid's four boxes, the engagement matrix, other lenses, and how placing goes wrong

The grid is the most-drawn picture in stakeholder work and the easiest to get wrong. This chapter explains what each box asks of you, how the engagement matrix turns the grid into actions, two other lenses that catch what the grid misses, and four ways a stakeholder lands in the wrong box.

1The four boxes, and what each asks of you

  • Manage closely (high power, high interest): regular, personal, two-way. You ask them before you decide. Femke, Joost, the landlord, and, as it turned out, the permit office.
  • Keep satisfied (high power, low interest): enough attention that they never have a reason to start caring. Marco on Antwerp's spending; the fire service.
  • Keep informed (low power, high interest): regular, clear updates; listen to them, they often see problems first. The waitlist; the school.
  • Monitor (low, low): watch for change. The press, the rival.

The boxes are about your time, not about who matters as a person. A box is a decision about how much of you each stakeholder gets.

2The engagement matrix: from picture to plan

The grid says how much attention; the engagement matrix says what for. For each stakeholder mark where they are now (C) and where the project needs them (D) on five levels: unaware, resistant, neutral, supportive, leading.

Not everyone needs to be supportive. A regulator should stay neutral and fair; what you need is that it understands your application. Where C and D are the same, keep doing what you do. Where they differ, write an action with an owner and a date. In November the permit office had C = D = neutral, so there was nothing to do; that was the mistake hiding inside the first one.

3Two other lenses

The grid has two axes, and some stakeholders don't fit them. Two other lenses help:

  • Salience: how much a stakeholder pulls on the project, from three things: power (can they make it happen or stop it?), legitimacy (is their claim recognised, by law or custom?) and urgency (do they need an answer now?). A regulator scores high on all three the day a deadline comes. Neighbours gain legitimacy in a public inquiry.
  • Direction of influence: upward (sponsor, board), downward (team), sideways (peers, other departments), outward (suppliers, authorities, the community). Antwerp's surprises all came from outward.

4Four ways a stakeholder lands in the wrong box

  • By habit: placed as they were on the last project. The permit office, placed by Rotterdam's rules.
  • By org chart: senior people placed high, outsiders low. A clerk with a stamp can stop a launch.
  • Power to help mistaken for power to stop: the landlord can help; the permit office can stop. Look for both.
  • Placed once: the neighbours' power rose the day the public inquiry opened. Re-place after every change of phase.

The cure for all four is the same: for each high-power stakeholder, ask someone who knows this place what that stakeholder can actually do to the project, and by when.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small internal project

  • A quick sort: who decides, who's affected.
  • No grid.
  • Talk to the first group weekly.

Standard

Antwerp

  • Grid and engagement matrix.
  • Gaps turned into owned actions.
  • Re-placed monthly.

Heavy

public infrastructure, mergers

  • Formal stakeholder analysis with salience.
  • Engagement strategies per group.
  • Independent consultation records.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Placed by habit

    Last project's rules. Fix → check power against this place's rules.

  • Senior = powerful

    A permit clerk can stop you. Fix → ask what each can do to the project.

  • No current vs desired

    A grid with nothing to do. Fix → mark C and D, act on the gaps.

  • Drawn once

    Power shifts with phases. Fix → re-place monthly and after surprises.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • After the register, before the communications plan.
  • When entering a new country or organisation.
  • When a stakeholder surprises you.
  • At each phase.

Skip it when…

  • A two-person job with one customer you see every day.

A new project in a city you know well: do you still check the high-power boxes with someone local?

Yes, briefly: rules and people change; the check costs an hour.

The food-safety agency only needs to be neutral, not supportive. Why?
