a.k.a. planning risk responses: avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept, escalate

What Do We Do About It?

TL;DR

For each important risk, choose what to do, not just what to watch. For a threat: avoid it (change the plan so it can't happen), transfer it (to someone better placed, by contract or insurance), mitigate it (make it less likely or less bad), or accept it (with money set aside and a trigger, or knowingly). For an opportunity: exploit, enhance, share or accept. If it's not the project's to handle, escalate it to whoever owns it. Check that a response is worth what it costs, and what risk it leaves behind.

  • Lives in: the register's response, owner, trigger and cost columns
  • Pairs with: the risk register, expected monetary value, implementing responses

1The situation Antwerp, 22 February 2027

R-07, the permit, is now red at 16, owned by Sanne since 10 February. The register's response column still says “monitor”. So do six others. Watching a risk is not a response; it's hope, with a review date. The steering group meets on 5 March and Femke will ask what's being done. The four opportunities have no responses at all.

  • “Monitor” stood in for a decision.
  • Nobody had compared what a response costs with what it saves.
  • Opportunities were listed and left alone.

2Real-life analogy the storm

A homeowner facing storms has four answers. Avoid: don't build on the cliff edge. Transfer: buy insurance, so someone else pays for the damage. Mitigate: fix the loose tiles and clear the gutters, so the damage is smaller. Accept: keep some savings for whatever's left. Sensible homeowners use three of the four. Insurance alone doesn't keep the rain out of the bedroom.

3How it solves it four options per threat, a sum, a trigger, and the opportunities

On Monday 22 February Sanne runs a response workshop with Joost, Lena, Maarten and Pieter. For R-07 they weigh all four. Avoid: deliveries only after 07:00 and a smaller cooling plant, so no permit is needed; Joost rejects it, since Koen delivers at 05:30 and a smaller plant can't hold the range. Transfer: hire Hilde, a permit agent Maarten recommends, for €6k from the Belgian set-up line, from 8 March. Maarten warns: “She takes the paperwork. The date stays yours.” Mitigate: an informal meeting with the city on Thursday 11 March; the cooling supplier's drawings by Monday 15 March (€2k to bring them forward); the application lodged by Friday 26 March. Accept the rest: the contingency covers it, with a trigger: not lodged by 26 March goes to the steering group. The sum: before, 60 % × €40k = €24k; after, 30 % × €40k = €12k; the responses cost €8k; they're worth it, and they shorten the late tail. Then the rest of the register: R-04, Bancontact certification, is escalated to Cho's payments roadmap, where it belongs; Tomás sends Rotterdam's shelving drawings to Pieter (exploit O-01); Femke will ask the landlord about insulation (enhance O-02).

  1. Consider all the options

    Avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept; escalate if it's not yours.

  2. Price each response

    What it costs, what it saves.

  3. Choose, often more than one

    Name the residual risk.

  4. Set a trigger for the rest

    The sign that it's happening, and who acts.

  5. Look for secondary risks

    What the response itself could cause.

  6. Do the same for opportunities

    Exploit, enhance, share, accept.

Vocabulary for the exam

Avoid
Change the plan so the threat can't happen or can't affect the project.
Transfer
Move the threat's consequences, and its handling, to a third party.
Mitigate
Reduce the probability or the impact.
Accept
Take the risk: actively (a reserve, a fallback) or passively.
Escalate
Hand a risk to the person who owns it, outside the project.
Exploit / enhance / share
Make an opportunity certain / more likely / shared with a partner who can help.
Residual risk
What remains after the response.
Secondary risk
A new risk caused by a response.
Trigger
A sign that a risk is about to happen, or a date by which a response must have worked.

＋Going deeper threat responses, opportunity responses, is it worth it, and what a response leaves behind

Choosing a response is a small business decision: what will we spend to change the odds or the damage? This chapter goes through the five threat responses and the four opportunity responses with Antwerp's examples, shows the sum that tells you whether a response is worth it, and covers the risks a response leaves behind or creates.

1Five answers to a threat

  • Avoid: change the plan so the threat can't happen: a different design, a different site, a smaller scope. Often the strongest and most expensive. Antwerp's (no early deliveries, a smaller plant) was rejected in February, and partly adopted in July.
  • Transfer: hand the consequences to someone better placed: a fixed-price contract, a late penalty, insurance, an agent. It moves money or work; it rarely moves the date. Hilde took the permit paperwork; the opening date stayed Larder's.
  • Mitigate: act now to make the threat less likely (meet the city early) or less bad (drawings ready, so a re-application is quick).
  • Accept: actively, with a reserve and a fallback plan ready; or passively, deciding to deal with it if it comes. The rival opening first was accepted passively.
  • Escalate: the threat belongs to someone outside the project. Bancontact certification was Cho's roadmap's; the project told Cho and stopped pretending to own it.

2Four answers to an opportunity

  • Exploit: make it certain. Tomás sent Rotterdam's shelving drawings in January; about €6k saved.
  • Enhance: make it more likely. Femke asked the landlord to pay for roof insulation, with the energy numbers. (The answer was no; it cost a letter.)
  • Share: bring in a partner who can help it happen and share the benefit. The bakery next door ran a joint opening offer: a free loaf with a first order, a Larder voucher with a coffee.
  • Accept: take it if it comes. The waitlist grew faster than planned; nothing to do but staff for it.

3Is a response worth it?

Compare the expected cost of the risk before and after the response, and the cost of the response. For R-07: before, 60 % × €40k = €24k; after, 30 % × €40k = €12k; the responses cost €8k. €12k less exposure for €8k: worth it.

Two cautions. A response can be worth it even when the sum is close, if it shortens a tail the sponsor fears (here, the simulation's late runs). And a response that costs more than it saves can still be right when the effect is one the sponsor won't accept at any odds; that's what appetite is for.

4What a response leaves behind

  • Residual risk: what's left. R-07 went from 16 to 12, still red. The residual is accepted with a reserve and a trigger.
  • Secondary risk: what the response creates. An agent who goes on holiday in July; Hilde named a stand-in.
  • Fallback plan: what you'll do if the response fails: here, open with dry goods only (rejected in July, but worth having thought about).
  • Triggers: a date or a sign that says it's time to act: “not lodged by 26 March.” A trigger needs someone watching it. The next note is about what happened when nobody was.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • One action per top risk.
  • An owner and a date.
  • No sums.

Standard

Antwerp

  • All four options weighed for the red risks.
  • Cost vs saving; residual and secondary risks.
  • Triggers; opportunities too; escalate what isn't yours.

Heavy

large programmes

  • Response plans per risk with budgets.
  • Contracts and insurance designed around risk.
  • Fallback plans rehearsed.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • “Monitor” as a response

    Hope with a review date. Fix → choose a real response.

  • Transfer and forget

    The date doesn't move with the paperwork. Fix → keep owning the outcome.

  • No sum

    Responses that cost more than they save. Fix → compare before, after and cost.

  • No trigger

    The residual surprises you. Fix → a date or a sign, and a watcher.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • For every red and amber risk.
  • When a risk crosses a threshold.
  • When opportunities appear.
  • When a risk belongs to someone else.

Skip it when…

  • Green risks you accept knowingly: say so in one word.

8Self-check quiz

Name the threat responses.

Avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept, and escalate.

Name the opportunity responses.

Exploit, enhance, share, accept.

What did Hilde transfer, and what didn't she?

The paperwork; not the date, which stayed Larder's.

A risk's response costs €10k and cuts its EMV from €12k to €6k. Do it?

Not on the sum (€6k saved for €10k), unless it shortens a tail the sponsor won't accept; say which.

Your payments lead says Bancontact certification is “the project's risk”. Is it?

No: it's on the payments roadmap; escalate it to its owner and track it as a dependency.
