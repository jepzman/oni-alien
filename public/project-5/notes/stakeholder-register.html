Who Cares About This?

TL;DR

A stakeholder is anyone the project affects, or who can affect it: inside the organisation and outside, friendly or not, with a contract or without one. Identify them early, from several sources (the charter, lessons from the last project, a walk round the site, the experts on local rules), and write them in a register: who they are, what they need from the project, how much they can help or stop it, what they think of it now, who talks to them, in which language. The people you forget are the ones who surprise you.

  • Family: who's involved
  • Lives in: one table, reviewed monthly
  • Pairs with: the stakeholder map, power and interest, the communications plan

1The situation Larder, 2 November 2026

Antwerp is Larder's first city abroad, planned for September 2027. Femke is the sponsor; Sanne started the project on 5 October. Her first stakeholder list is a copy of Rotterdam's: fourteen names, twelve of them inside Larder, plus the landlord and the builder. Lena reads it and asks: “Where's the city? Where's the food-safety agency? Where are the neighbours?” Rotterdam didn't contact its neighbours until 2 March, eight weeks before opening. In another country, with other rules, the list Sanne knew would be shorter than the list that mattered.

  • The list names the people Sanne knows, not the people the project touches.
  • Authorities and the street are missing.
  • Nothing says what each one needs or can do.

2Real-life analogy moving house

A family moving to another town makes a list: the family, the removal firm, the estate agent. The list that decides whether moving day goes well is longer: the new school (a place for the children), the town hall (registration, parking permit for the van), the neighbours who share the drive, the old landlord holding the deposit. Nobody on the second list is hostile. They just weren't asked.

3How it solves it four sources, four rings, one register

Sanne builds the register from four sources in a week. The charter (who it names, who signs). Rotterdam's lessons (neighbours told late, store IT forgotten). A walk round the street on Saturday 31 October with Joost: a school on the corner with a 07:30 drop-off, a bakery, a terrace of forty-six houses backing onto the loading yard. And an hour with Lena and Maarten, the Antwerp lawyer Lena has engaged, on who in Belgium has a say. The result is thirty-one stakeholders in four rings: Larder (12), partners and suppliers (8), authorities (5: the city's permit office, the food-safety agency FAVV-AFSCA, the fire service, the VAT office, the city's mobility department) and the community (6). Each row says what they need from the project, what they can do to it, their attitude now, who at Larder talks to them, and in which language.

  1. Start from more than one source

    The charter, past lessons, the site, local experts.

  2. Walk the place

    Who lives, works, parks and drives there?

  3. Include the ones without a contract

    Authorities, neighbours, rivals.

  4. For each: what they need and what they can do

    Help it, slow it, stop it.

  5. Note attitude and a named contact

    And the language they work in.

  6. Review it monthly

    Stakeholders arrive and leave.

Vocabulary for the exam

Stakeholder
Anyone the project affects or who can affect it.
Stakeholder register
The list of stakeholders with their needs, influence, attitude and contact.
Internal stakeholder
Inside the organisation: sponsor, teams, other departments.
External stakeholder
Outside it: suppliers, authorities, customers, neighbours.
Regulator
An authority whose permission the project needs.
Contact owner
The one person who speaks to a stakeholder for the project.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small internal change

  • A list of names and what each needs.
  • Ten minutes.
  • Kept in the plan.

Standard

Antwerp

  • A register from four sources.
  • Needs, power, attitude, contact, language.
  • Reviewed monthly.

Heavy

public or regulated projects

  • Formal stakeholder analysis and mapping.
  • Consultation records for authorities.
  • A register owned by a stakeholder manager.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Copying the last list

    It names the last project's people. Fix → start from four sources.

  • Only people with contracts

    Regulators and neighbours surprise you. Fix → include everyone affected.

  • Names without needs

    A phone book, not a register. Fix → write what each needs and can do.

  • Written once

    Stakeholders change. Fix → review it monthly.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • At the start, before the plan.
  • When the project enters a new place, country or organisation.
  • When a new partner or authority appears.
  • At each phase.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip it; on small work it's a five-line list.

8Self-check quiz

What makes someone a stakeholder?

The project affects them, or they can affect it.

Name Sanne's four sources.

The charter, Rotterdam's lessons, a walk round the street, Lena and Maarten.

Why was the copied list dangerous?

It named the people Sanne knew in a country she knew; Antwerp's authorities and street weren't on it.

In January the courier agency says Belgian couriers must be employed through a specific scheme. What does Sanne do with the register?

Add the scheme's body if it has a say, update the agency's row (needs, power), and name who at Larder talks to them.

A colleague says “neighbours aren't stakeholders, they have no contract.” Answer them.

They're affected by the store and can affect it (objections, complaints); that makes them stakeholders.

try it

Write your project's stakeholders from memory. Then walk round the place it affects, or ask someone who knows the local rules, and add who you missed. How many were on the second list?

remember!!

The movers you booked. The neighbours you forgot.
