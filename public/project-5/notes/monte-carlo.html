What If We Ran It a Thousand Times?

TL;DR

A Monte Carlo simulation runs the plan a thousand times. Each run draws every uncertain duration from its range (best, likely, worst) and flips every risk's coin; the computer works out the end date each time. Sorted, the thousand dates become odds: the date you have a 50 % chance of hitting (P50), the date you'd hit four times in five (P80), and which uncertainty drives the late tail. It turns “will we make 13 September?” into “51 % yes, 80 % by 27 September, and the permit decides the rest”. It needs ranges you trust, and a record of the model and its random seed.

1The situation Larder, 1 March 2027

Femke asks what every sponsor asks: will Antwerp open on Monday 13 September? The plan says yes. Underneath it are ranges: Pieter's fit-out of 9 to 13 weeks, a permit procedure where every step has a best and a worst case, and a 30 % chance that neighbours object in the public inquiry. Adding best cases gives August; adding worst cases gives November; neither is an answer. In Vol. 3 Sanne added three-point estimates for one chain. Antwerp has two chains that meet at the cold room, and a coin in the middle of one of them.

  • A single date hides every range underneath it.
  • Two chains meeting make the end later than either alone.
  • A yes-or-no risk can't be added like a duration.

2Real-life analogy leaving for the airport

A family doesn't ask “how long is the drive to the airport?” They imagine it many times. Most days the motorway is clear; some days there's a jam at the bridge; one day in ten, an accident. Out of a hundred imagined trips, ninety arrive within seventy minutes. They leave seventy-five minutes before check-in closes, and know they're taking a one-in-ten chance of a sprint through the terminal.

3How it solves it a thousand runs, a histogram, P50, P80, and what drives the tail

Sanne writes the model on one page (see “going deeper”) and a twenty-line script with Eli: two chains, every duration a triangular range (best, most likely, worst, from Pieter, Hilde and Maarten), the objection as a 30 % coin, and the date the cold room can start as the later of the two chains. She runs it 1,000 times with a recorded seed (2027), so anyone can re-run it and get the same chart. The answer for the steering group of Friday 5 March: 13 September is a coin toss: 51 % of runs open by then (P50). 80 % open by 27 September (P80), 90 % by 11 October. And the shape matters most: of the 162 runs later than 27 September, the permit set the date in 157; the fit-out drives the on-time runs and almost none of the late ones. Femke's two decisions follow from that. Keep 13 September as the planning date, and tell investors “September or early October”. And don't pay for earlier keys: a month's head start on the fit-out barely moves the late tail. The money goes to the permit's responses instead.

  1. Draw the model as chains of activities

    And where they meet.

  2. Give each uncertain step a range

    Best, most likely, worst, from people who know.

  3. Add yes-or-no risks as coins

    With their probability and effect.

  4. Run it a thousand times; record the seed

    So it can be repeated.

  5. Read P50, P80 and the shape

    Not just the average.

  6. Find what drives the late runs

    And spend responses there.

Vocabulary for the exam

Monte Carlo simulation
Running a model many times with random draws from each range to see the spread of results.
Triangular distribution
A range given by best, most likely and worst values.
P50 / P80
The date (or cost) reached in 50 % / 80 % of runs.
Histogram
A bar chart of how many runs ended in each week.
Sensitivity (driver) analysis
Which uncertainty most changes the result; drawn as a tornado diagram.
Seed
The starting number of the random draws; the same seed gives the same runs.

＋Going deeper what a simulation does, where the ranges come from, reading the output, and its limits

“Monte Carlo” sounds like casinos and statistics. The idea is simpler than a critical path: imagine the project many times, each time with different luck, and count. This chapter shows the model Sanne ran, where good ranges come from, how to read what comes out, and what a simulation can't tell you, including whether 18 October was in the chart.

1What it does, in plain words

Each run is one possible version of the project. For every uncertain step the computer picks a duration from its range: usually near the most likely value, sometimes near the best or worst. For every yes-or-no risk it flips a weighted coin. Then it works out the end date the way you would on a network diagram: chains add up, and where two chains meet, the later one wins.

Do that a thousand times and you have a thousand end dates. Sort them. The 500th is the P50; the 800th is the P80. Nothing in it is cleverer than the network diagram from Vol. 3; it's the network diagram, rolled a thousand times.

2Where the ranges come from

A simulation is only as good as its ranges, and good ranges come from people who've done the work in this place: Pieter for the fit-out, Hilde and Maarten for the permit. Ask for three numbers, as in Vol. 3's three-point estimates: the best case, the most likely, and a worst case they've actually seen. People's worst cases are usually too kind; asking “what's the longest you've seen this take?” helps.

Two cautions. Where two steps go wrong for the same reason (a slow city office slows both the completeness check and the decision), a simple model treats them as independent and the tail comes out too thin. And a coin like “objections, 30 %” is a judgment; write down whose.

3Reading the output

Three things matter, in this order:

  • The odds of the date you've promised. 13 September came out at 51 %. A planning date with even odds is fine if everyone knows it's even.
  • P80 and P90: the dates you'd choose if a slip is expensive. 27 September and 11 October.
  • The shape and its driver. Antwerp's histogram has a long right tail, and the late runs were nearly all the permit's. That is the most useful output: it says where a euro of response buys the most.

Re-run monthly with what has actually happened. On 9 April, with the drawings in and the application booked for 19 April, the P50 moved to 20 September and the P80 to 11 October. With the objections in, in July, the model gave P50 11 October and P80 25 October, before the city's case officer said the same thing in words.

4Limits, and was 18 October in the chart?

A simulation can only shake the uncertainties you put in. It knows nothing about risks you haven't named (that's what the management reserve is for), and it believes your ranges. It's also not a promise: an 80 % date is missed one time in five.

Antwerp opened on 18 October. In the chart of 1 March, 96 % of runs had opened by then; 18 October was in the far tail, about one run in twenty-five. Everything that happened was inside the model's ranges: the drawings took seven weeks (range 3–8), the objections came (the 30 % coin), the decision took its time. Nothing in the model was wrong. What went wrong was that the draws all came out bad, and a response that would have narrowed the drawings' range had no owner (two notes on).

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • Best, likely, worst for the whole.
  • Say a range, not a date.
  • No simulation.

Standard

Antwerp

  • A short model, ranges from experts, 1,000 runs, a seed.
  • P50, P80 and the driver.
  • Re-run monthly.

Heavy

large programmes

  • Integrated cost and schedule risk analysis.
  • Correlated ranges, risk drivers.
  • Specialist tools and reviewers.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Ranges from the wrong people

    A confident, wrong chart. Fix → ask those who've done it here.

  • Reading only the average

    Misses the tail. Fix → read P80, P90 and the shape.

  • No seed, no record

    Nobody can check it. Fix → record the model and the seed.

  • Treating P80 as certain

    One time in five it's missed. Fix → say the odds aloud.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When the date or cost matters and many things are uncertain.
  • When chains meet or yes-or-no risks sit on the path.
  • When choosing where to spend on responses.
  • When a sponsor needs a date with odds.

Skip it when…

  • Small work, or a single chain where a three-point estimate is enough.

8Self-check quiz

What is a P80 date?

The date reached in 80 % of the simulated runs.

What were Antwerp's P50 and P80 on 1 March?

13 September (51 % by then) and 27 September.

Why didn't Femke pay for earlier keys?

The late runs were driven by the permit, not the fit-out; earlier keys hardly moved the tail.

A lead asks why the chart changed when you re-ran it. What's your answer?

New facts went in (actual dates, a changed range); with the same inputs and seed it would be identical.

Femke asks whether 18 October was “in the model”. What do you say?

Yes, in the far tail: about 4 % of runs opened later than that; every step's range held it.

try it

Take a chain from your plan with three-point estimates. In a spreadsheet, draw each from its range 1,000 times, add them up, and sort. Compare the P80 with the date you've promised.
