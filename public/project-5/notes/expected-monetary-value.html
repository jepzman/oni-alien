a.k.a. “contingency vs management reserve”: expected monetary value and sizing the reserves

What Is It Worth?

TL;DR

Expected monetary value (EMV) is probability × impact in money, for each risk: a 30 % chance of a €40k cost is worth €12k today. Add the threats, subtract the opportunities, and you have a defensible size for the contingency reserve: money for known risks, managed by the project manager under a rule. Above it the sponsor holds a management reserve for the unknown ones, outside the baseline. EMV doesn't predict what will happen (each risk either happens or doesn't); it sizes the pot for the whole list.

  • Family: what could happen
  • Lives in: a column of the register and two lines of the budget
  • Pairs with: the budget and its reserves, probability and impact, simulation

1The situation Larder, February 2027

Rotterdam's reserve was a list of five risks at about €10k, doubled by Marco to €20k. It worked: three draws took it to €0.4k. For Antwerp, Marco asks for more. “Give me a number I can defend to the board, and tell me how much Femke should hold back for the things nobody can name.” The base estimate is €430k: builder €160k, cold room and fridges €84k, equipment and store IT €52k, people €68k, marketing in two languages €46k, Belgian set-up €20k.

  • A doubled list can't be explained line by line.
  • Known and unknown risks were in one pot.
  • R-07 had just jumped to the top; the reserve had to reflect it.

2Real-life analogy the insurer's sum

An insurer pricing house insurance doesn't guess. A burst pipe: 5 % a year, €8,000 to fix, so €400. A fire: one in a thousand, €300,000, so €300. The premium is the sum of those, plus a margin for what the tables don't know. No single house costs the premium; across the whole book, it balances.

3How it solves it EMV per risk, net of opportunities; two reserves, two owners

On Monday 1 March, after the responses of 22 February, Sanne puts a money impact and a probability on every risk that could cost money. The biggest is R-07: if the permit is late, a month of held staff, re-booked marketing, a re-booked cold-room installer and some crash work cost about €40k inside the project; after the responses its probability is 30 %, so €12k. Seven threats add up to €23.1k; two opportunities (the shelving design, the landlord's insulation) take off €4k: net €19.1k. The contingency is set at €20k, held by Sanne and drawn only through a logged change, as in Rotterdam. Above it, Femke holds a management reserve of €15k for what nobody has named: a first country is new to everyone. The budget becomes €430k + €20k = €450k, and €465k with the management reserve. Marco takes it to the board with one line per risk. He adds one note of his own: the €40k is only what lands in the project; the five weeks of a slip would cost far more outside it.

  1. List the risks that cost money

    Threats and opportunities.

  2. Give each an impact in money

    What it would cost if it happened.

  3. Use the probability after responses

    The residual risk.

  4. Multiply and add; subtract opportunities

    Net EMV.

  5. Size the contingency from it

    Owned by the project manager, by a rule.

  6. Add a management reserve for the unknown

    Owned by the sponsor.

Vocabulary for the exam

Expected monetary value (EMV)
Probability × impact in money; negative for threats, positive for opportunities, by convention.
Contingency reserve
Money for known risks, inside the project, under the project manager.
Management reserve
Money for unknown risks, outside the baseline, under the sponsor.
Known unknowns
Risks you've identified but can't be sure will happen.
Unknown unknowns
Risks nobody has identified.
Decision tree
A drawing of choices and chances, with EMV on each branch, to compare options.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • A percentage on top.
  • Say what it's for.
  • No EMV.

Standard

Antwerp

  • EMV per risk, net of opportunities.
  • Contingency from it; management reserve for the unknown.
  • Two owners, two rules.

Heavy

large capital projects

  • Contingency per risk with a confidence level.
  • Monte Carlo on cost.
  • Reserves released by stage.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • EMV as a forecast

    Each risk happens or doesn't. Fix → use it to size the pot, not to predict one risk.

  • One pot for everything

    Unknowns eat the known risks' money. Fix → contingency and management reserve, two owners.

  • Before-response probabilities

    Pays twice for one risk. Fix → use residual probability.

  • Only the project's money

    The real cost of a slip is hidden. Fix → note the cost outside it too.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When sizing the budget's reserves.
  • When comparing responses or options.
  • When the sponsor asks “how much could this cost us?”
  • After a big re-score.

Skip it when…

  • Small work with no risk large enough to price.

8Self-check quiz

What's the EMV of a 30 % chance of a €40k cost?

€12k.

Contingency or management reserve: a risk nobody had thought of?

Management reserve, the sponsor's.

Why subtract opportunities?

They're expected savings; net EMV sizes the pot for the whole list.

A lead wants to use the contingency for an unplanned launch party. What does Sanne say?

The contingency is for named risks, drawn by change; a party is a new wish, for the steering group or the operations budget.

Femke asks: if R-07 happens, will €20k cover it? Answer honestly.

No, R-07 alone would cost ~€40k; the contingency covers the expected cost of the list, and the management reserve is there for the rest.

try it

Put a money impact and a probability on your top five risks. Multiply and add. Is your reserve that size? Who holds the money for what nobody has named?

remember!!

Chance × cost, added up. One pot for the known, one for the rest.
