How Do We Win the Room?

TL;DR

The orals are where the panel decides whether it believes the bid. Bring the people who'll do the work, not the people who sell it. Show your thinking on the client's own data, not on generic slides. Answer the hardest question before it's asked. Route every question to the person who'd do that work, and rehearse with someone playing the toughest panel member. Ninety minutes, planned to the minute.

  • Family: up against the others
  • Lives in: bid/lodestar/orals/ (deck, run sheet, Q bank)
  • Pairs with: assumptions, big consultancy, references

1The situation The Grid, room 27B, 21 April 2027

On 7 April three bidders are shortlisted: Wrenfield, Vexmoor and Halvorsen. Orals are on Wednesday 21 April, 90 minutes each, in room 27B on floor 27. The panel: Elaine (chair), Priya, Hollis, Nkem from finance, Kofi Asante from metering, and Desmond, who runs the process but doesn't score.

  • MetroVolt sent each shortlisted bidder a 10,000-record sample on 1 April, as promised in clarification Q-05.
  • Bilal's profile of it: 7.8 % of records fail the address check. The bid assumed 5 % (A1-07).
  • Vivian's first plan: she presents for 40 minutes, “the techies” take questions. The panel has already met forty presenters who never came back after signing.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt the panel of six

Each panel member has a different fear. Elaine: another Orion. Hollis: an architecture that's pretty and wrong. Nkem: a price that grows. Kofi: metering forgotten again. Priya: choosing the small firm and being blamed. They'll score on what they see the team do in the room.

“Are these the people who'll actually be here at 2 a.m.?”

Wrenfield four from Wrenfield

Wrenfield has 90 minutes to be believed. Its best evidence is the people (Ines, Hana, Bilal, Callum, named in the bid) and the sample: the only bidder that will show MetroVolt its own data, including the bad news that 7.8 % isn't 5 %.

“Let them watch us think about their problem.”

An oral presentation is a short job interview for a team. The panel is imagining working with you.

3Real-life analogy the chef's trial

A restaurant hiring a head chef doesn't watch slides about cooking. It asks for a trial: cook a dish from our fridge, in our kitchen, while we watch. The owners learn more from how she handles a missing ingredient than from her CV. A good candidate tells them about the missing ingredient before they notice.

4How it works their data, the hard question first, and the doers answer

The run sheet. Ten minutes on who: Vivian opens for two of them and says the re-anchored sentence herself; then each presenter says what they'll personally do on Lodestar. Twenty minutes on their data: Bilal shows the sample profile live, including the 7.8 %, and what A1-07 means at that rate (about £26k on wave 1 for the extra 2.8 %, paid only if it holds). Fifteen minutes on the hardest question, asked by Ines before anyone else can: “Why won't this be another programme that never goes live?” Answer: waves, a pilot gate in month 9, paid discovery, and the people in this room named in the contract. Then 45 minutes of questions.

Routing. Every question goes to the person who'd do the work: architecture to Hana, data to Bilal, plan and money to Callum, everything else to Ines. Nobody answers outside their area; “Hana will take that” is the most convincing sentence in the room.

Rehearsal. Three run-throughs, timed. In the last one, Graham plays Hollis and is unpleasant. At minute 52 on the day, Lord Varnish walks in and asks about “synergy with Orion's hub”. Ines answers in one sentence (“where the hub's data model fits MetroVolt's data, discovery will tell us, and we'll reuse it”) and hands back to Elaine. Hollis writes something down. It turns out to be a 4.

  1. Bring the doers

    The named team presents; sales opens and closes.

  2. Use their data

    One real piece of the client's world, worked through live.

  3. Ask the hardest question yourself

    In the first half, with your answer.

  4. Route every question

    To the person who'd do that work; nobody else answers.

  5. Rehearse three times

    Timed; once with someone playing the toughest panellist.

  6. Plan for the visitor

    A one-sentence answer for the senior person who drops in.

Vocabulary

Orals
A presentation and Q&A with the evaluation panel, usually scored.
Run sheet
The minute-by-minute plan for the presentation.
Question bank
Likely questions, each with an owner and a short answer.
Murder board
A hostile rehearsal panel that asks the worst questions.
Moderation
Panel members agreeing a score together after scoring alone.
Clarification at orals
Anything said can be written into the contract; say only what you'd sign.

5What the contract says what's said in the room counts

Lodestar ITT · §7 presentations3 Feb 2027
on the record

7.2 The presenting team must include the Key Personnel proposed in the tender response.

so no “A team for the orals, B team for the work”.

7.4 Statements made at the presentation may be recorded and incorporated into the contract as clarifications of the tender response.

Bilal's 7.8 % is now on the record. good: it was true.

In plain words: anything you say at the orals can end up in the contract. That's why the people who'll do the work should answer: they won't promise what they can't deliver.

6Account health the same move, green, amber and red

Green

they know you already

  • Shorter presentation, more conversation.
  • Show what's new since last time.
  • Still the doers answer.

Amber Lodestar now

they've never met you

  • Their data, live.
  • The hard question first.
  • Named people, routed answers.

Red

the panel has a favourite

  • Make it hard to score you low: evidence, not charm.
  • Answer the favourite's strengths specifically.
  • Leave them a document worth keeping.

7Pressure gauge how hard this is on the team

5 of 5 · ninety minutes

three rehearsals · two nights of bad sleep · one surprise visitor

  • Everyone is nervous. Give each person one part they own completely; nerves shrink when the job is small and clear.
  • After the last rehearsal, stop changing slides. Sleep is worth more than slide 14.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • The sales team presents

    The panel never meets the people who'd do the work. Fix → the named team presents.

  • Generic slides

    The same deck as for every client. Fix → one real piece of their world, live.

  • Hiding bad news

    The 7.8 % comes out in due diligence, later and worse. Fix → show it, priced, before they find it.

  • Everyone answers everything

    Contradictions in front of the panel. Fix → route every question to one owner.

  • Arguing with the senior visitor

    Winning the point, losing the room. Fix → one sentence, then back to the chair.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any shortlisted bid with a presentation.
  • Pitches, design reviews and steering committees, too.
  • When the panel has been burned before.

Skip it when…

  • Written-only evaluations: put the same thinking in the document.

11Self-check quiz

Who should present at the orals?

The named people who'll do the work; sales opens and closes.

Why profile the client's own sample?

It shows the team thinking about their real problem, and it surfaces bad news honestly.

Why ask the hardest question yourself?

You choose the timing and the answer, and it shows you're not hiding from it.

What is routing?

Sending each question to the one person who'd do that work.

Why be careful what you say at the orals?

Statements can be recorded and incorporated into the contract.

what would you do?Hollis asks Callum a detailed question about the change-detection design. Callum starts to answer. What do you do?

Gently route it: “Hana designed that part; Hana?” The panel trusts an answer from the owner, and Callum avoids promising something he can't defend.

try it

Plan your next important presentation as a run sheet: who speaks, for how long, about what. Add one real piece of the audience's own world, and the hardest question they could ask, answered by you in the first half.

remember!!

Bring the doers, use their data, and ask the hard question before they do.
