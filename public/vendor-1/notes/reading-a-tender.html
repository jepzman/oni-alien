How Do I Read a Tender?

TL;DR

A tender tells you three things, and only one of them on purpose. Read it three times: once for how it will be scored (weights, pass / fail lines, what the evaluators must write down), once for the work hidden in every “will” and “shall”, and once for the history nobody wrote down (who drafted it, what failed before, why the timetable is so short). Put the answers on one sheet before anyone writes a word of the bid.

1The situation Wrenfield and MetroVolt, February 2027

On Wednesday 3 February 2027 at 15:12, MetroVolt publishes the tender for Lodestar: 96 pages, 212 requirements, bids due on 24 March at noon. Vivian, Wrenfield's account director, forwards it to eleven people with the subject line “THIS IS OURS!!!”. By 16:00 two engineers have started sketching an architecture.

  • Thursday 4 February, 07:40, the Leeds train. Ines reads it properly for the first time, with three highlighters.
  • Page 71 says bids are scored 60 % quality, 40 % price, and that 14 requirements are pass / fail: miss one and the bid is out.
  • Section 4 calls the customer data a “Golden Customer Record”. That isn't MetroVolt's phrase. Ines has heard it before, on a Vexmoor slide.
  • The project being replaced isn't mentioned once. Its name was Orion. It was cancelled last month.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Desmond writes it

Procurement needs a tender that is fair, defensible and scoreable: the same questions for every bidder, weights fixed in advance, nothing a losing bidder could challenge. Desmond assembled it from the business case, Hollis's architecture notes and, under deadline, Orion's old requirements.

“If it's in writing and scored the same for everyone, nobody can sue us.”

Wrenfield Ines reads it

The tender is the only map Wrenfield has. Ines needs to know how to score, what the work really is and whether the game is already decided. The document answers the first question openly, the second in small print, and the third only by accident.

“Who wrote section 4, and why does it sound like Vexmoor?”

Neither side is hiding anything on purpose. A tender is written by tired people under rules, and read by hopeful people under deadline. The reading sheet is how you stay calm.

3Real-life analogy the job advert, read three times

Reading a job advert for a role you want, you highlight three things in three colours. How you'll be judged: interview 60 %, test 40 %. What the job really is: “will own all systems” means you're on call. And the history: “immediate start” and a re-advertised post mean the last person left in a hurry, and you want to know why before you sign.

4How it works three reads, three colours, one sheet

Read one: how it's scored (yellow). Find the evaluation section first. Lodestar's says quality 60 % (solution 20, delivery approach 15, legacy and data 10, team 10, social value 5) and price 40 %. Evaluators must write down a reason for every score, so give them sentences they can copy. The 14 pass / fail lines go to the top of the sheet: they're the only part that can end the bid in an afternoon.

Read two: the work (blue). Count every “will” and “shall”: each one is a promise you'd be signing. Most are routine. Three carry most of the risk: “migrate all customer data from MAVIS” (that sentence is the project), “integrate with the existing billing platform” (Halvorsen, another company) and “provide support for five years” (a cost nobody priced).

Read three: the history (pink). What's missing? Orion. Who drafted section 4? Someone who used Vexmoor's words. Why six weeks for a £4–6m programme? Either it's urgent, or someone has already had a head start.

  1. Don't start writing

    Nobody drafts a word until the reading sheet is done. Two days, not two hours.

  2. Read for the scoring

    Weights, sub-weights, pass / fail lines, page limits, what evaluators must justify.

  3. Count the promises

    Every “will” and “shall” into a spreadsheet; mark the three that carry the risk.

  4. Read for the history

    What failed before, who wrote which section, why this timetable.

  5. List what you don't know

    These become clarification questions and assumptions.

  6. One sheet, shared

    The whole bid team reads the same sheet on day three.

Vocabulary

ITT / RFP
Invitation to tender / request for proposal: the client's formal request for bids.
Evaluation criteria
How bids are scored, with weights fixed before bids arrive.
Pass / fail requirement
A must-have that excludes the bid if not met, whatever the score.
Compliance matrix
A table answering every requirement: comply, partly, no, with a reference.
Clarification period
The window for written questions; answers go to every bidder.
Incumbent
The supplier already doing related work for the client.

5What the contract says the tender's own rules (instructions to bidders)

Lodestar ITT · instructions to bidderspublished 3 Feb 2027
pass / fail

6.3 Bids will be evaluated on quality (60 %) and price (40 %) using the criteria in Annex B. A score of 0 against any requirement marked pass / fail will exclude the bid from further evaluation.

fourteen of them. read those first. then read them again.

6.7 Bidders may not contact MetroVolt staff about this procurement except through the clarification process in section 5.

so: no calls to Priya. Vivian, please.

9.1 MetroVolt is not obliged to accept the lowest or any bid.

In plain words: the instructions to bidders are a contract about the contest itself. Break 6.7 and you can be excluded however good your bid is. The pass / fail lines matter more than any brilliant paragraph.

6Account health the same move, green, amber and red

Green

you've worked with them before

  • You already know who wrote which section.
  • Read for changes since last time.
  • Ask your contacts about history, through the proper channel.

Amber Lodestar now

new client, open contest

  • Read three times before writing.
  • Treat the history as unknown and say so.
  • Turn every gap into a question or an assumption.

Red

it reads like it was written for someone else

  • Read for whether you can win at all.
  • Count requirements only one supplier can meet.
  • Go to bid / no-bid early; don't fall in love.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Writing on day one

    The architecture is sketched before anyone knows how it's scored. Fix → two days of reading, then a kick-off with the sheet.

  • Answering what you'd like to build

    A brilliant section that scores 5 % and a thin one that scores 20 %. Fix → write to the weights.

  • Skipping the pass / fail lines

    A missing certificate excludes a great bid. Fix → put them at the top of the sheet and assign an owner each.

  • Ignoring the history

    You find out about Orion at the orals, from the panel. Fix → ask what came before, in public, through the clarification process.

  • Breaking the contact rules

    A friendly call to the sponsor gets the bid excluded. Fix → every question goes through the procurement channel.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any formal tender, RFP or framework call-off.
  • Before the bid kick-off.
  • Before deciding whether to bid at all.
  • When a tender lands with an exciting subject line.

Skip it when…

  • A two-page quote request for a small piece of work: one quick read and a phone call will do.

11Self-check quiz

What are the three reads of a tender?

How it's scored, the work hidden in each “will” and “shall”, and the history nobody wrote down.

Why read the evaluation section first?

It tells you what earns points and what excludes you, so you write to the weights.

What is a pass / fail requirement?

A must-have that excludes the bid if it isn't met, however well you score elsewhere.

Which three Lodestar sentences carried most of the risk?

Migrate all customer data, integrate with the existing billing system, and support for five years.

Why does the history matter if it isn't in the document?

It explains the timetable, the wording and the client's fears; the panel will be thinking about it.

what would you do?A colleague calls the sponsor to “just ask what they really want” during the tender. What do you do?

Stop it, and put the question through the clarification process instead. Under the rules, contact outside the process can get the bid excluded, and the answer has to be shared with every bidder anyway.

try it

Take a tender, an RFP or even a detailed job advert. Read it three times with three colours: how it's scored, the promises in every “will”, and the history behind it. Fill one sheet. Which three sentences carry the most risk?
