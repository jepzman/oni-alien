What Are We Assuming?

TL;DR

A fixed price is only fixed for the world you priced. Write that world down as three lists: assumptions (what we believe is true), dependencies (what the client must do, by when) and exclusions (what we aren't doing). Each line needs a number, an owner, a test date and what happens if it's false. Then get the lists into the contract. A risk you don't write down is a risk you've agreed to carry, at a fixed price.

1The situation Wrenfield and MetroVolt, March 2027

The Lodestar bid is due on 24 March: £4.8m fixed price to move MetroVolt's first 380,000 customer accounts off MAVIS, a customer system written in 1997. Vivian's draft of the bid has an assumptions page. It has three lines.

  • Wed 10 March, 18:40, Leeds. Vivian's draft: “1. MetroVolt will cooperate. 2. Access will be provided. 3. Data will be of reasonable quality.” Price: £4.8m.
  • Thu 11 March, 10:00, The Grid, floor 19. The bidders' meeting. Desmond Rook (procurement) takes questions in writing only. Ines asks how many customer records fail the address check. The written answer: “MetroVolt's data is of good quality.” No number.
  • 10:52. Elaine Corbett, the programme director, says it out loud: “Orion's vendor had four assumptions. We paid for every one of them.”
  • 12:15, the lift. A young analyst (Pip, his badge says) sees the Wrenfield lanyard and says quietly: “If you're pricing the data, ask for a sample. Nobody's looked since 2014.”
  • Thursday night. Ines and Hana write down everything that would ruin the price if it turned out wrong. By 23:00 the list has 41 lines.

Wrenfield Ines writes it

Each line is a risk Wrenfield can price, share or hand back. Anything not written down belongs to Wrenfield by default, at a fixed price, for an 80-person firm. Lodestar would be a third of next year's revenue.

“If they think 5 % is unfair, I want to hear it now, not in December.”

They are both right. The client fears exits; the vendor fears an open cheque. A good list answers both: few enough to read, specific enough to test, and fair enough that the client could have written it.

3Real-life analogy the builder's quote that says “assumes no asbestos”

A builder quotes £18,400 to knock through a wall in a 1970s house. The quote says: assumes no asbestos; excludes rewiring; if asbestos is found, removal is priced separately. That isn't the builder being difficult. Nobody can see inside the wall until it's open. The quote names what's in the wall, who pays if it's there, and when you'll both find out.

4How it works three lists, four boxes per line

Split everything you're unsure of into three lists:

  • Assumptions: things you believe are true and have priced on. “No more than 5 % of records fail address validation.”
  • Dependencies: things someone else must do, by a date. “Halvorsen provides the billing interface spec by 30 July 2027.”
  • Exclusions: work you're not doing, named so nobody assumes you are. “Training beyond 40 super-users.”

Then every line must fill four boxes: a number (not “reasonable”), an owner, a test (how and when you'll find out) and a consequence (what happens to the price or the date if it's false). A line that can't fill all four is a worry, not an assumption. Move it to the risk log.

  1. Read the tender for every “will”

    “The supplier will migrate all customer data.” Each one hides an assumption about what that data is like.

  2. Ask the team what would ruin the price

    Twenty minutes, everyone, sticky notes. Engineers know where the asbestos is.

  3. Fill the four boxes

    Number, owner, test, consequence. If a line can't, it goes to the risk log.

  4. Decide for each: carry, share or hand back

    Carry it (add contingency), share it (a rate per unit above the line) or hand it back (exclude it, openly).

  5. Put the top eight in the bid itself

    The rest go in an appendix, grouped. Forty-one lines on page 3 read like fear.

  6. Get them into the contract

    A clause that says what happens when one is false (SOW §3, below).

  7. Test the dangerous ones first

    Ask for a data sample before you sign, if you can. Book the rest into discovery with dates.

Vocabulary

Assumption
Something believed true and priced on. If it's false, the price or date changes.
Dependency
Something another party must deliver, by a date, for you to deliver.
Exclusion
Work you are explicitly not doing.
Contingency
Money or time in the price for risks you carry yourself.
Relief
The contract lets you move a date when the client's dependency is late (usually day for day).
Change request (CR)
The agreed way to change scope, price or date once the contract is signed.

5What the contract says SOW §3, after MetroVolt's legal team had a go at it

Lodestar SOW · draft v0.3 · redlined9 June 2027
accepted subject to

3.2 The Charges are based on the Assumptions in Appendix C. If an Assumption proves materially incorrect, the Supplier shall absorb any resulting cost either Party may raise a Change Request under clause 7.

their legal tried “absorb”. three calls to get it out.

3.4 MetroVolt shall provide the Dependencies in Appendix C by the dates shown. Where a Dependency is late, the Supplier is entitled to relief day for day on affected Milestones.

3.5 Exclusions are outside the Services and may be added only by Change Request.

In plain words: an assumption only protects you if the contract says what happens when it's wrong. A list in the bid that never reaches the contract is a wish. Read the redlines for the word absorb.

6Account health the same move, green, amber and red

Green

trust is high

  • The list is a shared map of risks.
  • Walk it together at kick-off; review it monthly.
  • False assumptions are fixed with a short CR, without drama.

Amber Lodestar now

they've been burned before

  • Fewer, sharper lines in the main bid.
  • Offer to test the biggest one early, before signing.
  • Price what you can (a rate per 1 %), so it doesn't read as an exit.

Red

it's a war

  • The list is evidence. Quote it by ID and clause number.
  • Confirm every dependency date in writing.
  • Raise CRs promptly; late claims look like excuses.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Escape hatches

    “The client will cooperate.” It can't be tested, so it reads as a trap and protects nothing. Fix → a number, an owner and a date, or delete it.

  • Two hundred lines

    Nobody reads them, and the client's lawyer strikes the lot. Fix → eight in the bid, the rest grouped in an appendix.

  • Lost on the way to the contract

    The bid had the list; the SOW doesn't. Fix → check §3 and Appendix C line by line before signing.

  • Never tested

    You find out in month nine, when it's an argument. Fix → a test date on every line, the dangerous ones before signing.

  • Excluding the obvious

    Excluding data migration from a migration bid. You lose the bid, or the client's trust. Fix → price it with a rate instead.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • The price is fixed, or capped.
  • You can't see inside the legacy system before you bid.
  • Another vendor or the client's own IT must deliver something you need.
  • The client has been burned before, and the next failure will need someone to blame.

Skip it when…

  • It's time and materials: the client already carries the risk. Keep the dependencies and drop most of the rest.

11Self-check quiz

What's the difference between an assumption and a dependency?

An assumption is something you believe is true and priced on. A dependency is something someone else must do, by a date.

What are the four boxes every line must fill?

A number, an owner, a test (how and when you'll find out) and a consequence if it's false.

Why is “the client will cooperate” a bad assumption?

It can't be measured or tested, so it protects nobody and reads like an escape hatch.

Why did the word “absorb” matter in clause 3.2?

It would have made Wrenfield pay for every false assumption, turning the list into decoration.

What are the three things you can do with a risk on a fixed-price bid?

Carry it (contingency), share it (a rate above a threshold) or hand it back (exclude it openly).

what would you do?Vivian says 41 assumptions will lose the bid and wants three. Elaine has said Orion had four. What do you propose?

Keep all 41, but show eight in the bid, each priced or tested, and group the rest in an appendix. Offer to test A1-07 on a sample before signing. Show Vivian what each dropped line would cost Wrenfield if it's false.

try it

Take a quote or estimate you've given recently. Write down five things that would ruin it if they turned out wrong. For each, fill the four boxes: number, owner, test, consequence. How many were in the quote?
