Fixed Price, or Time and Materials?

TL;DR

The commercial model decides who carries the uncertainty. Time and materials puts it on the client; fixed price puts it on you; capped and outcome-based split it in different ways. Match the model to what you know: pay for the fog by the day (a capped, paid discovery), then a fixed price once you can see, paid on milestones the client can check, with a rate card for whatever changes.

  • Family: shaping the answer
  • Lives in: bid section 6 (commercial) + the pricing model

1The situation Wrenfield, 18 March 2027

The tender asks for a fixed price for phase 1. After Orion, MetroVolt wants the risk on someone else's balance sheet. Wrenfield's whole revenue is about £9m a year, and nobody has seen inside MAVIS.

  • A pure fixed price for discovery and wave 1 means pricing the fog. Either the price is huge (and loses) or the risk is huge (and might sink Wrenfield).
  • Pure time and materials would be honest, and MetroVolt has already said no: “we did that with Orion”.
  • Clarification Q-09 came back on 3 March: discovery may be priced and contracted separately. That's the gap.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Nkem and Desmond

Finance wants a number for the board that won't move. Procurement wants prices that can be compared across five bidders. Both remember Orion's open-ended invoices. A fixed price feels like safety, even if it buys a bidder's contingency.

“If it's fixed, it's not our problem when it slips.”

Wrenfield Callum and Ines

Wrenfield needs a model where it's paid for what it learns before it promises, and where the fixed part is fixed for a known world. It also needs cash: a firm of 80 can't wait twelve months for the first payment.

“We'll fix the price when we can see what we're pricing.”

A fixed price isn't free safety for the client: it's insurance, and the premium is the vendor's contingency. A paid discovery lowers the premium for both.

3Real-life analogy the kitchen fitter's two prices

A kitchen fitter charges a day rate to survey and design, then gives a fixed price to fit the design you both signed, paid in stages: units in, worktops on, appliances working. If you change your mind about the island, there's a price list for that. Nobody fixes a price for a kitchen they haven't measured.

4How it works match the model to what you know

Callum puts five models on a page and asks one question of each: who carries the fog? Time and materials: MetroVolt, all of it (refused). Fixed price for everything: Wrenfield, all of it (too dangerous). Capped T&M: shared, but MetroVolt hears “Orion”. Outcome-based (paid per account migrated): tempting, but data quality isn't in Wrenfield's control. Discovery, then fixed: each carries the fog it can see.

The bid offers: a 14-week discovery, time and materials, capped at £380k; then £4.8m fixed for wave 1, paid on five milestones the client can check (design approved 15 %, pilot live 25 %, wave 1 live 35 %, hypercare ended 15 %, 10 % retained until the warranty ends). A rate card covers change. The fixed price is “subject to the Assumptions in Appendix C”.

The margin is checked at both ends of the estimate: about 29 % at P50, 19 % at P80. Graham's floor is 15 %. If the P80 margin had been below it, the answer would have been a higher price or a smaller scope, not hope.

  1. Ask “who carries the fog?” of each model

    Write the answer for each, honestly.

  2. Separate learning from building

    A capped, paid discovery before the fixed price.

  3. Fix the price for a written world

    “Subject to the assumptions in Appendix C”.

  4. Pay on things the client can see

    Working software, not documents or months.

  5. Add a rate card

    So every change has a price before anyone argues.

  6. Check the margin at P50 and P80

    If P80 is below your floor, change the deal.

Vocabulary

Time and materials (T&M)
The client pays for the time spent; the client carries the risk.
Fixed price
One price for a defined scope; the supplier carries the risk.
Capped T&M
Paid by time, up to a maximum.
Outcome-based pricing
Paid per result (e.g. per account migrated).
Milestone payment
A payment released when a defined, checkable result is accepted.
Retention
A part of the price held back until the warranty ends.

5What the contract says charges and payment, as it will be signed

Lodestar SOW · §9 charges (draft)from the bid
fixed, for a written world

9.1 Discovery Services shall be charged on a time and materials basis at the Rates in Schedule 5, up to a maximum of £380,000.

the cap protects them. the T&M protects us.

9.2 Wave 1 Services shall be charged as a fixed price of £4,800,000, subject to clause 3 (Assumptions), payable on acceptance of the Milestones in Schedule 4.

“subject to clause 3”. the most valuable four words in the bid.

9.5 Changes shall be priced using the Rates in Schedule 5.

In plain words: the commercial model is written in the charges clause. Check that the fixed price is tied to the assumptions, the milestones to acceptance, and changes to a rate card agreed now, while everyone is friendly.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Fixing the price of fog

    A fixed price for a system nobody has seen. Fix → paid discovery first.

  • Milestones on documents

    Paid for a design document; the client can't see progress. Fix → pay on working results.

  • No rate card

    Every change becomes a negotiation. Fix → agree rates now, while friendly.

  • Margin checked only at P50

    Bad luck takes the whole margin. Fix → check at P80 against your floor.

  • Outcome pricing on things you don't control

    Paid per account, while the client owns the data quality. Fix → only pay-per-result where you control the result.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any fixed-price bid.
  • Legacy work with hidden complexity.
  • A client who has been burned by open-ended T&M.

Skip it when…

  • A small, well-known piece of work: a simple fixed price is fine.

11Self-check quiz

Who carries the risk under time and materials? Under fixed price?

The client under T&M; the supplier under fixed price.

Why a paid discovery before the fixed price?

So nobody fixes a price for a system they haven't seen; the fog is paid for by the day.

Why pay on milestones the client can see?

Both sides can tell progress from activity, and payment follows real results.

Why was outcome-based pricing rejected?

Payment per account migrated depends on data quality, which Wrenfield doesn't control.

What does “subject to clause 3” do in §9.2?

It ties the fixed price to the written assumptions, so a false one can change the price.

what would you do?Desmond asks if Wrenfield would take discovery as fixed price too, for “simplicity”. What do you answer?

Offer a fixed price for a smaller, defined discovery scope (named outputs, 14 weeks) at the cap, if that helps procurement compare bids. But keep the build price tied to what discovery finds.

try it

Take a piece of work you're quoting. Write down who carries the uncertainty under T&M, fixed price and a discovery-then-fixed model. Which part do you actually know enough to fix? What would you pay milestones on?

remember!!

Pay for the fog by the day. Fix the price for a written world. Pay on results you can see.
