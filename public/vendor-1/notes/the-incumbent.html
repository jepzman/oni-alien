What About the Vendor Already Here?

TL;DR

The incumbent knows the client's systems, people and history better than you ever will in six weeks, and you'll probably need them whoever wins. Don't treat them as the enemy. Find out what they protect (a renewal, a reputation, their rates) and what you need from them (an interface, test support, a contact), meet them early and in the open, and put the overlap in writing. You can compete for one contract and cooperate on the next one.

1The situation a café opposite The Grid, 2 March 2027

Halvorsen Systems has run MetroVolt's billing since 2016. It has 34 custom integrations, the only people who understand MetroVolt's tariff engine, and a file from MAVIS every night at 02:10. Halvorsen is also bidding for all of Lodestar.

  • Wrenfield's design keeps Halvorsen billing. So dependency D-03, the billing interface spec, belongs to a rival.
  • Vivian's view: “They'll stall us. Let's not mention them.” Hollis will score the integration section; he'll notice.
  • On Tuesday 2 March Ines emails Sven Aldous, Halvorsen's account director, and asks him for coffee. He brings biscuits.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Hollis watches

MetroVolt has two big suppliers that will have to share a file at 02:10 for years. Hollis wants to see that the new one won't start a war with the old one. A bid that pretends Halvorsen doesn't exist is a bid that will blame Halvorsen later.

“Whoever wins, they'll have to work with Sven.”

Wrenfield Ines meets Sven

For Wrenfield, Halvorsen is a rival (it wants Lodestar too) and a dependency (Wrenfield's plan needs its interface). Ines needs Sven's cooperation in writing before she can price D-03, without giving away Wrenfield's design.

“Compete for this one. Cooperate on the next ten years.”

Incumbents are rarely villains. They're protecting a business they built honestly, often over years nobody else remembers.

3Real-life analogy the new builder and the old electrician

A new builder is hired for an extension, and the house's electrician has done the wiring for twelve years. The electrician also quoted for the extension and lost. The builder still needs him: he knows where every cable runs. The smart builder buys him a coffee, asks what he needs, and agrees in writing who connects what, at what rate.

4How it works what they protect, what you need, and a letter

Before the coffee, Ines writes two lists. What Halvorsen protects: its billing contract (up for renewal in 2029), its reputation if the new system breaks billing, and its rates for interface work. What Wrenfield needs: the interface specification by 30 July 2027, support in integration testing, a named technical contact.

At the café she says plainly: “You're bidding, we're bidding, one of us will lose. Whoever wins needs the billing interface working. If we win, we'll need your help, and we'll pay your standard rates for it. If you win, nothing changes for you.” She shares nothing about Wrenfield's design.

Sven asks for one thing: that the winner won't blame Halvorsen for MAVIS's data. Ines agrees that interface defects will be triaged jointly, by evidence. Halvorsen's letter of 9 March says it will support whichever supplier wins, at standard rates, with a named contact. The bid quotes it in the integration section. Hollis scores that section 4 out of 5.

  1. Map what the incumbent owns

    Systems, integrations, knowledge, contracts and renewal dates.

  2. Write what they protect

    Renewal, reputation, rates. Usually reasonable.

  3. Write what you need

    Specific: documents, dates, people, support.

  4. Meet early, openly, and say it's a contest

    Don't pretend; don't fish for their bid.

  5. Offer something real

    Paid work at their rates, fair blame rules.

  6. Get the overlap in writing

    A short letter you can quote in the bid.

Vocabulary

Incumbent
The supplier already serving the client in this or a related area.
Co-opetition
Competing and cooperating with the same firm at the same time.
Interface agreement
A written deal on how two systems, and two suppliers, connect.
Lock-in
Being hard to replace because of knowledge, data or integrations only you hold.
Letter of support
A short written commitment from a third party, quoted in a bid.
Joint triage
Investigating a cross-supplier defect together, by evidence, before assigning blame.

5What the contract says what the letter makes possible later

Lodestar SOW · Appendix C (draft)from the bid
third party

D-03 The billing supplier provides the interface specification for customer and meter data by 30 July 2027 and technical support during integration testing, as set out in its letter of 9 March 2027.

a dependency on a third party is only real if someone promised it in writing.

3.6 Where a Dependency is to be provided by a third-party supplier of MetroVolt, MetroVolt shall use reasonable endeavours to procure its provision.

MetroVolt manages Halvorsen. we don't.

In plain words: you have no contract with the incumbent; the client does. So the dependency should say what the incumbent promised, and the client should be responsible for getting it delivered.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Pretending they don't exist

    The bid ignores the system everyone knows you'll depend on. Fix → name them, and show the plan.

  • Fishing for their bid

    Using the coffee to learn their price. Fix → say it's a contest and keep to the interface.

  • Badmouthing them

    The client chose them, and still pays them. Fix → talk about the interface, not their faults.

  • A verbal promise

    “Sure, we'll help” evaporates after you win. Fix → a dated letter.

  • Making them your dependency, not the client's

    You have no contract with them. Fix → the client procures their support.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • An incumbent holds a system you must integrate with.
  • The incumbent is bidding against you.
  • The client will have to live with both of you.

Skip it when…

  • There's no incumbent, or no shared boundary.

11Self-check quiz

Why is the incumbent both a rival and a dependency?

It bids for the same work, and the winner needs its systems and interfaces.

What did Halvorsen protect?

Its 2029 billing renewal, its reputation if billing broke, and its rates.

What did Ines offer Sven?

Paid work at standard rates and joint, evidence-based triage of interface defects.

Why should the dependency be MetroVolt's to procure?

Wrenfield has no contract with Halvorsen; only MetroVolt can make Halvorsen deliver.

What should you never do at a meeting with a rival bidder?

Fish for their price or share your own design or price.
