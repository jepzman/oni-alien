Who Wrote This Tender?

TL;DR

When a tender uses another supplier's words, it tells you who the client has been talking to, not necessarily that the game is fixed. The test: are there requirements only one supplier could meet? If yes, ask about them openly in a clarification question and be ready to walk away. If not, it's familiarity: ask whether reuse is expected, design for what the client actually needs, and borrow their words where it helps them read your bid.

1The situation Wrenfield, 19 February 2027

Hana finds it first. Section 4 of the tender describes customer data in five phrases: “Golden Customer Record”, “the hub”, “single customer view”, “survivorship rules” and, in requirement 4.7, “reuse of existing hub components where appropriate”. Four of them are on a 2024 Vexmoor slide about Orion, still on a conference website.

  • Vexmoor built Orion's data hub. Orion was cancelled in January.
  • If “the hub” must be reused, Vexmoor knows it and nobody else does.
  • Vivian: “It's rigged. We should complain.” Graham: “We should withdraw.” Neither has asked a single question yet.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Desmond, under deadline

Desmond had three weeks to turn a business case into a tender. Section 4 came from the Orion requirements, because they were the only written description of MetroVolt's customer data. He removed Orion's name. He didn't notice the vocabulary.

“We reused what we had. That's not favouritism, that's January.”

Wrenfield Hana and Ines

To Wrenfield it looks like a head start for Vexmoor. Maybe it is. But requirement 4.7 says “where appropriate”, not “must”. The only way to find out is to ask, in writing, where every bidder will see the answer.

“Are we being told to build on Orion, or allowed to?”

Most “wired” tenders are written by busy people who copied what they had. A few really are written for one supplier. The clarification answer tells you which.

3Real-life analogy the house listing written by the old owner

A house for sale is described with the previous owner's words: “the snug”, “Dad's workshop”. That tells you who wrote the listing, not that the house is promised to a relative. But if the listing says “buyers must keep the workshop exactly as it is”, you'd want to ask why, before you make an offer.

4How it works test for lock-in, ask openly, then decide

Ines and Hana go through section 4 requirement by requirement with one question: could only one supplier meet this? Three phrases are just vocabulary. One (4.7, “reuse of existing hub components where appropriate”) could become a lock-in if MetroVolt meant “must”.

They write it as a neutral clarification question, with no accusation and no hint of Wrenfield's design: “Requirement 4.7: is reuse of Orion components expected, or may bidders propose an alternative approach to customer data?” The answer, published to all bidders on 3 March: “Bidders may propose reuse; no Orion component is mandated.” The same answer list shows that Vexmoor asked whether it may reuse the hub. They plan to. That's useful too.

Wrenfield keeps bidding, uses MetroVolt's words (“customer record”, not “golden”) where it helps the evaluators, and designs for what MetroVolt needs rather than for or against Orion.

  1. Notice the borrowed words

    Phrases, structure, odd specifics: write them down.

  2. Apply the one-supplier test

    Could only one firm meet this requirement as written?

  3. Ask, neutrally, in writing

    Through the clarification process; no accusations.

  4. Read the answer, and the others' questions

    Both tell you how the field is thinking.

  5. Decide with the facts

    Lock-in confirmed: re-run bid / no-bid. Familiarity: carry on.

  6. Never badmouth the last supplier

    The panel worked with them; some of them liked them.

Vocabulary

Wired tender
A tender written so only one supplier can win; rarer than people think.
Lock-in requirement
A requirement only one supplier can meet: a named product, years on a platform.
Neutral question
A clarification question that reveals nothing about your design or suspicions.
Challenge
A formal complaint about a procurement; a last resort with real costs.
Head start
Knowledge one bidder has from past work; legal, but you need to price around it.
Client vocabulary
The client's own words for things; use them so evaluators can score you.

5What the contract says the rules on equal treatment

Lodestar ITT · instructions to bidders3 Feb 2027
answered to all

5.2 Clarification questions must be submitted in writing by 24 February 2027. MetroVolt will publish all questions and answers, without the name of the bidder, to every bidder.

so the question must be useful to us even when Vexmoor reads it.

5.5 Where a clarification changes the requirements, MetroVolt will issue an addendum and may extend the deadline.

4.7 wasn't changed. just explained.

In plain words: equal treatment cuts both ways: your questions are shown to rivals, and their questions are shown to you. Ask neutrally, and read everyone else's questions as intelligence.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Seeing a conspiracy in every copied phrase

    Most tenders borrow from what came before. Fix → apply the one-supplier test.

  • Asking a loaded question

    “Why is this tender written for Vexmoor?” helps nobody. Fix → ask what's expected, neutrally.

  • Revealing your design in a question

    Rivals read every question. Fix → ask about the requirement, not your solution.

  • Attacking the last supplier

    The panel worked with them for three years. Fix → talk about what you'll do, not what they did.

  • Challenging without advice

    A formal challenge costs money and goodwill. Fix → only with a clear breach, and proper advice.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A tender uses a past supplier's vocabulary.
  • A requirement names a product, platform or years of experience on it.
  • Someone on the team says “it's rigged”.

Skip it when…

  • The words are generic industry terms everyone uses.

11Self-check quiz

What is the one-supplier test?

Asking whether only one supplier could meet a requirement as written.

Why are most “wired” tenders not really wired?

Busy people copy the only written description they have, usually from the last project.

Why must a clarification question be neutral?

Every bidder sees it, and the client reads it; it should reveal nothing and accuse no one.

What did the Q&A reveal about Vexmoor?

They asked whether they may reuse the Orion hub, so they plan to.

Why not criticise Orion in the bid?

The panel worked on it; attacking it attacks them.

what would you do?The answer had said “bidders must reuse the hub”. What then?

That's a lock-in. Go back to bid / no-bid: can you price learning the hub, or partner, or is it a no? A formal challenge only with advice and a clear reason.

try it

Take a tender or a requirements document you've received. Highlight any phrase you've seen in someone else's material. Apply the one-supplier test to each. Which one would you ask about, and how would you word it neutrally?

remember!!

Borrowed words usually mean a tired author, not a fixed game. Ask neutrally, then decide.
