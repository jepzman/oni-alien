Who Will Vouch for Us?

TL;DR

References win on specifics, and the most convincing ones include something that went wrong and how you handled it. Choose referees by the client's fears, not by who loves you most. Brief them on what the client is worried about; never script their answers. For a site visit, show the people who'd step in if a key person fell ill, and how you actually work on an ordinary Tuesday.

1The situation reference calls on 23 April, site visit on 28 April 2027

Two days after the orals, Desmond asks for two references and a site visit. Elaine sends her list of questions in advance. The first: “Tell me about a time Wrenfield gave you bad news.”

  • Vivian wants Wrenfield's happiest client, a small charity website, and a short script.
  • MetroVolt's fears, heard at the orals: legacy data, honesty about delays, working alongside another supplier. The charity website answers none of them.
  • Halvorsen is out of the contest after the orals. It's Wrenfield and Vexmoor.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Elaine calls

Elaine doesn't want to hear that Wrenfield is lovely. She wants to know what it's like when things go wrong: who calls, how early, and whether the story changes. Every reference she took for Orion was glowing.

“Tell me about the bad day.”

Wrenfield Ines chooses

The most useful reference is Pennine Housing Group: a tenancy system for 140,000 tenancies, moved in 2025, six weeks late. Wrenfield told them in week three and re-planned in the open. It's not the happiest story. It's the one that answers question 1.

“Our best reference is our worst month, handled well.”

A reference with a problem in it is more believable than one without, because every real project has one.

3Real-life analogy the tradesperson's past customers

Before hiring a builder, you ask to speak to past customers. The one who says “perfect, no problems” tells you little. The one who says “they found rot under the floor, told us that afternoon, showed us photos and the price, and still finished a week late” tells you exactly what you need: what happens on the bad day.

4How it works choose by their fears, brief, don't script

Ines lists MetroVolt's three fears and picks one referee for each. Pennine Housing Group (Joanne Pryce, IT director): a legacy tenancy system, data cleaned in waves, six weeks late and told in week three. Ashby Building Society (Tom Hendry, head of change): a 2024 integration with a mainframe, working daily alongside the core banking vendor.

Ines calls each referee before MetroVolt does. She tells them what MetroVolt is worried about and asks them to be honest, including about what went wrong. She doesn't send a script. Joanne tells Elaine: “They rang me on a Tuesday in week three, said we'd be six weeks late and why, and the date they gave me then was the date we went live.”

At the site visit on 28 April, Elaine and Hollis meet the two engineers who'd cover for Hana, see Wrenfield's weekly status template and a real (anonymised) RAID log, and watch a stand-up. Hollis asks to see a test report. He gets the real one, including two failing tests.

  1. List the client's fears

    From the tender, the questions and the orals.

  2. Pick a referee for each fear

    Similar problem, recent, reachable.

  3. Call them first

    Thank them, tell them the client's worries.

  4. Ask them to be honest

    Including what went wrong. Never script answers.

  5. Show an ordinary day at the site visit

    Real templates, real logs, the people behind the key people.

  6. Thank them afterwards

    Whatever the result.

Vocabulary

Reference
A past client who agrees to describe working with you.
Site visit
The client visits your office to see how you work.
Due diligence
The client's checks before contract: references, finances, security.
Bench
The people in your firm who could step in on the account.
Scripted reference
A referee given answers to recite; usually obvious and useless.
Proof point
A specific, checkable fact supporting a claim.

5What the contract says what due diligence can ask for

Lodestar ITT · §8 due diligence3 Feb 2027
checked

8.1 MetroVolt may contact references, visit the bidder's premises and request evidence of financial standing before award.

they'll also read our accounts. £9m turnover, no debt. good.

8.3 Information provided during due diligence that is materially inaccurate may lead to exclusion.

the real test report, then. with the two failures.

In plain words: references and visits are part of the evaluation, and misleading the client during them can exclude you. Honest is also the safe option.

6Account health the same move, green, amber and red

  • One reference may do.
  • Offer the visit anyway.
  • Still pick by their fears.

Amber Lodestar now

  • A referee for each fear.
  • Honest stories, including a bad one.

  • Specifics only; no adjectives.
  • Expect calls you didn't arrange.
  • Don't over-manage the referees.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • The happiest client

    A glowing reference on a different kind of work. Fix → choose by the client's fears.

  • Scripting

    Referees recite; the client notices. Fix → brief on the worries, never the answers.

  • Surprising the referee

    They get a call they weren't expecting. Fix → call them first.

  • A staged site visit

    A tidy office and a perfect log. Fix → show an ordinary Tuesday.

  • Forgetting to thank them

    You'll need them again. Fix → thank them whatever happens.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • References and site visits are requested.
  • The client's fears are known.
  • You have a past project with a problem handled well.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip it when asked. If you have no relevant reference, say so and offer something else (a pilot, a paid discovery).

11Self-check quiz

Why is a reference with a problem in it more convincing?

Every real project has one; hearing how it was handled is the evidence the client needs.

How should you choose referees?

One for each of the client's fears, on similar work.

What's the difference between briefing and scripting a referee?

Briefing tells them the client's worries; scripting gives them answers to recite.

What should a site visit show?

An ordinary day: real templates, real logs, and the people behind the key people.

Why show the real test report with two failures?

A perfect one looks staged, and misleading the client in due diligence can exclude you.

what would you do?Your best reference left their company last month. What do you do?

Tell the client, offer their successor or another client on similar work, and, if they're willing, the former contact as a personal reference. Don't hide the change.

try it

List your client's three biggest fears. For each, find a past project that answers it, including what went wrong. Who would you call, and what would you tell them before the client does?

remember!!

The best reference is your worst month, handled well, told by someone else, unscripted.
