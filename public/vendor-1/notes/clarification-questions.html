What Do We Ask Before We Answer?

TL;DR

Clarification questions are public: every bidder sees every question and every answer. Ask only what changes your price, risk or design; word it so it reveals nothing about your solution or your doubts; and keep it answerable in two sentences. Then read all the answers, especially the questions other bidders asked. They're the only honest look you'll get at how your rivals think.

1The situation Wrenfield, 23 February 2027

The clarification window closes on Wednesday 24 February at 17:00. The team has 31 candidate questions. Lottie's first draft asks whether MAVIS can emit change events “for our event-stream strangler approach using Wrenfield DataLens”.

  • It names Wrenfield's design, its tool and its pattern, in a document every rival will read.
  • Fourteen of the 31 wouldn't change anything about the price or the plan.
  • Two are really five questions in one, which MetroVolt would answer with “please refer to the tender”.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Desmond answers 63 questions

Every answer has to be checked by Hollis, Elaine or legal, then published to all. Desmond has one week. Long, vague or leading questions get a short, safe answer: “please refer to the tender”.

“Ask me something I can answer in two lines.”

Wrenfield Ines filters 31 into 9

Each question is a chance to shrink a risk before pricing it, but also a window rivals can look through. Ines wants answers that change Wrenfield's price or plan, without telling Vexmoor what Wrenfield is planning.

“If the answer won't change a number, why ask?”

Good questions help both sides: the client finds the holes in its own tender, and every bidder prices the same thing.

3Real-life analogy questions at a press conference

At a press conference every journalist hears every question. A good reporter asks the one question that gets a fact they can use, in words that don't reveal their story. A bad one gives a speech, asks three things at once, and gets “no comment”. And the smartest reporters listen hardest to everyone else's questions.

4How it works three gates, then read everything

Each of the 31 candidates goes through three gates. Does the answer change our price, risk or design? Fourteen don't, so they become assumptions instead. Does the wording give anything away? Six are reworded: Lottie's becomes “Does MAVIS provide any mechanism to identify records changed since a given time? If so, please describe it.” Can it be answered in two sentences? Two are split. Nine are sent.

The answers come back on 3 March: all 63, from five bidders. Three of Wrenfield's change the price (data volumes, the Halvorsen interface and that discovery can be paid separately). And the other bidders' questions are a map: Vexmoor asked about reusing Orion's hub and about offshore delivery; someone asked about the five-year support term four different ways.

  1. List every question

    Everything anyone on the team wonders. Don't filter yet.

  2. Gate 1: does it change a number?

    No? Make it an assumption instead.

  3. Gate 2: does it give anything away?

    Reword it to ask about the requirement, not your answer.

  4. Gate 3: two-sentence answer?

    Split anything compound.

  5. Send before the deadline, not at it

    Late questions get rushed answers.

  6. Read all the answers as a team

    Mark what changes the bid; note what rivals asked.

Vocabulary

Clarification question
A written question to the client during a tender; answers go to all bidders.
Q&A log
The published list of all questions and answers; part of the tender.
Addendum
A formal change to the tender issued after questions.
Leading question
A question that assumes or pushes an answer; it usually gets a non-answer.
Assumption
What you'll write down and price when you choose not to ask.
Competitive intelligence
What you can learn about rivals legitimately, e.g. from their published questions.

5What the contract says how answers become part of the deal

Lodestar ITT · instructions to bidders3 Feb 2027
part of the ITT

5.4 Answers published under paragraph 5.2 form part of the tender documents and will be incorporated into the contract with the successful bidder.

so Q-05's “sample by 1 April” is now a promise. good.

5.6 MetroVolt may decline to answer questions it considers commercially sensitive or already addressed in the tender.

the “please refer to the tender” clause.

In plain words: a clarification answer is part of the contract you'll sign. That's what makes a good question valuable: it turns a client's vague intention into a written commitment, for every bidder.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Giving away your design

    A question names your pattern, tool or plan. Fix → ask about the requirement, not your answer.

  • Asking everything

    Thirty questions, most of which change nothing. Fix → the ones that don't change a number become assumptions.

  • Compound questions

    Five questions in one get one vague answer. Fix → one question, two-sentence answer.

  • Only reading your own answers

    You miss what rivals are planning. Fix → read all of them as a team.

  • Asking at the deadline

    Rushed answers, or none. Fix → send early.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every formal tender with a clarification window.
  • When an answer would change your price, risk or design.
  • When a requirement could be read two ways.

Skip it when…

  • The answer wouldn't change anything you'd do: write an assumption instead.

11Self-check quiz

Who sees a clarification question?

Every bidder, along with its answer.

What are the three gates?

Does it change price, risk or design; does it give anything away; can it be answered in two sentences.

What happens to a question that fails gate one?

It becomes an assumption instead.

Why do clarification answers matter after the contract?

They become part of the tender documents and the contract.

What did Wrenfield learn from Vexmoor's questions?

That Vexmoor plans to reuse Orion's hub and may deliver offshore.

what would you do?A colleague wants to ask “Why is the timetable so short?” What do you suggest?

It's leading and won't change a number. If the timetable is a risk, ask something answerable, e.g. whether the contract start date is fixed, or write an assumption about the start date.

try it

Take the open questions on a proposal you're working on. Put each through the three gates. How many would you actually send, and how would you reword one so a rival learns nothing from it?

remember!!

Every rival reads your questions. Ask what changes a number, in words that give nothing away.
