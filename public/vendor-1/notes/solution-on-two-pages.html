How Do I Design on Two Pages of Facts?

TL;DR

In a bid you design with almost no facts. Do it honestly: put three real options side by side (not one favourite and two straw men), judge them on the same few criteria (especially how each one fails), choose one you can draw on a single page, and write down what you must learn before you commit. Pitch the design at the level you'd be happy to be held to, because the bid design often becomes part of the contract.

  • Family: shaping the answer
  • Lives in: bid section 3 + appendix A (options)
  • Pairs with: assumptions, estimating, the strangler fig

1The situation Wrenfield, 9 March 2027

Everything Wrenfield knows about MAVIS fits on two pages of the tender: written in 1997, COBOL on a mainframe, 2.31 million live accounts, a nightly batch, and a file sent to Halvorsen billing at 02:10. The clarification answers added one fact: MAVIS can't say what changed during the day, only produce a nightly extract.

  • The bid needs a solution section worth 20 % of the quality score, in six pages.
  • Hana has a favourite design (an event stream off the mainframe's logs). It's elegant. Nobody knows yet if MAVIS can feed it.
  • The tender says the bid's design “will form Schedule 2 of the contract”. Whatever Wrenfield draws, it may have to build.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Hollis will score it

Hollis has seen Orion's beautiful architecture diagrams, and a project that never went live. He'll score how believable the design is, not how modern. He wants to see that the bidder understood MAVIS and has a plan for the parts nobody understands yet.

“Show me you know what you don't know.”

Wrenfield Ines and Hana draw it

Ines needs a design that scores well, can be priced, and won't trap Wrenfield if MAVIS turns out worse than two pages suggest. Hana needs her idea taken seriously. Both need the design at the right level: firm about the approach, open about the mechanisms.

“Commit to the shape, not to the plumbing.”

The level of detail is the real decision. Too vague and it scores badly; too detailed and Schedule 2 locks you into a guess.

3Real-life analogy the architect's sketch before the survey

An architect asked to extend an old house sketches three options before the structural survey: a full rebuild, a rear extension, or converting room by room while the family lives there. She can say which one she recommends and why, but she writes “subject to survey” on the drawing, and she doesn't specify the steel beams yet.

4How it works three real options, one page, and what you must learn

Ines and Hana write three options that a reasonable person could choose. A · big bang: a new platform for all 2.3 million accounts in one cutover. B · wrap and extend: put APIs in front of MAVIS and keep the mainframe. C · strangle in waves: a new customer platform takes one district at a time while MAVIS shrinks.

They score all three on the same five criteria: time to first value, cost, risk, fit with phase 2, and how it fails. That last one decides it. A fails all at once, which is how Orion was shaped. B never fails, and never finishes: MAVIS stays, and its last COBOL expert retires in 2029. C fails one wave at a time, and every wave is a rehearsal for the next.

The bid commits to the shape (waves, a new customer platform, MAVIS read-only for migrated districts, Halvorsen kept) and lists the mechanisms as “to be confirmed in discovery”: how to detect changes in MAVIS (Hana's event stream is option 1 of 3), how to reconcile records, and the Halvorsen interface. One context diagram, one wave diagram, two pages.

  1. List what you know and what you don't

    Two columns, before any drawing.

  2. Write three real options

    Ones a reasonable person could choose. No straw men.

  3. Score them on the same criteria

    Include “how does it fail?”

  4. Choose, and say why in one sentence

    The sentence goes in the bid.

  5. Commit to the shape, not the plumbing

    Mechanisms you can't know yet: “confirmed in discovery”.

  6. Draw it on one page

    If it needs three, the panel won't believe it either.

5What the contract says what the tender does with your design

Lodestar ITT · draft contract terms3 Feb 2027
becomes schedule 2

3.1 The Supplier's solution as described in its tender response shall form Schedule 2 (Solution) of the Contract.

so “event stream” in the bid = “event stream” in the contract.

3.3 Elements of Schedule 2 identified as subject to discovery may be refined by agreement following the discovery phase without a Change Request, provided the Charges are unaffected.

the escape hatch. use it on purpose.

In plain words: your bid design can become a contract schedule. Be precise about the approach you'll stand behind, and label the mechanisms you can't know yet as subject to discovery, so refining them later isn't a change request.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Straw-man options

    Two silly options and the favourite. Fix → three a reasonable person could choose.

  • Over-specifying in the bid

    Unproven plumbing ends up in Schedule 2. Fix → commit to the shape; label mechanisms “subject to discovery”.

  • Never asking how it fails

    Options are compared on cost and speed only. Fix → add “how does it fail?” as a criterion.

  • A twelve-box diagram

    Nobody believes what they can't follow. Fix → one page, one picture.

  • Hiding what you don't know

    The panel knows MAVIS is a mystery. Fix → list the unknowns; it reads as competence.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any bid with a solution section.
  • Legacy replacements where you can't see inside.
  • When the team has a favourite design.
  • When the bid becomes a contract schedule.

Skip it when…

  • You already run the system and know its insides: go straight to a detailed design.

11Self-check quiz

Why three options?

So the choice is visible and defensible; one option looks like a favourite, two look like a straw man.

Which criterion decided Lodestar's design?

How each option fails: big bang fails all at once; waves fail one at a time.

Why not choose “wrap and extend”?

MAVIS would never leave, and its last COBOL expert retires in 2029.

What does “commit to the shape, not the plumbing” mean?

Promise the approach (waves, new platform), but leave unproven mechanisms to discovery.

Why does the level of detail matter in this tender?

The bid design becomes Schedule 2 of the contract.

what would you do?Hana insists the event stream must be in the bid because it's “the best design”. What do you say?

It may well be, and it's option 1 of 3 for change detection. Until discovery shows MAVIS can feed it, putting it in Schedule 2 would make a guess contractual.

try it

Take a design decision you face. Write three options a reasonable person could choose, score them on five criteria including “how does it fail?”, and list what you'd need to learn before committing. Can you draw the winner on one page?
