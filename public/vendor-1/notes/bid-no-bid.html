Should We Even Bid?

TL;DR

A bid isn't free: Lodestar's will cost Wrenfield about 110 person-days whether it wins or not. Decide whether to bid with a scorecard of honest questions (can we win, can we deliver, can we afford to lose, can we afford to win?), a written reason to win, and the conditions you'd need. Decide early, before anyone falls in love with the drawings, and write down why.

1The situation Wrenfield, Leeds, 17 February 2027

Two weeks in, the bid team has already spent 31 person-days. On Wednesday 17 February Graham Wren calls the bid / no-bid meeting: eight people, one hour, a vote. Vivian opens with “this is the one that changes Wrenfield”. That's exactly what worries Ines.

  • Lodestar is worth about £5m over 18 months. Wrenfield's whole revenue last year was about £9m.
  • A fixed-price failure that size wouldn't hurt Wrenfield: it could close it.
  • Wrenfield has never met Priya, Elaine or Hollis. Vexmoor has worked there for three years.
  • The first vote is 6–2. Ines votes against. So does Oskar.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Desmond hopes for bids

MetroVolt wants at least three serious bids, or the procurement looks uncompetitive and the board asks why. A bidder who drops out late, after asking forty questions, wastes evaluators' time. A bidder who bids badly wastes more.

“Please bid properly, or tell us early you won't.”

Wrenfield Ines votes against, then for

Ines isn't against Lodestar. She's against bidding it the way Vivian wants: fixed price for everything, twelve months, whatever team is free. She'd vote for a bid with paid discovery first, Hana named as tech lead, and no twelve-month promise.

“I'll say yes to the bid we can deliver, not the one we can sell.”

The useful outcome of a bid / no-bid isn't the vote. It's the written conditions that the vote depends on.

3Real-life analogy the mountain guide's turn-back time

A mountain guide decides before the climb whether conditions are good enough, and sets a turn-back time: if we're not at the summit by 13:00, we go down, however close it looks. The decision is made at the bottom, calmly, because at the top everyone is tired and the summit looks very close.

4How it works ten honest questions, a reason to win, and conditions

Callum's scorecard has ten questions, each scored 0–3, filled in by each person alone before the meeting so the loudest voice doesn't set the scores. Lodestar averages 19 / 30; Wrenfield's threshold is 18. The low scores are the useful part: we don't know the client (1), the risk isn't bounded (1), could we afford to win? (1).

Then the reason to win, in one sentence the panel would recognise: “A small senior team that has moved a legacy customer system before, and will tell you the truth about your data.” If you can't write it, don't bid.

Ines changes her vote when the conditions go into the minutes: (1) a paid discovery phase before any fixed price; (2) Hana named as tech lead, not “a senior engineer”; (3) no twelve-month promise in writing. Graham agrees. A second check is set for 10 March, after the clarification answers.

  1. Count what the bid will cost

    People, days and the work you'll turn down meanwhile.

  2. Score alone, then together

    Each person fills the scorecard before the meeting.

  3. Read the low scores aloud

    They're the risks you'll be pricing.

  4. Write the reason to win

    One sentence the panel would agree with. No sentence, no bid.

  5. Write the conditions

    What must be true for a yes. Into the minutes.

  6. Set a turn-back point

    A date to check the conditions again, and walk away if they fail.

Vocabulary

Win theme
The one reason a client should choose you, repeated across the bid.
Cost of sale
What it costs to win (or lose) a contract: mostly people's time.
Win rate
Bids won ÷ bids made; a falling one usually means poor qualification.
Turn-back point
A planned moment to re-check the decision and withdraw if needed.

5What the contract says our own board's rules for bidding

Wrenfield Digital · bid policy v3approved by the board, Jan 2026
conditions apply

2.1 Any bid above £1m or above 20 % of annual revenue requires a recorded bid / no-bid decision by the managing director and two directors.

Lodestar is both.

2.4 Fixed-price bids above £2m require a priced risk register and a named delivery lead before submission.

Ines, apparently.

2.6 Conditions recorded at bid / no-bid must be re-confirmed before submission.

C3 is going to be fun.

In plain words: the most important contract in a bid is the one with your own firm. Writing the conditions into the minutes turns “Ines is worried” into “the board decided”.

6Account health the same move, green, amber and red

Green

you know them and they know you

  • The scorecard is quick.
  • The reason to win is your track record there.
  • Still write the conditions.

Amber Lodestar now

first contact, strong fit

  • Score honestly; low scores become risks to price.
  • Conditions in the minutes.
  • A turn-back date after the answers.

Red

the client barely knows you exist

  • Ask whether you're making up the numbers.
  • No reason to win, no bid.
  • Spend the 110 days on a client who knows you.

  • If your conditions are in the minutes, you don't have to win the argument twice.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Deciding by excitement

    The size of the prize decides, not the odds. Fix → score alone first, then talk.

  • No reason to win

    The bid says the same as everyone else's. Fix → one sentence the panel would recognise, or no bid.

  • Conditions nobody wrote down

    The worries are forgotten by week five. Fix → conditions in the minutes, re-checked before submission.

  • Never walking away

    Six weeks in, it's too late emotionally. Fix → set a turn-back date and keep it.

  • Forgetting “can we afford to win?”

    A big fixed price can hurt more than losing. Fix → score it explicitly.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any bid that costs more than a few days.
  • Any fixed price, or a deal large for your firm.
  • When the room is excited.
  • Before the first draft, and again after clarifications.

Skip it when…

  • A repeat order from a client you know, on terms you've delivered before.

11Self-check quiz

Why fill in the scorecard alone first?

So the most senior or loudest person doesn't set everyone's scores.

What is a reason to win?

One sentence that says why this client should choose you; if you can't write it, don't bid.

Why ask “can we afford to win?”

A large fixed-price contract can hurt a small firm more than losing it would.

What turned Ines's vote from no to yes?

Three conditions in the minutes: paid discovery, Hana named, and no twelve-month promise.

What is a turn-back point?

A planned date to re-check the conditions and withdraw if they've failed.

what would you do?At the turn-back point the clarification answers say discovery can't be paid separately. What do you do?

Condition C1 has failed, so go back to Graham and the directors: either withdraw, or price the discovery risk into the fixed price openly and re-vote. Don't quietly carry on.

try it

Take an opportunity you're chasing (a bid, a project, a job). Score it on the ten questions, alone. Write your reason to win in one sentence, and three conditions for a yes. Would you still go?

remember!!

Decide at the bottom of the mountain. Write the conditions, and keep the turn-back time.
