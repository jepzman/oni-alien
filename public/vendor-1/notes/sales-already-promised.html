What If Sales Already Promised?

TL;DR

When someone has already promised a date you can't hit, you can't un-say it, but you can re-anchor it: give the client something real by the promised date, say the whole truth next to it, and do it in writing, early, near where the old number lives. Do it with the person who promised, not around them. The later the correction, the more it looks like failure instead of honesty.

  • Family: up against the others
  • Lives in: bid executive summary, first paragraph
  • Pairs with: estimating in fog, the orals

1The situation Wrenfield, 22 March 2027

Two days before the deadline, Vivian admits the whole story. At a dinner on 19 January, before the tender came out, she told Priya Nair: “Wrenfield could get all your customers off MAVIS in twelve months.” Priya repeated it to Lord Varnish. Lord Varnish put it in an executive update.

  • Phase 1 is 380,000 accounts. “All customers” is 2.3 million. Ines's estimate for everything is about 30 months.
  • The tender's rules (clause 6.7) forbid contact with MetroVolt staff. Vivian can't call Priya to correct it.
  • If the bid simply says “12 months” for wave 1, MetroVolt will hear “12 months for everything”, and remember Wrenfield said it.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Priya, holding the number

Priya didn't invent “twelve months”; a supplier told her. She's repeated it upwards. If the bids now say thirty, she looks naive in front of Lord Varnish. She'll favour a bidder who lets her keep face and tells her the truth.

“I need a twelve-month story I can still tell.”

Wrenfield Ines, Vivian and Graham

Vivian's promise is Wrenfield's promise now. Ines won't repeat it. Graham won't let the bid contradict Vivian so bluntly that Priya feels lied to. They need a sentence that keeps the date, shrinks the promise and states the whole truth.

“Something true by month twelve, and the rest said out loud.”

Re-anchoring isn't saying “we were wrong”. It's giving the client something real by the date they heard, and the true shape of the rest.

3Real-life analogy the decorator who said “done by Christmas”

A decorator says “the whole house, done by Christmas”, then measures it and realises that's impossible. She doesn't wait until 20 December. In November she says: “By Christmas you'll have the living room and the kitchen finished, so you can host. The bedrooms follow in January, one a week.” The family still gets Christmas.

4How it works keep the date, shrink the promise, say it in writing

The three of them agree a rule: Vivian's date stays; the scope attached to it changes; the whole truth sits right next to it. The first paragraph of the executive summary, in bold:

“In twelve months, 380,000 MetroVolt customers will be off MAVIS, and the first 20,000 within nine. Moving all 2.3 million will take about thirty months, in waves, each faster than the last because each one teaches the next.”

It's in writing, so it's what MetroVolt quotes from now on. It's early: in the bid, not after signature. It's near the old number: the phrase “twelve months” is there, so Priya's story survives. And it gives something real by the old date. Vivian reads it and says, quietly, “that's what I should have said in January.” At the orals she'll say it herself.

  1. Find out exactly what was promised

    Words, date, to whom, and who it was repeated to.

  2. Do it with the person who promised

    Not around them. They have to own the correction too.

  3. Keep the date, change what's attached

    Something real and valuable by the promised date.

  4. Put the whole truth next to it

    The full timeline, in the same sentence or the next.

  5. Write it where the old number lives

    Executive summary, first paragraph.

  6. Say it out loud at the first chance

    The orals, by the person who made the promise.

Vocabulary

Sales handover
Passing what sales said and promised to the people who'll deliver it.

5What the contract says what the bid's own words commit to

Lodestar bid · executive summary and §4.224 Mar 2027
in writing

ES.1 In twelve months, 380,000 MetroVolt customers will be off MAVIS, and the first 20,000 within nine. Moving all 2.3 million will take about thirty months, in waves.

the only place “twelve months” appears without a scope next to it: nowhere.

ITT 5.4 The tender response, including its executive summary, will be incorporated into the contract.

so the re-anchor is contractual. good.

In plain words: the bid becomes part of the contract, so the re-anchored sentence is now what Wrenfield promised, not the dinner. Make sure the old number never appears on its own anywhere in the document.

7Pressure gauge how hard this is on the team

4 of 5 · someone else's promise

a hard conversation with a senior colleague · two days to the deadline

  • Vivian didn't lie; she was hopeful at dinner. Treat it as a fixable mistake, not a crime, and she'll help fix it.
  • If you correct it around her, you'll win the bid and lose the account director.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Hoping it was forgotten

    It wasn't. It's in a board paper. Fix → find out exactly where the number went.

  • Correcting it around the promiser

    Wins the bid, loses the colleague. Fix → do it with them; let them say it.

  • Contradicting it bluntly

    The sponsor looks naive in front of her boss. Fix → keep the date, change what's attached.

  • Waiting until after signature

    Then it looks like failure. Fix → re-anchor in the bid.

  • The old number on its own, anywhere

    One stray “12 months” undoes the paragraph. Fix → search the document for it.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Sales, a manager or a colleague has promised something undeliverable.
  • A number has been repeated upwards.
  • Before signature, while the words can still change.

Skip it when…

  • The promise is achievable: then don't argue with it, deliver it.

11Self-check quiz

What does re-anchoring mean?

Replacing a remembered number with a true one, kept close to the old one.

Why keep the date instead of correcting it?

The client has repeated it; giving something real by that date lets them keep their story.

Why couldn't Vivian just call Priya?

The tender rules forbid contact with MetroVolt staff outside the clarification process.

Why involve the person who made the promise?

They have to own the correction, or you lose them as an ally.

Why search the whole bid for the old number?

One stray “twelve months” on its own would undo the re-anchor.
