How Long Will It Really Take?

TL;DR

An estimate is a range with odds, not a date. Estimate bottom up in ranges (best, likely, worst), run them together to get a P50 and a P80, then check the result against a reference class: what similar projects really took, including the client's own failures. Commit to a date between P50 and P80, price the effort at P80, and say both numbers out loud so nobody remembers only the nice one.

1The situation Wrenfield, 15 March 2027

Nine days before the deadline, three numbers are on the whiteboard and none of them is Wrenfield's estimate. Vivian's: twelve months, for everything, said to Priya over dinner on 19 January. Orion's: planned at twelve months, cancelled at thirty-six. The industry's: five comparable customer-system replacements took 14 to 30 months in full.

  • Phase 1 is only wave 1: 380,000 of 2.3 million accounts. Vivian's “everything” was never in scope.
  • Callum's first plan is a single bar chart ending exactly at month 12. Nobody can say how likely it is.
  • MetroVolt's board will remember one number. It had better be one Wrenfield can hit.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Nkem, finance

MetroVolt's finance team needs one date and one price for the business case. Orion gave them both, and both were wrong. Nkem will believe a range more than a precise date, but the board paper still needs a line that says “live by”.

“Give me a date you'd bet your own money on.”

Wrenfield Ines and Callum

Wrenfield needs a date it can commit to, an effort it can price, and a story that explains the gap between them. A precise single number would look confident and be a lie. A wide range would look honest and lose the bid.

“Commit to a date. Price the bad luck.”

The trick is to stop treating the date you commit to and the effort you price as the same number.

3Real-life analogy the satnav's arrival time

A satnav says “arrive 14:20”. An experienced driver knows that's the likely time on a good day, and that on a Friday it's more like 14:50. For a wedding you leave for 14:50, and you tell the bride 14:30. You also remember that the last three times you drove there, it took longer than the satnav said.

4How it works bottom up in ranges, then check against history

Bottom up. Hana, Bilal, Oskar and Callum split wave 1 into nine work streams (platform, customer data, change detection, Halvorsen interface, migration tooling, reconciliation, testing, cutover, training). Each gets three numbers: best, likely, worst. A spreadsheet runs them together 10,000 times. Result, from contract start: P50 11 months, P80 14 months.

Reference class. Ines asks five former colleagues and two references for real durations of first waves in similar replacements: 9 to 16 months. The two methods agree, which is the point of doing both. Orion is in the reference class too, and the answer to “why will this be different?” goes into the bid: waves, a pilot gate, and paid discovery.

The decision. Commit to first 20,000 customers live in month 9 and all 380,000 in month 12. Price the effort at P80, so the contingency pays for bad luck instead of Wrenfield's margin. Both numbers go in the bid, with a sentence explaining them.

  1. Split the work into streams

    Seven to twelve, each with an owner who'll do the work.

  2. Three numbers per stream

    Best, likely, worst. Worst means “bad but normal”, not disaster.

  3. Combine them

    A simple simulation gives P50 and P80; adding the likelies doesn't.

  4. Find the reference class

    What similar projects really took, including failures.

  5. Compare and explain

    If they disagree, find out why before you commit.

  6. Commit between P50 and P80; price at P80

    And write both numbers down.

Vocabulary

Three-point estimate
Best, likely and worst case for one piece of work.
P50 / P80
Dates you'd hit with 50 % / 80 % likelihood.
Reference class forecasting
Estimating from the real outcomes of similar past projects.
Monte Carlo simulation
Running the ranges together thousands of times to get a spread of outcomes.
Cone of uncertainty
Estimates are widest at the start and narrow as you learn.
Planning fallacy
People's habit of underestimating their own projects, even knowing others overran.

5What the contract says how dates become promises

Lodestar bid · section 4.2 (delivery plan)as submitted 24 Mar 2027
milestone dates

4.2.1 The Supplier will deliver the Pilot Milestone (20,000 accounts live) within 9 months and the Wave 1 Milestone (380,000 accounts live) within 12 months of the Contract start date, subject to the Assumptions in Appendix C.

“of the contract start date”, not “of 1 June”. the start will slip. it always does.

4.2.4 Milestone dates will be re-baselined at the end of discovery by agreement.

the cone narrows; the dates may move. in writing.

In plain words: tie every date to the start of the contract, not to a calendar day, and to the assumptions. Then a late signature or a false assumption moves the date without a fight.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • One number

    A single date with no odds. Fix → P50 and P80, always together.

  • Adding up the likelies

    The total is far more optimistic than any stream. Fix → combine ranges with a simulation.

  • Ignoring the client's own history

    Orion is the best reference class you have. Fix → include it, and say why you're different.

  • Pricing at the committed date

    Bad luck comes out of your margin. Fix → price effort at P80.

  • Dates on the calendar

    A late signature eats your schedule. Fix → dates in months from contract start.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any estimate someone will be held to.
  • Fixed-price bids.
  • When a date has already been said out loud.
  • When the client has a failed project behind it.

Skip it when…

  • A two-week task you've done many times: a single honest number is fine.

11Self-check quiz

What do P50 and P80 mean?

Dates you'd hit with a 50 % and an 80 % likelihood.

Why combine ranges with a simulation instead of adding the likely values?

Adding likelies ignores that some streams will go badly; the total comes out far too optimistic.

What is reference class forecasting?

Estimating from what similar projects really took, not from your own plan.

Why commit to month 12 but price at P80 (month 14)?

The date is the promise; the price has to survive normal bad luck without eating the margin.

Why tie dates to the contract start date?

So a late signature moves the dates automatically.

what would you do?Graham asks you to commit to the P50 date to win. What do you say?

A P50 date is a coin toss. If he wants it, the price must carry the risk, or the bid must offer something smaller by that date. Show him the curve and what the bad half costs Wrenfield.

try it

Pick a piece of work you're estimating. Split it into five to nine streams with best, likely and worst numbers. Then find three similar projects and what they really took. Do the two agree? Which date would you commit to, and which would you plan for?

remember!!

An estimate is a range with odds. Commit between P50 and P80, and price the bad luck.
