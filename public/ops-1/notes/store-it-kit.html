How Does a New Store Get Its IT?

TL;DR

Each new site needs the same IT: a line, a backup, Wi-Fi, devices, accounts, monitoring and a place in the on-call rota. Write it down once as a kit: what's needed, who orders it, its lead time, and in which week of the launch it happens. Order the slowest thing first, set up devices centrally so they arrive ready, and let each launch improve the kit for the next one.

1The situation Larder, 18–25 May 2026

At Sanne's lessons-learned session on 18 May, the top lesson from Rotterdam is “store IT is an eighth workstream”. From Eli's side it's easy to see why. The internet line was ordered on 30 March with a six-week lead time and arrived 16 days after opening. Thirty handhelds were set up by hand over two evenings, and 40 accounts were made by hand. Utrecht is next, with a target of eight weeks from lease to opening, and none of this is written down.

  • Long lead times are discovered, not planned.
  • Device set-up is evening work for one person.
  • Each store ends up slightly different.

2Real-life analogy the café chain's opening kit

A café chain opening its tenth café has a new-café kit: a list of everything from the coffee machine to the card reader, with who orders each one and how long it takes. The card reader takes six weeks, so it's ordered on day one. The till arrives already set up by head office, and the new manager plugs it in. After each opening, the kit gets a little better.

3How it solves it three phases, the slowest thing first, devices ready on arrival

On Monday 25 May Eli writes the store IT kit v1: 34 items in three phases, each with an owner and a lead time.

  • Week 1: order the internet line (6 weeks) and the 4G backup router, and book a Wi-Fi survey of the empty store.
  • Weeks 4–6: handhelds, label printers and the router are enrolled in device management centrally and shipped ready. Write the network sheet.
  • Last two weeks: accounts through the joiners checklist, the failover test, the store heartbeat alert, and the store added to the catalogue and the on-call handover.

Device management goes live on 26 May: €3 per device a month (about €280 for 92 devices), which is within the team's own spending limit. A handheld now enrols itself when it's switched on, so set-up drops from 20 minutes to 2, and PIN resets and app updates can be pushed from one screen. Sanne adds the kit to the launch playbook as workstream eight, with Eli as its owner.

  1. List everything the last site needed

    From the issue log and the tickets, not memory.

  2. Add an owner and a lead time to each

    Lead times decide the order.

  3. Put the slowest thing in week 1

    Lines, leases, permits.

  4. Set devices up centrally

    They arrive ready; nobody sets them up in the store.

  5. End with tests, not hopes

    Failover, heartbeat, a test order, accounts working.

  6. Improve the kit after each launch

    Version it; date the changes.

Vocabulary for the exam

Lead time
Time from ordering something to having it.
Standard build
One agreed set-up, used every time.
Device management (MDM)
Software that enrols, configures, updates and wipes devices centrally.
Zero-touch enrolment
A device configures itself the first time it's switched on.
Workstream
A group of launch work with its own owner.
Versioned checklist
A checklist with a version number and dated changes.

4The maturity ladder where are we, and what is the next rung?

1By hand

each store from scratch

  • Lead times discovered late.
  • Devices set up by hand.

2Written down Larder now

a versioned kit

  • Owners, lead times, three phases.
  • Devices enrolled centrally.

3Scripted

scripted set-up

  • Network, accounts and monitoring from one config file per store.
  • Tests run by script.

4Automatic

a store is configuration

  • A new store is a pull request.
  • Devices and alerts follow automatically.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Writing it from memory

    The forgotten line is forgotten again. Fix → build it from the issue log and the tickets.

  • No lead times

    The order of work is wrong. Fix → every item has one; the slowest goes first.

  • Setting up devices in the store

    Evenings by hand, each slightly different. Fix → enrol centrally; ship ready.

  • A kit that never changes

    Each launch's lessons are lost. Fix → version it, date the changes.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • You'll open (or set up) a similar site again.
  • A launch found long lead times too late.
  • Devices are set up by hand.
  • Before Utrecht.

Skip it when…

  • A truly one-off site you'll never repeat.

8Self-check quiz

What was Sanne's top lesson from Rotterdam?

Store IT is an eighth workstream.

Why is the internet line in week 1?

It has the longest lead time (six weeks); the order of work follows lead times.

What does device management change?

Handhelds enrol themselves when switched on: set-up goes from 20 minutes to 2, and updates and resets come from one screen.

How does the kit end?

With tests: failover, heartbeat, accounts working, the store in on-call. Not with hope.

What happens to the kit after Utrecht?

It's improved and versioned with the lessons, before the next store.

what would you do?A new store's lease is signed eight weeks before opening. Which three kit items happen this week?

Order the internet line, order the 4G backup router, and book the Wi-Fi survey: the slowest things first.

try it

Take the last site, office or team you set up. List every IT item it needed, with a lead time for each. Sort by lead time. Which ones would you now order in week 1?

remember!!

A launch is late by the length of its slowest order. Write the kit once, order the slow thing first, and ship devices ready.
