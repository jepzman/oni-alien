Can I Have a New Handheld?

TL;DR

Most of what reaches a service desk isn't broken; it's a standard request: a PIN reset, a new device, access to a tool. Give each common request a short form, an owner, fixed steps and a promised time, and push the simplest ones to the people closest to them (a store lead can reset a PIN). The common becomes fast and predictable, which leaves time for the unusual.

1The situation Larder, early June 2026

The new queue has 74 tickets after four weeks. Eli sorts them. 46 are the same six requests every time: PIN resets (23), broken handhelds (9), label printers (6), new laptops, tool access and new starters. Each is handled differently depending on who picks it up, and a store asking “how long?” gets “depends”. The queue has made the work visible. It hasn't made it any faster.

  • A picker with a locked handheld waits for IT to unlock it.
  • A broken handheld is picked up when someone has time.
  • Repeat work fills the queue, so new problems wait behind it.

2Real-life analogy the coffee bar's menu board

A coffee bar's menu board lists six standard drinks, each made the same way every time, with a price and roughly how long it takes. Order a flat white and you know what you'll get, and when. Something special? Ask the barista, and it may take longer. The board makes the common things fast, and frees the barista for the special orders.

3How it solves it six standard requests, and push the simplest to the store

From 1 June the queue offers six request types, each with its own two-line form, an owner, steps and a promised time:

  • PIN reset: done by the store lead in the device tool. 5 minutes, no ticket needed. Tomás and Yara learn it on 29 May.
  • Broken handheld: swapped from the store's spare pool of 3, kept by the store lead. The broken one goes into the queue for Eli to repair or replace. Picking loses one shift at most.
  • Label printer: check the known fixes first, then swap. 1 hour.
  • New laptop (3 working days), tool access (approved by the tool's owner, 1 working day), new starter (the joiners checklist, ready on day one if asked 3 days before).

Six weeks later, PIN resets have left the queue entirely, and the median time to close a request is 4 hours, down from 2 days.

  1. Sort a month of tickets

    Find the requests that repeat.

  2. Pick the top five or six

    Not every request needs a form.

  3. Write steps, owner and promised time

    One short page each.

  4. Push the simplest to the front line

    Store leads reset PINs.

  5. Keep spares where the work is

    Swap first, repair later.

  6. Measure time to close per request

    And move the next one down the ladder.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A form for everything

    Forty request types nobody can choose between. Fix → the top five or six; the rest are normal tickets.

  • Approval theatre

    A manager approves PIN resets. Fix → approve only what costs money or grants access.

  • No spares

    Every broken device waits for a repair. Fix → keep a pool where the work happens.

  • Treating requests as incidents

    Urgent flags on laptops; real outages wait. Fix → separate the two in the queue.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • The same requests fill the queue every week.
  • People ask “how long?” and get “it depends”.
  • Front-line staff wait for IT for simple things.
  • Before a hiring wave.

Skip it when…

  • Requests are rare and all different: keep them as normal tickets.

8Self-check quiz

What's the difference between a service request and an incident?

A request asks for something normal (a reset, a device, access); an incident is something broken.

What does a standard request need?

A short form, an owner, fixed steps and a promised time.

Why do store leads reset PINs?

It's simple, safe and urgent for the picker; making them wait for IT wastes a shift.

What is the spare pool for?

Swap a broken handheld at once, so picking continues, and repair the broken one later.

Which requests need approval?

Those that cost money or grant access, and the approval comes from whoever owns the thing.

what would you do?Rotterdam's spare pool is empty on a Friday afternoon and two handhelds break. What should happen now, and on Monday?

Now: borrow from Noord or Zuid, or move pickers to shared devices for the peak. Monday: refill the pool, and check whether three spares is enough for a 30-device store.

try it

Sort the last month of your requests. Which five repeat most? For each, write who does it, whether it needs approval, and a promised time. Which one could the requester do themselves?

remember!!

Make the common requests boring: same steps, same time, done by whoever is closest. Save the thinking for the new ones.
