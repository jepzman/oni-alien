What Do We Run, and Who Owns It?

TL;DR

A service catalogue lists what you run as the people who use it would describe it: “ordering”, not “VM 2”. Each line has an owner who answers for it, its users, how much it matters (a tier) and what happens if it stops. It turns a pile of servers and SaaS logins into a short list you can rank, staff and promise things about.

  • Lives in: ops/catalogue.md, reviewed every quarter

1The situation Larder, 2 April 2026

Eli writes everything he looks after on sticky notes: 47, from Larder Core's three VMs to Zuid's label printers and the domain name. Grouped by what people use them for, they become 11 services. Four of them have no owner at all: the store network, staff accounts and laptops, the nightly price script, and the domain and email. They're Eli's by default, because nobody else knows they exist.

  • Nobody can say which service matters most, so the loudest one wins.
  • An unowned service only gets attention when it breaks.
  • On-call can't be shared when nobody knows what they're on call for.

2Real-life analogy the menu and the oven list

A restaurant has two lists. Guests read the menu: dishes in plain words, each with a price. The kitchen has an equipment list: ovens, fridges, the dishwasher. Nobody orders an oven. The menu is what you promise, and each dish has a chef who answers for it when it comes back.

3How it solves it eleven lines, an owner each, and a tier

For each group Eli writes one line: name (in users' words), what it does, users, owner, tier and if it stops. Critical means customers can't order, pay, be picked for or be delivered to. Important means someone has a workaround for a few hours. Normal can wait until morning.

At Thursday's tech leads' meeting (2 April, 09:30) owners sign their own lines. The four orphans go to Eli for now, but written down, with a note: “Eli owns 6 of 11: next problem.” The catalogue becomes the spine for everything after it: dependencies, the on-call rota, the alerts and the runbooks.

  1. List everything you look after

    Sticky notes, no sorting yet.

  2. Group by what people use it for

    A service is something a user would name.

  3. Write one line per service

    Name, what it does, users, owner, tier, if it stops.

  4. Get each owner to sign their line

    An owner who wasn't asked isn't an owner.

  5. Mark the orphans honestly

    Unowned is a finding, not a failure.

  6. Review it every quarter

    And whenever something new goes live.

Vocabulary for the exam

Service catalogue
The list of live services, described for the people who use them.
Service owner
The person accountable for a service working: they don't have to do every task themselves.
Tier
How much a service matters; it sets on-call, alerting and recovery targets.
Component
A part a service runs on: a VM, a database, a router, a SaaS account.
Orphan
A service nobody owns; usually discovered during an incident.
Service portfolio
Everything in the pipeline, live and being retired; the catalogue is the live part.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Listing servers, not services

    “VM 2” means nothing to the person reporting a problem. Fix → name it as a user would.

  • Owners who weren't asked

    A name in a table nobody agreed to. Fix → owners sign their own line.

  • Everything is critical

    The tier stops meaning anything. Fix → critical = customers can't order, pay or get delivered.

  • A catalogue that never changes

    New services go live and never get added. Fix → add it to the go-live checklist.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Nobody can answer “what do we run?” in one minute.
  • Before you set up on-call or alerts.
  • A new service is about to go live (Rotterdam).
  • Incidents keep finding things nobody knew about.

Skip it when…

  • A team with one service and one owner.
  • You already have a maintained catalogue: review it instead.

8Self-check quiz

What is the difference between a service and a component?

A service is what users use and would name (ordering); a component is what it runs on (a VM, a database).

What does a service owner have to do?

Answer for the service working: decisions, priorities, being reachable. Not every task personally.

What makes a Larder service critical?

If it stops, customers can't order, pay, be picked for or be delivered to.

Why write down the orphans instead of quietly taking them?

Unowned services are a risk the business should see; hidden, they stay Eli's forever.

When must the catalogue be updated?

At every go-live, and at a quarterly review.

what would you do?A new service goes live without anyone adding it to the catalogue. Two months later it breaks at night. What would have helped?

A go-live checklist line: “added to the catalogue with owner and tier”. Then on-call would have known who to call and how much it mattered.

try it

List everything you look after on sticky notes. Group them into services a user would name. Write owner, tier and “if it stops” for each. How many have your name on them? How many have none?

remember!!

A service is what users would name, and every service needs a name next to it: its owner.
