What Is IT Operations For?

TL;DR

IT operations exists so that the services customers pay for keep working: an order placed, paid, picked and delivered. Servers, networks and laptops are the means, not the point. ITIL 4 calls the whole picture a service value system: value is created with the customer, when the service is used. Start every operations question from the outcome: who would notice, and how much would it cost them?

2Real-life analogy the hospital's boiler room

Nobody goes to a hospital for the boilers, the lifts or the backup generator. They go for an operation. The facilities team exists so the operating theatre is warm, lit and powered at 3 a.m. A good facilities manager knows which machines the theatre needs, and measures the team by operations that went ahead, not by boilers serviced.

3How it solves it from what customers buy, down to what must keep running

Eli draws one page. At the top is what customers buy: express in 30 minutes, a one-hour slot, and from May a weekly office restock for Korenwolf. Below that is the work that delivers it: order, pay, pick, dispatch, deliver. At the bottom is what IT operations keeps running for each step. On the side are the two numbers he'll judge his work by: a four-hour outage ≈ €60k of lost orders, and King's Day 2025, when checkout timed out for 40 minutes.

Then he checks the page against ITIL's four dimensions. Every service needs people (who runs it), information and technology (what it runs on), partners and suppliers (the PSP, StockPilot, the cloud) and a value stream (the steps from order to doorstep). His page had the technology, but it was missing the people and the partners.

  1. Write down what customers buy

    In their words: “groceries in 30 minutes”, not “the order API”.

  2. Draw the steps that deliver it

    Order, pay, pick, dispatch, deliver: the value stream.

  3. List what each step runs on

    Apps, services, devices, networks, vendors.

  4. Put a price on downtime

    Euros, orders or angry customers per hour, even roughly.

  5. Check the four dimensions

    People, technology, partners, value streams: which is missing?

  6. Trace last week's work up the page

    Anything that doesn't trace up needs a reason.

Vocabulary for the exam

Service
A way of delivering value to customers: they get an outcome without owning the costs and risks.
Value
What the customer gets out of it: groceries on time, not uptime.
Outcome
The result the customer wants. An output is what we produce on the way.
Service value system
ITIL 4's picture of how demand becomes value: principles, governance, the value chain, practices, improvement.
Four dimensions
People and organisation, information and technology, partners and suppliers, value streams and processes.
IT operations
The day-to-day work of keeping services running, and restoring them when they stop.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Starting from the servers

    A list of machines says nothing about which one matters. Fix → start from what customers buy.

  • No price for downtime

    Every fix looks equally urgent. Fix → a rough euro figure per hour is enough.

  • Forgetting people and partners

    The technology is drawn; the person who knows how it works and the vendor's phone number are not. Fix → check all four dimensions.

  • A page nobody reads again

    It's written once and then ignored. Fix → trace last week's work up it every month.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • You've inherited “everything technical”.
  • Your week is full but you can't say what it protected.
  • Before building a catalogue or an on-call rota.
  • Someone asks “why do we need an ops person?”

Skip it when…

  • The team already plans from clear service outcomes.
  • A one-off script with no users.

8Self-check quiz

What is IT operations for, in one sentence?

Keeping the services customers pay for working, and restoring them quickly when they stop.

Where is value created, according to ITIL 4?

With the customer, when they use the service: not in the server room.

Name the four dimensions.

People and organisation; information and technology; partners and suppliers; value streams and processes.

Why put a price on downtime, even a rough one?

So trade-offs (what to fix first, what to spend) can be made in the same units as the business uses.

Which dimension was missing from Eli's first page?

People (it all lived in his head) and partners (vendor contacts were in his inbox).

what would you do?A manager asks you to “just keep the servers up”. What do you ask back?

Which customer outcomes the servers support, and what an hour without each one costs. Then you can say which servers matter most.

try it

Write what your customers buy at the top of a page, the steps that deliver it in the middle, and what you keep running at the bottom. Now take last week's calendar: how many of your jobs trace up the page?

remember!!

Nobody buys uptime. They buy an order that arrives. Start from that, and price the hour it doesn't.
