What Is Connected to What?

TL;DR

A dependency map shows, for each service, the components and other services it needs to work, inside and outside the company. It's what on-call looks at first: if checkout fails, which of the eleven things below it is broken? ITIL calls the information configuration items and their relationships. Keep it per service, small and checked against reality, and the surprises show up before an incident does.

  • Family: knowing what you run
  • Lives in: ops/services/<service>.md, one drawing each
  • Pairs with: the catalogue, deployment diagrams, runbooks

1The situation Larder, 7 April 2026

Eli asks Ben, Cho and Dee to draw what checkout needs in order to work, and draws his own. Four drawings, none alike. Ben has five boxes; Cho draws the payment provider and stops; Dee starts at the app. Eli's has nine, and it misses the address lookup service that every checkout calls. If the team can't agree what checkout depends on, an incident starts with an argument.

  • Each engineer knows their own corner.
  • Outside services (SaaS, DNS, certificates) are the least drawn and the most surprising.
  • Rotterdam will put more load on all of them in three weeks.

2Real-life analogy the stage manager's cue sheet

A theatre's stage manager draws what each part of the show hangs on: the lights on the dimmer rack, the dimmer rack on the mains, the sound on the mixing desk, and the mixing desk on one extension lead under the stage. When the sound dies mid-scene, she doesn't debate it. She sends someone to the lead.

3How it solves it one drawing per critical service, then go and check

Eli takes the four drawings and merges them into one per critical service, starting with checkout. Then he checks it against reality. He reads the network rules and the code that calls outside services, and looks at a week of outgoing traffic. That finds eleven dependencies, and two surprises:

  • The address lookup SaaS is on a free plan, 10,000 calls a day. Amsterdam uses about 9,000. With Rotterdam, the estimate is 14,000. He moves it to a paid plan (€49 a month) on 8 April.
  • The nightly price script runs on VM 2 only: if VM 2 is down at 02:00, tomorrow's prices are yesterday's. He writes it down as a known single point, and it becomes a runbook.

Each map lives next to its service page, and every incident review asks: was the map right?

  1. Pick one critical service

    Checkout first: it pays the bills.

  2. Ask several people to draw it

    The differences are the interesting part.

  3. Merge, then check against reality

    Network rules, outgoing calls, config, contracts.

  4. Mark outside dependencies clearly

    SaaS, DNS, certificates, the cloud provider.

  5. Circle single points and limits

    Free plans, one machine, one person.

  6. Check the map in every incident review

    Was it right? Fix it the same day.

Vocabulary for the exam

Dependency
Something a service needs in order to work.
Configuration item (CI)
ITIL's name for a thing you track: a server, an app, a contract, a certificate.
Relationship
How two CIs are linked: runs on, calls, needs.
CMDB
A configuration management database. Useful when maintained, misleading when not.
Single point of failure
One part whose failure stops the whole service.
Hidden dependency
One nobody drew: found by reading traffic, or by an incident.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Only drawing what you built

    SaaS, DNS and certificates are left out, and they're the ones that surprise you. Fix → list outside dependencies first.

  • A giant map of everything

    Too big to read in an incident. Fix → one small map per critical service.

  • Trusting the drawing

    It shows what people believe. Fix → check it against traffic, rules and contracts.

  • A CMDB nobody maintains

    Wrong data is worse than none. Fix → keep it next to the service and fix it in reviews.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Engineers disagree about what a service needs.
  • Before a big change in load (a new city).
  • Incidents keep finding surprises.
  • You use several SaaS services on a critical path.

Skip it when…

  • A tiny service with no outside calls.
  • You already have accurate tracing that draws it for you: check it, don't redraw it.

8Self-check quiz

What does a dependency map show?

For one service: everything it needs to work, ours and outside, and what happens if each one fails.

How did Eli find the address lookup limit?

By checking the drawing against reality: outgoing calls and the vendor's plan, not by asking people.

Why one small map per service instead of one big one?

It can be read in an incident, and the owner can keep it right.

What is a configuration item?

Anything you track because it affects a service: server, app, contract, certificate.

When should the map be checked again?

In every incident review, and before big load changes.

what would you do?Checkout fails at 19:00. The on-call engineer suspects the database, but it looks healthy. What do they do next?

Open checkout's dependency map and go down the list: PSP, address lookup, DNS, certificate. Check each one's status instead of debating the database.

try it

Ask three colleagues to draw what your most important service needs, separately. Compare. Then check the merged drawing against network rules and outgoing calls. What did nobody draw?

remember!!

If the team draws it four different ways, the incident will start with an argument. Draw it once, then check it against reality.
