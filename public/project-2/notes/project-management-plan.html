What's the Plan?

TL;DR

The project management plan is the one plan everyone works from. Its job is integration: making the parts (what, when, how much, who, what could go wrong, how we run it) agree with each other. Seven good workstream plans are not a project plan until someone has laid them side by side and fixed the places where they clash. Keep it as short as the project allows; a plan nobody reads integrates nothing.

  • Family: one plan
  • Lives in: page 4 of the project file, plus the parts it points to
  • Pairs with: the whole-project map, baselines, tailoring

1The situation Larder, 20 February 2026

The plan Sanne promised for 20 February exists, in seven pieces: each lead planned their own workstream on 4 February and has refined it since. Each is sensible. Then she lays them side by side on the meeting-room table. The fit-out ends on 3 April. Stocking runs 6–17 April. The first pickers start on 13 April: a week after the stock they are meant to put on the shelves. And nobody's plan has a line for training on the handhelds.

  • Each plan is right; together they don't fit.
  • The clashes sit between workstreams, where nobody looks.
  • There is no single thing to point at and call “the plan”.

2Real-life analogy the conductor's score

In an orchestra every musician has their part. The conductor has the full score: all the parts on one page, bar by bar. It isn't more music. It is the same music lined up, so the conductor can see that the brass come in two bars before the drums, and fix it in rehearsal, not in the concert.

3How it solves it lay the parts side by side, fix the clashes, write one page

Sanne and the leads spend an hour on cross-checks (listed under “going deeper”). Three fail. Joost moves the first picker group to 30 March, so they train at Noord and are on site from 6 April. Handheld training gets an owner (Tomás). And the plan has no time or money set aside for its top risks: the reserve is €6k. Sanne says so in writing; it is the plan's weakest point. Then she writes one page: the goal, five milestones, the seven workstreams with their leads and next date, the money by workstream, the top risks, and pointers to the six parts. Joost gets it on the 20th, as promised; it goes to the steering group on the 27th.

  1. Let each workstream plan its own work

    They have the facts.

  2. Lay the plans side by side

    On a wall or a table.

  3. Run the cross-checks

    Where does one plan need what another doesn't give?

  4. Fix each clash with the two leads involved

    Not by yourself.

  5. Write the one page

    And point to the parts.

  6. Say plainly what is still weak

    A plan is a claim; be honest about it.

Vocabulary for the exam

Project management plan
The document that says how the project will be carried out, monitored and closed.
Integration
Making the parts of a project work as one.
Subsidiary plan
A part of the plan for one area: scope, schedule, cost, risk and so on.
Milestone chart
The plan as a few key dates.
Assumption
Something the plan treats as true without proof.
Reserve
Time or money set aside for what can go wrong.

＋Going deeper integration: the cross-checks that turn seven plans into one

“Integrate the plans” is the least concrete instruction in project management. This chapter makes it concrete: six cross-checks you can run in an hour, what Rotterdam's three failures looked like, and how to keep the plan integrated after the first day.

1Six cross-checks

Integration means asking of every pair of parts: does one need something the other doesn't give? Six pairs catch most problems.

  • Scope × schedule. Is there time for every must-have before its milestone?
  • Scope × money. Is every must-have paid for? Is anything paid for that isn't a must-have?
  • Schedule × people. Are the people there, and trained, on the day their work starts?
  • Schedule × sourcing. Do long-lead orders arrive before they are needed?
  • Risk × reserve. Is there time and money set aside for the top risks?
  • Everything × the charter. Does the whole still fit the limits and serve the benefit?

2Rotterdam's three failures

  1. Schedule × people.

    Pickers started a week after stocking. Fixed by moving the first group of 15 to 30 March. Cost: one extra week of wages, about €9k, found inside the people budget by starting the second group a week later.

  2. Schedule × sourcing.

    The handhelds were to be ordered “in March” with a three-week delivery; training needs them on 30 March. Order moved to 23 February.

  3. Risk × reserve.

    Not fixed, only made visible. The reserve is €6k and the schedule has no spare week. Sanne writes it on the page as the weak point and tells the steering group what she would need: either €15k more or a decision, now, on which must-haves would go first.

Notice what the checks did not find: there was no line for store IT and internet, so nothing clashed with it. Cross-checks compare the plans you have. They can't see a plan that is missing (the issue log, later in this volume).

3Keeping it integrated

A plan is integrated on the day it is written and starts drifting the day after. Three habits keep it together.

  • One owner for the page. Leads change their own plans; only Sanne changes the page, and she changes it when a milestone, the money or a must-have moves.
  • The weekly meeting is an integration meeting. Thursdays at 09:30 each lead says what they need from another lead in the next two weeks. That sentence is a cross-check, spoken.
  • Changes go through one door. A change is checked against all the parts before it is approved (change control, later in this volume).

A larger project would keep each part as its own document (the older editions call them subsidiary plans: scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, communications, risk, procurement, stakeholder). The logic is the same; only the weight differs.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

one team

  • A list of what, by when, by whom.
  • One conversation to check it hangs together.
  • Kept on a board.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • A plan on a page, pointing to six short parts.
  • Cross-checks with the leads.
  • Updated when a milestone, money or scope moves.

Heavy

large, contracted work

  • A full plan with subsidiary plans.
  • Formal review and approval.
  • Version control and change control on every part.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Stapling

    Seven plans in one folder called “the plan”. Fix → cross-check, then write one page.

  • The project manager rewrites the parts

    Leads stop owning them. Fix → fix clashes with the leads.

  • A plan with no weak point

    Nobody believes it. Fix → say what is fragile.

  • Written once

    It drifts. Fix → one owner; update on real change.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • After the workstreams have planned.
  • Before asking for a baseline.
  • When hand-offs keep failing.
  • When someone asks “where's the plan?”

Skip it when…

  • A single small team: the board is the plan.

8Self-check quiz

What is the plan's main job?

Integration: making the parts agree with each other.

Name three cross-checks.

Scope × schedule, schedule × people, risk × reserve (also scope × money, schedule × sourcing, all × charter).

Who fixes a clash between two workstreams?

The two leads, with the project manager.

Ben moves a demo by two days inside his sprint. Does the plan on a page change?

No. It changes only when a milestone, the money or a must-have moves.

The reserve is €6k and there is no spare week. Should the plan hide that?

No. Sanne writes it as the weak point and tells the steering group what would fix it.

try it

Put your project's plans side by side (even as sticky notes). Run two cross-checks: are the people there when the work starts, and do long orders arrive in time? Write down the first clash you find and who must talk to whom.

remember!!

Seven parts are not a score until someone lines them up.
