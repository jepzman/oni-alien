What Did We Learn?

TL;DR

Projects learn expensive things and then forget them. Managing knowledge means two things: using what the organisation already knows when you start, and capturing what you learn in a form the next project will actually meet. Collect lessons as you go (one line a week), sort them once, and turn the good ones into changes to templates, checklists and playbooks. A lesson filed in a report has been written down. It hasn't been learned.

  • Lives in: one line a week; then the playbook and templates

1The situation Larder, 18 May 2026

After Zuid opened in November 2024 there was a meeting, and a document with 38 lessons. It was saved in a folder. Nobody opened it again. In January Sanne learned what went wrong at Zuid by asking Tomás, who told her from memory. Now Rotterdam is open, Utrecht starts in the spring, and Sanne has 41 lines of her own. If she writes a report and files it, Utrecht will ask her from memory too, and she may not be at Larder.

  • Lessons gathered only at the end are half forgotten.
  • A long list in a folder is read by nobody.
  • Knowledge that lives in one head leaves with it.
meanwhile, in Eli’s notebook
  • How Does a New Store Get Its IT? (Eli, vol. 1): Eli turns that lesson into the store IT kit a week later: 34 items in three phases, the line ordered in week one, devices enrolled centrally.

2Real-life analogy notes in the margin of the recipe

In a family recipe book someone has written next to the cake: “less sugar, oven runs hot, ten minutes longer.” The note is on the recipe, so the next baker sees it at the moment it matters. Nobody keeps a separate notebook called “mistakes we made with cakes”, because nobody would read it before baking.

3How it solves it collect weekly, sort once, change the recipe

Since February the Thursday meeting has ended with one question: “anything we'd do differently next time?” One line, the week it happens: 41 lines by May. On 18 May, a week after hypercare, the leads spend ninety minutes sorting them. Twelve become changes to the launch playbook, each written as an instruction, with an owner. Nine become changes to templates and gate checklists. Twenty are noted and dropped: true, but nobody would act on them. Sanne also records what worked, so nobody “improves” it away. The real test comes at Utrecht's kick-off, where the playbook is the agenda.

  1. Start by reading what exists

    And asking who did it last time.

  2. Collect one line a week

    While the reason is fresh.

  3. Sort once, with the people who did the work

    Soon after the end.

  4. Rewrite each keeper as a change to something

    A checklist line, a template, a playbook step.

  5. Give each change an owner and do it

    Before the team scatters.

  6. Use it at the next kick-off

    That is the only proof.

Vocabulary for the exam

Lesson learned
Knowledge gained from doing the work, good or bad, that should change future work.
Lessons register
The running list of lessons during a project.
Explicit knowledge
What can be written down: steps, numbers, templates.
Tacit knowledge
What people know but find hard to write: judgment, feel. Shared by working together.
Retrospective
A regular look back by the team at how the work is going.
Playbook
A reusable description of how to do a recurring kind of project.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small team

  • A retrospective every few weeks.
  • One change at a time.
  • Nothing written beyond the change.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • One line a week.
  • A 90-minute sort at the end.
  • Changes to the playbook and templates, with owners.

Heavy

many projects, a PMO

  • A lessons database with categories.
  • Formal post-project reviews.
  • A PMO that feeds lessons into standards and training.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Only at the end

    Half is forgotten, the rest is polite. Fix → one line a week.

  • A report in a folder

    Zuid's 38 lessons. Fix → change the playbook, the template, the checklist.

  • Only what went wrong

    Good practice gets “improved” away. Fix → record what worked.

  • Blame

    People stop offering lessons. Fix → ask what to change, not who was at fault.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • From the first week, a line at a time.
  • At every phase gate and at the end.
  • At every kick-off: read the last project's lessons.
  • When the same mistake appears twice.

Skip it when…

  • A true one-off that nobody will ever repeat (rare).

8Self-check quiz

What are the two halves of managing knowledge?

Using what is already known, and capturing what is learned.

Why one line a week?

At the end, half is forgotten and the reasons are gone.

What makes a lesson “learned”?

Something the next project uses has changed because of it.

A lead writes: “communication could have been better.” What does Sanne do with it?

Ask what, exactly, they would change. If nobody can name a change, it goes on the dropped pile.

How will Sanne know the lessons worked?

Utrecht's kick-off uses the playbook, and Utrecht doesn't repeat Rotterdam's mistakes.

try it

At your next team meeting ask one question: “anything we'd do differently next time?” Write one line. Then pick one lesson you already have and turn it into a change to a checklist or template today.

remember!!

Write the lesson on the recipe, not in a drawer.
