a.k.a. “project governance”: the sponsor, the steering group and decision levels

Who Decides What?

TL;DR

Governance is the answer to three questions: who decides what, how fast, and how do they find out a decision is needed. A small project needs a sponsor, a steering group that meets on a rhythm, and a few written limits that send decisions up. Agree it before the first hard decision: in the middle of one, every rule looks like a move in the argument.

  • Family: starting
  • Lives in: one page, next to the charter
  • Pairs with: decision rights, the role card, escalation

1The situation Larder, 27 February 2026

Should the Rotterdam store open on Sundays from day one? The question has been to four meetings in two weeks. Joost thought Priya decided, because it changes the slots customers see. Priya thought Joost did, because it's his rota. Sanne has it on her list as “open”. Nobody is avoiding it. Nobody knows whose it is. The rota and the adverts are waiting.

  • Decisions without an owner go round in circles.
  • The steering group meets, but nobody knows what it's for.
  • Sanne's three triggers exist, but only between her and Joost.

2Real-life analogy a football club

At a football club the players decide every pass. The coach decides the line-up and the substitutions. The board decides the budget and the transfers. The referee applies rules nobody on the pitch can change. It works because everyone knows it in advance. Nobody phones the board about a throw-in, and no player buys a striker.

3How it solves it four levels, a rhythm, and what goes up

At the steering group of 27 February Sanne brings one page. Four levels: the teams (how the work is done), Sanne (how the plan fits together), the sponsor (the three triggers), the steering group (the launch date, the budget, go / no-go). For each: what it decides and how fast. Two vetoes that already exist at Larder are written in: Marco on spending, Lena on contracts and personal data. The steering group gets a rhythm (every second Friday, 30 minutes) and a rule: papers the day before, decisions in the room. Sunday opening is decided in four minutes: Joost's call, Priya consulted, not at launch.

  1. List the kinds of decision the project will need

    Ten is plenty.

  2. Put each at the lowest level that has the facts and the right

    Most stay with the teams.

  3. Write the limits that send a decision up

    Days, money, scope.

  4. Give the steering group a purpose and a rhythm

    What it decides; when it meets.

  5. Write in the vetoes that already exist

    Finance, legal, safety.

  6. Agree it before you need it

    And put it next to the charter.

Vocabulary for the exam

Governance
The framework of who decides, who oversees and how.
Steering group
The sponsor and senior people who decide what is above the project manager.
Decision authority
The right to make a certain kind of decision.
Threshold
A limit that, when crossed, sends a decision up a level.
Terms of reference
A short statement of what a group is for and how it works.
Veto
The right to stop a decision in one's own area.

tools: one page, a recurring 30-minute meeting, and the habit of asking “whose decision is this?” first.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

one team and its manager

  • “You decide, unless it costs more than X.”
  • A weekly chat with the sponsor.
  • Nothing written beyond that.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Four levels on one page.
  • A fortnightly steering group.
  • Thresholds and vetoes written down.

Heavy

many teams, vendors, public money

  • A project board with formal terms of reference.
  • Stage approvals and audit.
  • Delegated financial authority in writing.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Everything goes to the steering group

    It becomes a bottleneck. Fix → thresholds; most decisions stay low.

  • A steering group that only listens

    Status in, nothing out. Fix → papers before, decisions in the room.

  • Rules invented mid-argument

    They look like tactics. Fix → agree them before the first hard decision.

  • Forgotten vetoes

    Finance or legal stops it late. Fix → write them in from the start.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • At the start, with the charter.
  • When a decision has been to three meetings.
  • When a steering group has no clear purpose.
  • When new parties join.

Skip it when…

  • A sponsor and a team who already decide well together: keep it spoken.

8Self-check quiz

What three questions does governance answer?

Who decides what, how fast, and how they find out a decision is needed.

What does Rotterdam's steering group decide?

The launch date, the total budget, go / no-go and closing.

Who can veto a contract?

Lena.

Ben wants to swap the order of two app tasks inside a sprint. Who decides?

Ben's team. It moves no milestone and costs nothing.

A decision has been discussed in three meetings. What is Sanne's first question in the fourth?

“Whose decision is this?” Then that person decides, or a date is set for them to.

try it

Write down the last decision on your project that took too long. Whose was it? Now list five kinds of decision your project needs and put a name and a speed next to each.
