Can We Change That?

TL;DR

Change is normal. Uncontrolled change is what sinks projects. Change control is a short path every change to the baseline takes: write it down, size its effect on everything (not just the part it touches), have the right person decide, and only then build it into the plan and tell everyone affected. It isn't there to say no. It makes sure every yes is given with the cost in view, and that there is one current version of the promise.

  • Lives in: one change log; the baseline's version number

1The situation Larder, 23–31 March 2026

In the last week of March five requests reach Sanne. Priya: an in-store launch event. Joost: a second cold-room door. Tomás: the opening hours, again. Ben: move substitution approvals out of the Rotterdam launch. And Marco, who has noticed the reserve go from €20k to €1k: “who agreed to all this?” Each request is reasonable. Each asker says it's small. And two changes are already in the plan with nothing to show who approved them.

  • Changes agreed in corridors leave no trace.
  • Each is sized by the person asking, for their part only.
  • Nobody can say which version of the promise is current.
meanwhile, in Ana’s notebook
  • Trade-off Sliders (Ana, vol. 2): on 31 March the Rotterdam sliders put the launch date first and feature scope fourth: approvals go to Amsterdam first, Rotterdam in June.

2Real-life analogy the variation on a building site

A client walks through a half-built house: “could that wall move half a metre?” A good builder doesn't pick up a hammer. They write a variation: what moves, what else it affects (the wiring, the window), what it costs, how many days. The client signs, or doesn't. Only then does the wall move, and the drawings are updated, so the electrician isn't working from the old ones.

3How it solves it one log, one path, the thresholds you already have

Sanne starts a change log and first enters the two changes already made (the fridges, the builder's weekend work), with who approved them. Marco has his answer. Then each new request takes the same short path: a paragraph, the impact on all seven domains, and the decider set by the thresholds from February. The 4G router (€600): hers. The cold-room door (€6k, four days): Joost, who defers it to after opening. The launch event (€8k, three workstreams): Joost rejects it, with a reason. And approvals in Rotterdam: a change to what customers get, so it goes to the steering group, which decides on 31 March: Amsterdam first, Rotterdam in June. The baseline becomes v2, and this time Joost hears it once, in writing, from the right source.

  1. Write the request down

    One paragraph: what and why.

  2. Size it against everything

    Scope, dates, money, people, risk, stakeholders.

  3. Send it to the right decider

    By the thresholds.

  4. Decide: approve, reject or defer

    With the reason.

  5. Build approved changes into the plan and baseline

    New version, dated.

  6. Tell everyone affected

    Including those who asked for something else.

Vocabulary for the exam

Change request
A formal proposal to change the baseline: scope, schedule, cost or how the project is run.
Impact assessment
Working out what a change would do to every part of the project.
Change log
The record of every request and its outcome.
Change control board
The group that decides larger changes; here, the steering group.
Scope creep
Scope growing through small unapproved additions.
Re-baseline
Issuing a new approved baseline after a change.

＋Going deeper the path of a change, the impact check, and saying no well

Change control has a reputation as bureaucracy, earned by processes that are slow and built to refuse. This chapter shows the light version that works: the five steps, the seven-question impact check on a real change, the three possible answers, and what to do about changes that have already happened.

1Five steps, and where they usually break

Every change goes through the same five steps. Most trouble comes from skipping the second or the fifth.

  1. Raise.

    In writing, one paragraph. Anyone may raise one. Writing it down is what stops the corridor yes.

  2. Size.

    The effect on the whole project, not only on the asker's part. This is the step corridor decisions skip.

  3. Route.

    The thresholds decide who decides. Small changes stay with the project manager and take minutes.

  4. Decide.

    Approve, reject or defer. Each with a reason, written in the log.

  5. Implement.

    Update the plan and the baseline; tell everyone affected. A change approved but not communicated is how the electrician ends up with old drawings.

2The seven-question impact check

To size a change, walk it through the seven domains (the weekly scan from Vol. 1, used on one change). For C-03, moving approvals in Rotterdam from launch to June:

In money it is free. Its real cost is in stakeholders: the store team was once told (wrongly, by Sanne) that approvals would be there at launch, then told it was a Should, and now must be told June. That conversation is the price, and it is written into the change so it actually happens.

Its benefit shows in schedule and risk: a week of Ben's team for the blocked must-haves, and one less new thing on opening day. With the date ranked first on the sliders, the steering group took four minutes.

3Three answers, not two

  • Approve. Build it in; issue a new baseline if scope, a milestone or the budget moved.
  • Reject. With the reason, in the log. C-05, the launch event: €8k with €1k of reserve, three workstreams touched, and no arrow to any benefit on the benefits map. Priya didn't enjoy the answer, but she could see it wasn't personal: the same test had parked the tote bags in January.
  • Defer. A real third option, with a date. C-06, the second cold-room door: a good idea that costs four days on a milestone with none to spare. “After opening” is logged and handed over at closing, so it isn't lost.

A rejected or deferred requester can always come back with a smaller version. That is the process working.

4Changes that already happened

Two of Rotterdam's changes were made before there was a log. That is common, and the fix is simple: log them afterwards, with who approved them and when. The log isn't a court. Its job is to make the current promise traceable: the reserve went from €20k to €0.4k through four named decisions, each by the person entitled to make it.

What may not be done afterwards is the decision itself. If a change turns out to have been made by someone without the authority, it goes to the right person now, as an open question, even if the work is done.

On adaptive work the same idea is lighter still: re-ordering a backlog inside a sprint isn't a change request. Moving a shared milestone or the launch scope is. The boundary is the baseline.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

one team, adaptive work

  • The product owner re-orders the backlog.
  • Only changes to the goal or the date are discussed.
  • A note in the log.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • One change log.
  • A seven-line impact check.
  • Thresholds decide who decides; baseline versions.

Heavy

contracts, regulated work

  • Formal change requests and a change control board.
  • Contract variations with price and time.
  • Configuration management of every document.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • The corridor yes

    No trace, no sizing. Fix → write it down first.

  • Sized by the asker

    “It's small.” Fix → walk it through all seven domains.

  • A process built to refuse

    People go round it. Fix → small changes decided in minutes at a low level.

  • Approved, not communicated

    Half the team works to the old plan. Fix → telling people is step five.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • From the day there is a baseline.
  • Whenever someone says “it's only a small change”.
  • When the reserve shrinks and nobody knows why.
  • When two people hold different versions of the plan.

Skip it when…

  • Changes inside a team's own work that touch no milestone, money or promise.
