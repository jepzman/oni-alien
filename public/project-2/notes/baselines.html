What Exactly Did We Promise?

TL;DR

A baseline is the approved version of the plan: what we will deliver, by when, for how much. It is the promise, frozen and dated. Progress is measured against it, and it changes only through an approved change. Without a baseline, “on track” means “close to whatever the plan says this week”. Each part should be signed by the person who owns that promise, not assumed by the project manager.

  • Family: one plan
  • Lives in: the plan, marked “baseline v1” with a date
  • Pairs with: change control, status reports, MoSCoW

1The situation Larder, 9–13 March 2026

This one is Sanne's own mistake. In February she read Grace's roadmap, saw “substitution approvals” in the Now column, and wrote in her plan that Rotterdam would have them at launch. She told Joost. Joost told the store team. On 9 March Grace's release plan shows approvals reaching Rotterdam after Amsterdam. Three people hold three versions of the promise, and Sanne wrote the wrong one. Grace is polite about it. Joost less so.

  • The plan contained a promise its owner never made.
  • Nothing marked which version of the plan was “agreed”.
  • Without that, nobody can say what changed, or when.
meanwhile, in Grace’s notebook
  • MoSCoW and Its Traps (Grace, vol. 2): on 3 March Grace's MoSCoW session cuts the Rotterdam app scope from 14 Musts to 6. Approvals in Rotterdam is a Should.

2Real-life analogy the pencil mark on the door frame

Parents mark a child's height on the door frame, with a date. Next year they measure against the mark, not against what anyone remembers. If the door frame is repainted, they make a new mark on purpose and date it. Nobody quietly moves the old one to make the child look taller.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the project
The mark and its dateBaseline v1, 13 March 2026
Measuring against the markStatus: must-haves done, milestones, spend, all against v1

Quietly moving the markEditing the plan and calling it on track

3How it solves it each owner signs their part; the steering group approves the whole

Sanne corrects the misread the same day, in person, to Joost and the store team, and says it was hers. Then she fixes the cause. Each part of the plan goes to the person who owns that promise. Scope: Grace signs the six app Musts from her session on 3 March (approvals in Rotterdam is a Should, not promised); Joost signs the store and staffing must-haves. Schedule: each lead confirms their milestone. Cost: Marco confirms €380k, of which €335k is already committed. On 13 March the steering group approves the three together as baseline v1. From now on the plan can still change, but the baseline moves only through change control.

  1. Finish integrating the plan

    A baseline of a clashing plan is worthless.

  2. Have each owner sign their part

    Scope, schedule, cost.

  3. Write what is not promised

    The Shoulds and Coulds.

  4. Get the whole approved, with a date and a version

    By the level that owns the limits.

  5. Measure against it

    Not against the latest edit.

  6. Change it only through an approved change

    And number the new version.

Vocabulary for the exam

Baseline
The approved version of a plan, used to measure progress and manage change.
Scope baseline
The agreed description of what will be delivered.
Schedule baseline
The agreed dates.
Cost baseline
The agreed budget over time.
Variance
The difference between the baseline and what is happening.
Re-baseline
Approving a new baseline after a significant, agreed change.

tools: the plan on a page, three signatures, a version number and a date.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small team

  • “This is what we agreed on Monday.”
  • A dated message.
  • Re-agreed when it changes.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Scope, schedule and cost on one page.
  • Each signed by its owner.
  • Approved, versioned, dated.

Heavy

contracts, public money

  • Formal baselines under configuration control.
  • Time-phased budget for earned value.
  • Re-baselining only by the board.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • The project manager writes other people's promises

    As Sanne did. Fix → each owner signs their part.

  • A baseline that moves every week

    Nothing to measure against. Fix → change it only by approved change.

  • Baselining too early

    Before the plan hangs together. Fix → integrate first.

  • Only what is in

    The Shoulds are assumed. Fix → write what is not promised.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Once the plan is integrated.
  • Before the first real status report.
  • When people remember different promises.
  • After a major approved change (re-baseline).

Skip it when…

  • Purely exploratory work: agree a goal and a time box, not a baseline.

8Self-check quiz

What are the three baselines?

Scope, schedule and cost.

Who signed the app scope?

Grace: it's her promise.

What was Sanne's mistake?

She put a promise in her plan (approvals at launch) that its owner had never made, and passed it on.

On 20 March Eli re-orders two of his tasks. Does the baseline change?

No. The plan's detail changes; the baseline (must-haves, milestones, budget) doesn't.

Joost says “just update the baseline to the new date, it's cleaner”. What does Sanne answer?

The baseline moves only through an approved change, so everyone can see that it moved, when and why. Otherwise late becomes invisible.

try it

Write down what your project has promised: what, by when, for how much. Next to each line, write the name of the person who made that promise. Any line with your own name where it should be someone else's? Go and get it signed.

remember!!
