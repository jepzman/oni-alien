Where Are We, Really?

TL;DR

A status report answers four questions: where are we against the baseline, where will we end up if nothing changes (the forecast), what are we doing about it, and what do we need from you. Colours follow written rules, so two people with the same numbers pick the same colour. A report that collects numbers and changes nothing is monitoring without controlling: every variance ends in a decision, even if the decision is “accept it”.

  • Family: running and steering
  • Lives in: one page every Friday; the steering group every second Friday
  • Pairs with: baselines, own the outcome, escalation

1The situation Larder, 27 March 2026

One month to opening. Sanne's Friday page has seven lines and seven colours, and people have come to trust it. Then Marco asks: “why is schedule amber and not red? Would you have called it amber last week?” She starts to answer and stops. The colours are her judgment on the day. There is no rule behind any of them, no line showing whether things are improving, and no statement of where the project will end up.

  • A colour without a rule can't be checked or compared.
  • The page shows today, not the direction.
  • It says how things are, not what will happen or what to do.

2Real-life analogy the sat-nav

A sat-nav shows three things: where you are on the planned route, how far is left, and when you'll arrive, recalculated as you drive. When traffic builds it tells you at once and offers another route. It never says “making good progress”, and it never waits until you've missed the ferry to mention the jam.

3How it solves it position, forecast, action, ask — and colours with rules

For the steering group of 27 March Sanne rebuilds the page. Position: a small chart of must-haves done against the plan line (49; the plan said 52), the milestones against baseline, money committed (€355.5k of €380k). Forecast: at the recent rate of four a week, 65 of 71 by go / no-go. Action: Ben and Eli pair on the six blocked items; nothing new enters the launch list after 3 April. Ask: two decisions. And each colour now follows a written rule (under “going deeper”). By those rules the overall status is amber. Marco reads the rules twice and says: “so next week I can check you.”

  1. Measure against the baseline

    Done, dates, money.

  2. Show the trend

    This week against the last few.

  3. Forecast the end

    At the current rate, where do we land?

  4. Apply the colour rules

    Don't choose the colour; derive it.

  5. Say what you are doing

    For each variance.

  6. Say what you need, from whom, by when

    Or say plainly that you need nothing.

Vocabulary for the exam

Monitoring
Collecting and comparing: where are we against the plan?
Controlling
Deciding and acting on what monitoring shows.
Variance
The gap between baseline and actual.
Forecast
Where the project will end up if the current trend continues.
Trend
The direction over several periods.
RAG status
Red, amber, green: a summary colour, useful only with rules.
Corrective action
A step taken to bring performance back to the plan.

＋Going deeper colour rules, forecasting, and the watermelon

Most status reports fail in the same three ways: the colour is an opinion, there is no forecast, and nothing happens as a result. This chapter gives a working fix for each, using Rotterdam's real numbers, and ends with how to spot a report that is green on the outside and red inside.

1Write the colour rules down

A colour is a summary. It is only useful if two people with the same numbers would choose the same one. So write the rule for each line once, agree it with the steering group, and from then on derive the colour.

Good rules have three properties. They use numbers from the baseline (days, must-haves, euros). They are tied to authority: Rotterdam's red line for schedule is “more than three days late”, the same three days that send a decision to the sponsor. And they say what amber means, which is usually “off the baseline, with a recovery plan we believe”.

The overall colour is the worst line, not an average. One red domain can sink a project that is green in the other six.

2Forecast: where will we land?

Position tells you about the past. The steering group cares about the end. The simplest honest forecast needs two numbers: how much is left and how fast you are really going.

On 27 March: 71 − 49 = 22 left. The last three weeks finished 7, 4 and 4, and the pace has settled at about 4 a week. 22 ÷ 4 = 5½ weeks. There are 4 before go / no-go. So at this rate Rotterdam reaches 65 of 71, six short.

That sum does three useful things. It turns “a bit behind” into a number. It shows the size of the fix: the rate must reach 5½ a week, or six must-haves must leave the list. And it can be checked next week: did the rate rise? (It did: moving approvals to June freed Ben's team, and the next three weeks finished 6, 5 and 9.)

Always state the assumption (“at the average of the last three weeks”). Vol. 4 does the same sum for money, where it is called earned value.

3Monitoring without controlling

A report that measures and then stops is only half the process. For every line that is off the baseline, one of four things must be written:

  • Correct it: an action that brings it back (pairing on the blocked items).
  • Prevent it: an action so it doesn't get worse (nothing new after 3 April).
  • Change the baseline: through change control, because the promise itself should move (approvals to June).
  • Accept it: say so, with the reason and who agreed.

If none of the four is written, nobody is controlling anything.

4Spotting a watermelon

A watermelon report is green outside and red inside. Five signs, from the reader's side:

  • Colours with no numbers next to them.
  • No trend: you can't tell if this week is better or worse.
  • Progress as a percentage of effort (“80 % done”), not finished things.
  • Green for weeks, then red with no amber in between.
  • No “needed from you”, ever. Real projects need decisions.

And one from the writer's side: if you would be uncomfortable seeing the reader check it, it isn't finished.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small team

  • Say it weekly: done, left, will we make it, what I need.
  • No colours.
  • Five minutes.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • One page against the baseline.
  • A trend chart and a simple forecast.
  • Colour rules, actions and asks.

Heavy

large or contracted work

  • Earned value and schedule analysis.
  • Formal variance thresholds and reports.
  • Independent assurance of the numbers.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Colours by mood

    Can't be checked. Fix → written rules.

  • No forecast

    “Slightly behind” for weeks. Fix → left ÷ rate, with the assumption.

  • Monitoring only

    Numbers, no decisions. Fix → correct, prevent, change or accept.

  • Averaging

    Six greens hide one red. Fix → overall = the worst line.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every week once there is a baseline.
  • Before every steering group.
  • When someone asks “are we on track?”
  • When a status has been the same colour for a month.

Skip it when…

  • Never the questions. The page can shrink to four sentences.

try it

Take your last status report. For each colour, write the rule that would make someone else choose the same colour from the same numbers. Then add one sum: what's left ÷ your real rate. Does the answer fit in the time you have?
