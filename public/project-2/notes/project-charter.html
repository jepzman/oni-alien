Should This Project Exist?

TL;DR

A project charter is a short document, signed by the sponsor, that says the project exists: why, for what benefit, what is in and out, within which limits, who is the sponsor and who is the project manager, and what “done” means. It is permission to start and to spend, not a plan. A project without one runs on assumptions that differ from person to person. If yours has none, write it now.

1The situation Larder, 19 February 2026

Rotterdam has been “a project” since the Series B closed in September. On her way to a first plan, Sanne asks five people what it is for. Joost: a store. Femke: a date for the investors. Marco: proof that a city doesn't cost five months. Priya: more orders. Ana: a test of “a city is configuration”. All true, none written down, none signed. She has six weeks of notes (a value chain, a role card, a scan) and no single page that authorises any of it.

  • No agreed goal means every argument starts from zero.
  • Sanne spends €380k on the strength of a hallway conversation.
  • Nobody has said what “finished” is.

2Real-life analogy the ship's papers

Before a ship leaves port, the owner signs one page: where it is going and why, what it carries, who the captain is, what the voyage may cost. The captain keeps it in the cabin. It is not the route: the captain works that out. It is the permission to sail, and the thing both of them point to when a storm forces a choice.

3How it solves it one page, six boxes, two signatures

Sanne already has most of the content. She folds the value chain, the role card and the limits from her scan into one page with six boxes: why, benefits, in and out, limits, people, done when. The new parts are “out” (no new app features for Rotterdam) and “done when” (hypercare exit and handover to the store manager). Joost reads it in ten minutes, changes one word, and signs. Femke signs too. It becomes page 1 of the project file.

  1. Write why, in two lines

    Linked to the strategy.

  2. State the benefits with numbers and dates

    From the value chain.

  3. Say what is in, and what is out

    “Out” prevents most arguments.

  4. List the limits

    Date, money, people, rules.

  5. Name the sponsor, the project manager and their authority

    Including what goes up.

  6. Define “done”, and get it signed

    By the person who pays.

Vocabulary for the exam

Project charter
The document that formally authorises a project and gives the project manager authority.
Business case
The reasoning that the investment is worth making.
Sponsor
The person who provides resources and answers for the project in the business.
Scope (high level)
What the project will and won't deliver, in a few lines.

WHY        Larder promised investors six cities by end 2027. Rotterdam is the second,
           and the proof that a city opens in weeks, not months (Zuid took five).
BENEFITS   500 orders a day by end May                       owner: Joost
           90 % express on time by the end of the summer     owner: Joost
           a launch playbook: next city in ≤ 8 weeks         owner: Femke
IN         store fitted, stocked and staffed · Rotterdam set up in the platform and apps
           · ~40 pickers and couriers trained · launch marketing · two weeks of hypercare
OUT        new app features for Rotterdam · Larder for Business · any change to Amsterdam
LIMITS     opening 27 April 2026 (lease) · budget €380k (reserve €6k) · max 4 new hires
PEOPLE     sponsor Joost · project manager Sanne · steering: Femke, Marco, Priya, Joost

＋Going deeper what belongs in a charter, and what to do when you arrive late

A charter looks too simple to need a longer chapter. The trouble is in three places: it gets confused with the business case and the plan, it gets written too long, and on most real projects it gets written late or not at all. This chapter takes those in turn.

1Business case, charter, plan

Three documents, three questions, three owners.

  • The business case asks is this worth doing? It compares benefits with costs and risks. The sponsor owns it, and it stays alive: if the answer becomes “no”, the project should stop.
  • The charter says you may start. It takes the business case's conclusion, adds the goal, the limits and the named project manager, and is signed by the sponsor.
  • The plan says here is how. The project manager owns it, the team makes it, and it changes often.

At Larder the business case was never written as a document: it is the Series B condition and the lease. Sanne puts those two facts in the “why” box, so the charter doesn't pretend to an analysis that wasn't done.

2The test of each line: would we argue about it in April?

A charter should fit on one page. To decide what goes in, Sanne uses one test: is this something people might disagree about when the pressure is on?

  • Out of scope passes the test easily. In March someone will ask for “just one” Rotterdam feature. The line “OUT: new app features for Rotterdam” ends that conversation in a sentence.
  • Done when passes too. Without it, the project manager is still answering store questions in August.
  • Authority passes: the three triggers.
  • A list of tasks, a schedule, the names of every team member: these fail. They belong in the plan, and they change.

3Arriving late: the retro-charter

Textbooks put the charter before the first day of work. Sanne arrived in week 16 of something already moving, which is how most project managers meet their projects. A late charter is still worth writing, with three adjustments.

  1. Write what is true now.

    Not what should have been agreed in September. If the budget is already €380k with €6k of reserve, say so.

  2. Expect one real disagreement.

    Writing it down will surface one. Here it was “done when”: Joost assumed Sanne would stay on Rotterdam through the summer; Femke needed her on Utrecht. Better found in February than in May.

  3. Get it signed anyway.

    The signature is the point. It turns “everyone knows” into “we agreed”.

The same page is used again at the start of each phase, in a smaller form: before stocking begins, someone must say “yes, go” (the gates, two notes on).

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a two-week internal job

  • Three lines in a message: why, done when, who decides.
  • A thumbs-up from the sponsor.
  • No document.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • One page, six boxes.
  • Signed by the sponsor.
  • Page 1 of the project file.

Heavy

a large or contracted project

  • A full business case first.
  • A multi-page charter with assumptions and risks.
  • Approval by a board; funding released in stages.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • No charter

    “Everyone knows why.” Fix → write it, even late.

  • A ten-page charter

    It becomes a plan and goes stale. Fix → one page; the test is “would we argue about it?”.

  • Written by the project manager, signed by nobody

    No authority behind it. Fix → the sponsor signs.

  • No “out”, no “done when”

    Scope creeps and the project never ends. Fix → write both.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Before any project starts.
  • When you inherit a project without one.
  • At the start of each phase, in small.
  • When people disagree about the goal.

Skip it when…

  • Never. For tiny work it is three lines.

8Self-check quiz

Who signs the charter?

The sponsor.

Charter or plan: “the builder hands over on 3 April”?

Plan. It is a detail of how, and it may change.

Why write “out of scope”?

So that a later request can be answered by pointing at an agreed line.

In March Priya asks for a Rotterdam-only loyalty feature “since we're launching anyway”. What does Sanne do?

Point to OUT in the charter. If Priya still wants it, it is a change for the sponsor to decide, not a favour.

Sanne joined in week 16. Is it too late for a charter?

No. Write what is true now and get it signed; expect it to surface one disagreement.

try it

Write your project's charter in six boxes: why, benefits, in / out, limits, people, done when. Which box can't you fill? Take that question to your sponsor this week.
