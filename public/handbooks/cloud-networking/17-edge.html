The edge: CDNs, WAF and DDoS protection

Your users are spread out; your servers are in one region. Between them, cloud providers run hundreds of edge locations that can answer requests close to users, filter out bad ones, and soak up floods before they reach you. This chapter shows how to put that edge to work: what to cache and what never to cache, how a web application firewall decides, and what protects you when someone tries to knock your service over.

By the end you can

  • Explain how a CDN serves content from the edge and when it goes back to the origin.
  • Set cache keys and Cache-Control headers safely, including for dynamic pages.
  • Lock the origin so only the CDN can reach it.
  • Write WAF rules, including rate limits, and explain layered DDoS protection.

Before you start

  • HTTP, headers and TLS (Chapter 6).
  • Load balancers and their security groups (Chapter 16).

At Larder

Friday, 17:00. Larder's marketing team sends a newsletter with a discount to 400,000 subscribers. Within three minutes traffic to the web shop is twelve times normal. The app servers hit 100% CPU, autoscaling can't start new ones fast enough, and checkout, the one page that earns money, slows to a crawl. In the post-incident review, one number stands out: more than 80% of those requests were for the same homepage, the same product pages and the same images.

Larder's servers spent the evening building identical answers over and over. Section 17.3 shows what Eli changed before the next newsletter.

17.1The big picture

The edge is the provider's network of small sites, called points of presence (PoPs), in cities around the world, close to users and to their internet providers. Requests reach the nearest one, either through DNS that answers with a nearby address or through anycast, where the same address is announced from every site and the internet's routing delivers each user to the closest. At the edge, three jobs get done:

  • Cache: a content delivery network (CDN) keeps copies of responses and serves them without asking your servers.
  • Filter: a web application firewall (WAF) inspects HTTP requests and blocks bad ones.
  • Absorb: DDoS protection soaks up floods of traffic across the whole edge, far from your region.

17.2How a CDN decides

For each request, the edge builds a cache key, by default the host and path, optionally plus chosen query parameters, headers or cookies, and looks it up. A hit is answered at once, often in a few milliseconds. A miss goes to the origin, your load balancer or storage bucket, and the response is stored for next time, if the response allows it.

The origin decides what may be cached, and for how long, with the Cache-Control response header. The CDN's own settings can set minimums, maximums and defaults on top.

Table 17.1 · Cache-Control values you'll use
ValueMeansUse for
public, max-age=31536000, immutableAnyone may cache for a year; it will never changeFiles with a version in the name: app.3f9c2e.js, images by content hash
public, s-maxage=60, max-age=0Shared caches (the CDN) for 60 s; browsers must recheckPages that are the same for everyone and may be a minute old
stale-while-revalidate=30Serve the old copy for up to 30 s while fetching a fresh oneSmoothing refreshes of popular pages
privateOnly the user's browser may cache it, never the CDNPersonalised pages: account, basket
no-storeNobody may keep a copyCheckout, payment responses, anything sensitive

Two ways to update cached content: versioned names (a new file name for each version, so nothing ever needs removing, and old pages keep working) or invalidation (asking the CDN to drop paths). Prefer versioned names for assets; keep invalidation for mistakes and for HTML.

17.3Caching dynamic pages, safely

Static files are the easy part. The bigger win is often pages that are dynamic but the same for everyone for a short while: the homepage, category pages, a product page's price and stock for a store. Caching those for even 30 to 60 seconds, called micro-caching, turns thousands of identical requests per minute into one.

At Larder: solved

Eli put CloudFront in front of the ALB with three cache behaviours. Versioned assets: one year. Anonymous catalogue and homepage requests, keyed on path plus the store query parameter: s-maxage=60 with stale-while-revalidate. Everything under /account, /basket and /checkout: not cached at all, every request passed to the origin. An origin shield in Frankfurt sat between the edges and the ALB.

At the next newsletter, traffic at the edge was again twelve times normal. Requests reaching the ALB rose by less than double; the cache hit ratio for the catalogue was 93%. Checkout stayed fast, because it no longer shared servers with a stampede of identical homepage requests.

Watch out: never cache someone's page for someone else

The most damaging CDN bug is caching a personalised response under a key that doesn't include whatever makes it personal. Then the next visitor gets the first visitor's basket, name or address. Rules that prevent it: personalised paths are never cached; the origin sends private or no-store on anything personal; and if a response depends on a cookie or header, that cookie or header is either in the cache key or the response isn't cached. Test it by logging in as two users.

A little arithmetic

Cache hit ratio is what decides your origin's load. At 2,000 requests a second, a 90% hit ratio sends 200 to the origin; 99% sends 20. Every extra point of hit ratio near the top halves the remaining load more than once. That's why the cache key matters so much: adding a needless header or a tracking parameter to the key splits one cached object into thousands and sends the hit ratio down.

17.4Locking the origin

A CDN protects your origin only if attackers can't go around it. If the ALB still accepts traffic from the whole internet, anyone who finds its address can skip the cache and the WAF. Lock it, in one of these ways:

  • Private origin. CloudFront can reach an internal ALB in private subnets through a VPC origin; the load balancer then has no public address at all. The strongest option where it's available.
  • Allow only the CDN's addresses. Use AWS's managed prefix list for CloudFront's origin-facing addresses as the only source in the ALB's security group (Chapter 9).
  • A secret header. Have the CDN add a custom header with a secret value, and have the ALB reject requests without it. Combine it with the prefix list, since other CloudFront customers share those addresses.

For storage buckets, use origin access control: the bucket stays private and accepts reads only from your distribution. The lab does this.

17.5Web application firewalls

A WAF inspects each HTTP request against rules and allows, blocks, counts or challenges it. AWS WAF attaches to CloudFront, ALBs and API gateways. Rules come in a few kinds:

Table 17.2 · Kinds of WAF rule
KindExample
Managed rule groupsCommon exploits, known bad inputs, SQL injection, known malicious IP reputation, bot control. Maintained by the provider or vendors.
Rate-based rulesBlock an IP (or a key such as a header) that sends more than 300 requests in 5 minutes to /api/login.
Match rulesBlock requests whose path, header, body or query matches a pattern.
IP sets and geographyAllow an admin path only from the office; block countries you don't serve (with care).

Start every new rule in count mode. Watch what it would have blocked for a few days, fix false positives, then switch it to block. A WAF that blocks real customers is an outage you caused yourself.

A week after the newsletter, Larder's login endpoint got about 40,000 attempts an hour from around 600 addresses: credential stuffing, trying passwords leaked from other sites. A rate-based rule on /api/login per IP, plus the managed account-takeover rules, cut it to a trickle within the hour. The WAF didn't need to know which passwords were real; it only had to notice that no human logs in 70 times in five minutes.

17.6DDoS protection

A distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack tries to make a service unavailable by flooding it from many machines at once. Attacks come at different layers, and each layer has a different defence:

Table 17.3 · DDoS attacks and their defences
LayerAttackDefence
3 and 4 (network)Floods of UDP or SYN packets, reflection attacks, hundreds of gigabitsThe provider's edge absorbs it. AWS Shield Standard is on for everyone, free. Anycast spreads the flood across many sites.
7 (application)Floods of real-looking HTTP requests to expensive pagesCaching (most requests never reach you), WAF rate limits, bot rules, autoscaling.
Your billScaling up to serve an attackAWS Shield Advanced adds a response team and cost protection for attack-related scaling (about $3,000 a month).

Key idea

Push every public request through the edge. A flood absorbed in fifty cities is a non-event; the same flood aimed at one load balancer in one region is an outage.

17.8Lab: watch a cache miss become a hit

Lab 17 · CloudFront, a private bucket and cache headers

You'll build a private S3 bucket with one page, served only through a CloudFront distribution with origin access control, and watch misses, hits and invalidation. You need the AWS CLI and Terraform. Cost: within CloudFront's and S3's free tiers for this traffic. Creating and deleting a distribution takes several minutes each.

main.tfTerraform ≥ 1.5, AWS provider 5.x

provider "aws" { region = "eu-central-1" }

resource "aws_s3_bucket" "site" {
  bucket_prefix = "handbook-lab17-"
  force_destroy = true
}
resource "aws_s3_object" "index" {
  bucket        = aws_s3_bucket.site.id
  key           = "index.html"
  content       = "<h1>Hello from the origin, version 1</h1>"
  content_type  = "text/html"
  cache_control = "public, max-age=300"     # 17.2 · the origin decides
}

# 17.4 · the bucket stays private; only this distribution may read it
resource "aws_cloudfront_origin_access_control" "oac" {
  name                              = "handbook-lab17"
  origin_access_control_origin_type = "s3"
  signing_behavior                  = "always"
  signing_protocol                  = "sigv4"
}

resource "aws_cloudfront_distribution" "cdn" {
  enabled             = true
  default_root_object = "index.html"
  origin {
    domain_name              = aws_s3_bucket.site.bucket_regional_domain_name
    origin_id                = "s3"
    origin_access_control_id = aws_cloudfront_origin_access_control.oac.id
  }
  default_cache_behavior {
    target_origin_id       = "s3"
    viewer_protocol_policy = "redirect-to-https"
    allowed_methods        = ["GET", "HEAD"]
    cached_methods         = ["GET", "HEAD"]
    cache_policy_id        = "658327ea-f89d-4fab-a63d-7e88639e58f6"  # managed "CachingOptimized"
  }
  restrictions {
    geo_restriction { restriction_type = "none" }
  }
  viewer_certificate { cloudfront_default_certificate = true }
}

resource "aws_s3_bucket_policy" "site" {
  bucket = aws_s3_bucket.site.id
  policy = jsonencode({
    Version = "2012-10-17"
    Statement = [{
      Effect    = "Allow"
      Principal = { Service = "cloudfront.amazonaws.com" }
      Action    = "s3:GetObject"
      Resource  = "${aws_s3_bucket.site.arn}/*"
      Condition = { StringEquals = { "AWS:SourceArn" = aws_cloudfront_distribution.cdn.arn } }
    }]
  })
}

output "url" { value = "https://${aws_cloudfront_distribution.cdn.domain_name}/" }
output "id"  { value = aws_cloudfront_distribution.cdn.id }
  1. Create it with terraform init and terraform apply; the distribution takes several minutes to deploy.
  2. Miss, then hit. Run this twice: 
    curl -sI $(terraform output -raw url) | grep -iE '^(x-cache|age|x-amz-cf-pop|cache-control)'
    The first says Miss from cloudfront, the second Hit from cloudfront with an Age header. x-amz-cf-pop names the edge location that answered, a code for a city near you.
  3. Try to go around it. Open the bucket's own URL (https://<bucket>.s3.eu-central-1.amazonaws.com/index.html): AccessDenied. Only the CDN can read the origin.
  4. Change the page. Edit content to say “version 2” and apply. The CDN still serves version 1 until max-age runs out. Then invalidate: aws cloudfront create-invalidation --distribution-id $(terraform output -raw id) --paths "/*", wait a minute, and fetch again.
  5. Clean up with terraform destroy. Terraform disables the distribution, waits, then deletes it.

Stretch goal. Attach an AWS WAF web ACL with a rate-based rule of 100 requests per 5 minutes per IP, in count mode, then loop curl 200 times and find the counted requests in the WAF's sampled requests. (A web ACL costs about $5 a month, prorated; delete it afterwards.)

17.9Common mistakes

MistakeWhat happensInstead
Personalised pages cached under a shared keyOne user sees another's data.private/no-store on personal responses; never cache those paths.
Origin open to the internet behind a CDNAttackers bypass the cache and WAF.Private origin, prefix list, or a secret header.
Everything in the cache keyHit ratio collapses; origin load stays high.Key on what really changes the response.
Invalidating on every deploy instead of versioningSlow, partial updates; old pages ask for missing files.Versioned asset names; long TTLs.
New WAF rules straight to blockReal customers blocked.Count first, review, then block.
Public endpoints that skip the edgeThe one path a DDoS can reach directly.Put every public request through the edge.

✓Chapter summary

  • The edge is the provider's sites near users. It caches, filters and absorbs before traffic reaches your region.
  • A CDN looks up a cache key; hits are served at the edge, misses go to the origin, through a shield if you have one.
  • The origin's Cache-Control decides what's cached. Version assets, micro-cache shared pages, never cache personal ones.
  • Lock the origin so only the CDN can reach it.
  • A WAF blocks bad requests with managed rules, rate limits and matches; start in count mode.
  • DDoS defence is layered: the edge absorbs network floods; caching and WAF handle application floods.

Key terms

Edge / point of presence
A provider site near users that runs CDN, WAF and DDoS services.
Anycast
One address announced from many places; routing delivers users to the nearest.
CDN
A network of caches that serves content from the edge.
Origin
The server or bucket the CDN fetches from on a miss.
Cache key
The parts of a request that decide which cached copy answers it.
Origin shield
A regional cache layer between edges and the origin.
Cache hit ratio
The share of requests answered from cache.
WAF
A firewall that inspects HTTP requests.
DDoS
An attack that floods a service from many sources to make it unavailable.

Check your understanding

  1. Which Cache-Control header would you send for app.3f9c2e.js, for the homepage, and for the basket page? apply

    Show answer

    public, max-age=31536000, immutable for the versioned file; something like public, s-maxage=60, max-age=0, stale-while-revalidate=30 for the homepage; private, no-store for the basket.

  2. At 5,000 requests per second, how much does raising the hit ratio from 95% to 99% reduce origin load? apply

    Show answer

    From 250 to 50 requests per second at the origin: a fivefold reduction from a four-point change.

  3. Your site is behind CloudFront, but the ALB's security group allows 443 from 0.0.0.0/0. Why is that a problem, and what are two fixes? explain

    Show answer

    Anyone who finds the ALB's address can bypass the cache, the WAF and the edge's DDoS absorption. Fixes: make the ALB internal and use a VPC origin; or allow only CloudFront's managed prefix list, plus a secret custom header the ALB checks.

  4. Why put a new WAF rule in count mode first? explain

    Show answer

    To see what it would block before it blocks anything: managed rules and patterns can match legitimate requests (unusual inputs, partner integrations), and blocking customers is an outage you caused.

  5. An attacker floods your login page with real HTTPS requests from 10,000 home routers. Which defences help, and which don't? design

    Show answer

    Network-level DDoS protection doesn't help much: these are valid requests. Caching doesn't help either, since login can't be cached. What helps: WAF rate-based rules per IP and per other keys, bot-control and account-takeover managed rules, challenges such as CAPTCHAs, and keeping login on servers separate from checkout so the attack can't starve it.
