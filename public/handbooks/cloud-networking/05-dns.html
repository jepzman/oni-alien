DNS: the internet's address book

Every connection starts with a name, and the Domain Name System turns names into addresses. It's simple to use and surprisingly subtle underneath: answers come from caches, caches keep old answers on purpose, and the same name can mean different things inside and outside your network. “It's always DNS” is a joke engineers tell because it's so often true. After this chapter, you'll know why.

By the end you can

  • Explain how a name is resolved, from your laptop to the authoritative server.
  • Choose the right record type: A, AAAA, CNAME, alias, MX, TXT and others.
  • Plan a DNS change with TTLs so it takes effect when you want.
  • Use dig to find out what a resolver actually answers, and why.

DNS did exactly what it was designed to do. Section 5.8 shows what Eli should have done, and what he does now.

5.1The big picture

Computers connect to IP addresses; people and configuration files use names. The Domain Name System (DNS) maps one to the other. It has to answer billions of questions a day for names owned by millions of organisations, so it's built from three ideas:

  • A hierarchy. Names are a tree, and each part of the tree is run by whoever owns it.
  • Delegation. Nobody knows every answer. Each server knows its own part of the tree and where to find the parts below.
  • Caching. Every answer says how long it may be reused, so most questions never travel far.

5.2Names, zones and delegation

Read a name from right to left. www.larder.example. is the host www, in the domain larder, in the top-level domain example, under the root, the trailing dot that's usually left off.

A zone is a part of the tree managed as one unit, with its own set of authoritative name servers: the servers whose answers for that zone are the official ones. The root zone's servers know only where each top-level domain's servers are. The .example servers know only where each domain's servers are. Larder's servers know the actual records in larder.example. Handing a part of the tree to other servers is delegation, done with NS records in the parent zone.

Registrar vs DNS hostYou register a domain with a registrar, which puts your name servers into the parent zone. You host the zone with a DNS service, such as Route 53. They're often the same company, but don't have to be.

CNAMEs and the zone apex

A CNAME says “this name is an alias for that one”. Cloud load balancers and CDNs give you a name, not fixed addresses, because their addresses change, so you point your own names at theirs with a CNAME. There's one catch: a CNAME can't sit at the zone apex, the bare domain larder.example, because the apex must also hold NS and SOA records and a CNAME can't share a name with anything else.

DNS hosts solve this with alias records: you point the apex at the cloud resource, and the DNS host looks up its current addresses and answers with plain A/AAAA records. On Route 53, alias records to AWS resources are also free to query and need no TTL of their own.

5.4How a name is resolved

Your laptop doesn't walk the tree itself. Its operating system has a small stub resolver that sends the question to a recursive resolver: your ISP's, your company's, a public one like 1.1.1.1, or, in a VPC, the provider's. The recursive resolver does the work and caches everything it learns.

Two things are worth noticing. First, the referrals are cached too: a resolver that has looked up any .example name already knows where the .example servers are, so real lookups usually skip straight to step ④ or answer from cache. Second, your laptop trusts its resolver completely. If the resolver is slow, wrong or unreachable, every connection your laptop makes is slow, wrong or broken.

5.5TTLs and caching

Every record has a TTL (time to live) in seconds. A resolver that gets an answer may reuse it for that long without asking again. Caching is what makes DNS fast and resilient, and it's also why DNS changes aren't instant.

Key idea

When you change a record, the old answer can live on for up to its old TTL. Lower the TTL well before the change, not at the same time.

Some practical TTLs:

  • 3,600 seconds (one hour) or more for records that rarely change: MX, TXT, NS.
  • 60–300 seconds for records you may need to change quickly: endpoints during a migration, failover records.
  • Aliases to cloud load balancers follow the provider's own short TTL (60 seconds for AWS load balancers).

Failed lookups are cached as well. If you query a name before its record exists, resolvers cache the “no such name” answer (NXDOMAIN) for the time set in the zone's SOA record, often 5 to 15 minutes. Creating the record right after won't help those resolvers until that runs out.

Watch out: not everyone respects TTLs

Some programs cache DNS answers themselves, longer than the TTL or forever. Older Java runtimes with a security manager cache successful lookups indefinitely; some HTTP clients resolve once at startup and keep the address; long-lived connections never look up again at all. After any change, keep the old endpoint running until its traffic has dropped to zero.

5.6DNS in the cloud

Each provider runs a recursive resolver inside every VPC, and a DNS hosting service for your zones.

Private zones deserve a word now. A private hosted zone for internal.larder.example is visible only from the VPCs you attach it to, so you can give internal services stable names (db.internal.larder.example) without publishing them. A private zone can even use the same name as a public zone, giving one answer inside and another outside. That's split-horizon DNS, very useful and very confusing, and the subject of Chapter 15 together with resolving names between the cloud and your offices.

Two VPC settingsOn AWS, a VPC's DNS resolution setting turns the resolver on, and DNS hostnames gives instances names. Both must be on for private hosted zones to work. The Chapter 8 lab sets them.

DNS that makes decisions

Cloud DNS services can also choose which answer to give: by weight (send 10% of lookups to a new stack), by latency (the closest region), by the user's location, or by health (stop returning an endpoint whose health check fails). These routing policies are a cheap and powerful way to shift traffic between regions. They inherit every caching caveat above, so they shift traffic over minutes, not seconds. You'll use them for failover between regions in the capstone, Chapter 25.

5.7Worked example: reading a dig answer

dig is the standard tool for asking DNS questions. Here's what Eli sees when he checks Larder's API name from his laptop:

$ dig api.larder.example

;; ->>HEADER<<- opcode: QUERY, status: NOERROR, id: 4127
;; flags: qr rd ra; QUERY: 1, ANSWER: 3, AUTHORITY: 0, ADDITIONAL: 1

;; ANSWER SECTION:
api.larder.example.   247   IN  CNAME  api-prod-1234.eu-central-1.elb.amazonaws.com.
api-prod-1234.eu-central-1.elb.amazonaws.com. 47 IN A 198.51.100.31
api-prod-1234.eu-central-1.elb.amazonaws.com. 47 IN A 198.51.100.77

;; Query time: 3 msec
;; SERVER: 192.168.1.1#53(192.168.1.1)
Table 5.3 · What each part tells you
PartMeaning
status: NOERRORThe name exists. Other statuses: NXDOMAIN (no such name), SERVFAIL (the resolver couldn't get an answer).
flags: … ra, no aaThe answer came from a recursive resolver (“recursion available”), not straight from an authoritative server.
247 and 47Seconds left in the resolver's cache. The CNAME was cached 53 seconds ago with a TTL of 300.
Two A recordsThe load balancer has an address in each of two zones; clients pick one.
Query time: 3 msecAnswered from cache. An uncached lookup takes tens of milliseconds.
SERVER: 192.168.1.1Which resolver answered: here the home router. In a VPC you'd see the VPC resolver.

To skip caches entirely and ask the source, use dig +trace api.larder.example, which walks the tree like Figure 5.1, or ask an authoritative server directly with dig @ns-1.awsdns-01.org api.larder.example.

5.8Changing a record safely

At Larder: solved

api.larder.example had a TTL of one hour, so some resolvers would keep the old answer until 23:00 at the latest, exactly when Eli deleted the old load balancer. That alone was cutting it fine. But StockPilot's integration servers resolve the name once when they start and keep the address until they restart, which they do once a week. No TTL would have helped them.

Eli restored the old load balancer from code, pointed it at the new servers, and asked StockPilot to restart their workers. Larder's runbook for endpoint changes now has five steps, below.

  1. A day ahead, lower the TTL to 60 seconds, and wait at least the old TTL so every cache holds the short one.
  2. Run old and new side by side. Both endpoints must serve correctly during the change.
  3. Change the record, then check with dig against several resolvers (@1.1.1.1, @8.8.8.8, the VPC resolver).
  4. Watch the old endpoint's traffic until it reaches zero. Find and contact whoever is still sending.
  5. Remove the old endpoint, then raise the TTL back to its normal value.

5.9Lab: ask DNS questions properly

Lab 5 · dig, from cache to root

You'll look up records, watch a TTL count down, follow a lookup from the root, and compare resolvers. You need dig (preinstalled on macOS; apt install dnsutils on Ubuntu; on Windows, use WSL or Resolve-DnsName in PowerShell). Cost: nothing.

  1. Basic lookups. dig www.wikipedia.org, then dig +short www.wikipedia.org AAAA, dig +short wikipedia.org MX, dig +short wikipedia.org NS and dig +short wikipedia.org TXT. Match each answer to a row of Table 5.1.
  2. Watch a TTL. Run dig www.wikipedia.org three times, ten seconds apart, and watch the TTL in the answer count down. That's the cache in your resolver.
  3. Follow the tree. dig +trace www.wikipedia.org. Find the root's referral, the .org referral, and the final answer from Wikipedia's own name servers.
  4. Compare resolvers. dig @1.1.1.1 www.wikipedia.org and dig @8.8.8.8 www.wikipedia.org. Do they give the same addresses? Big sites often answer differently depending on where you ask from.
  5. Get an error on purpose. dig no-such-name-1234.wikipedia.org. Find NXDOMAIN and the SOA record in the authority section: its last number is how long the “no” may be cached.
  6. Reverse lookup. dig -x 1.1.1.1 +short. Which name does the address map back to?

Stretch goal. If you have an AWS account, create a Route 53 private hosted zone in the Chapter 8 lab VPC, add a record, and look it up from an instance in that VPC and from your laptop. (Hosted zones cost $0.50 a month; delete it afterwards.)

✓Chapter summary

  • DNS maps names to records through a hierarchy of zones, each served by its own authoritative servers and reached by delegation.
  • Your device asks a recursive resolver, which follows referrals from the root and caches what it learns.
  • Use A/AAAA for addresses, CNAME to point at other names, and alias records at the zone apex and for cloud resources.
  • TTLs control caching. Lower them a day before a change; old answers live for the old TTL, and some apps ignore TTLs entirely.
  • Every VPC has a provider resolver; private zones give internal names, and can create split-horizon answers.
  • Use dig to see the status, the TTL left, and which resolver answered.

Key terms

Zone
A part of the DNS tree managed as one unit.
Authoritative server
A server whose answers for a zone are the official ones.
Delegation
A parent zone pointing to a child zone's servers with NS records.
Recursive resolver
The server that finds answers for clients and caches them.
Record
A name, type, value and TTL in a zone.
CNAME
A record that makes one name an alias of another.
Alias record
A provider feature that answers with a resource's current addresses, usable at the apex.
Zone apex
The bare domain itself, like larder.example.
TTL
How many seconds an answer may be cached.
NXDOMAIN
“No such name”, itself cached for a while.
Private hosted zone
A zone visible only inside the networks it's attached to.
Split-horizon DNS
Different answers for the same name inside and outside a network.

Check your understanding

  1. Why can't larder.example itself be a CNAME to a load balancer, and what do you use instead? recall

    Show answer

    The zone apex must hold NS and SOA records, and a CNAME can't share its name with any other record. Use an alias record (Route 53) or ALIAS/ANAME (other hosts), which answers with the load balancer's current addresses.

  2. A record has a TTL of 86,400 seconds. You change it at 10:00. When can you be sure every well-behaved resolver sees the new value? apply

    Show answer

    Not until 10:00 the next day: a resolver that cached it just before the change may keep it for a full day. Lowering the TTL to 60 a day ahead would have made it about a minute.

  3. dig on an instance shows the new address, but the application on that instance keeps calling the old one. Name two possible causes. explain

    Show answer

    The application caches DNS itself (for example, resolving once at startup or a runtime that caches indefinitely), or it holds long-lived connections opened before the change. Others: an entry in /etc/hosts, or the app using a different resolver than dig.

  4. An instance resolves db.internal.larder.example fine, but a new VPC's instances get NXDOMAIN. What's the likely cause? apply

    Show answer

    The private hosted zone isn't associated with the new VPC (or the VPC's DNS resolution and hostnames settings are off). Private zones answer only inside the VPCs they're attached to.

  5. You need to fail over a public website from Frankfurt to a standby region. Why is a DNS failover record a reasonable choice, and what's its main limitation? design

    Show answer

    It's simple, cheap and works across regions and providers, with health checks deciding the answer. Its limitation is caching: clients switch over only as cached answers expire, so with short TTLs failover takes minutes, and clients that ignore TTLs may not switch at all.
