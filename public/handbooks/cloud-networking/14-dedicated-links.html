Hybrid connectivity II: dedicated connections

A VPN borrows the internet. A dedicated connection is your own private circuit into the provider's network: steady latency, many gigabits, cheaper data transfer, and a long list of things to order, cable and pay for. This chapter explains how Direct Connect and its equivalents are put together, how to make them resilient, and how to decide whether you need one at all.

By the end you can

  • Explain how a dedicated connection reaches the cloud, from your router to a VPC.
  • Choose between dedicated and hosted connections, and between virtual interface types.
  • Design for 99.9% and 99.99% availability.
  • Work out when a dedicated connection pays for itself.

At Larder

After an investor dinner, Femke, Larder's CEO, asks Ana a pointed question: “A competitor told me they run their stores on private lines to the cloud. The internet feels fragile for something this important. Should we?” Ana asks Eli to work out what a private connection would cost Larder, what it would fix, and what it wouldn't.

Eli's answer, in section 14.6, is a short decision record. Getting to it needs the rest of the chapter.

14.1The big picture

A dedicated connection is a physical circuit, usually fibre, from your network to a router owned by the cloud provider. It never touches the public internet. AWS calls it Direct Connect, Azure ExpressRoute, Google Cloud Cloud Interconnect. All three meet you in the same kind of place: a colocation facility, a building where carriers, providers and customers rent space and connect to each other with short fibre cross-connects.

What you get, compared with a VPN: steady latency (no internet congestion in between), high bandwidth (1 to 400 Gbps per connection), and lower data-transfer-out prices. What you don't get by default: encryption. The circuit is private, not encrypted (14.4).

14.2Getting a connection

There are two ways to buy one:

Dedicated connectionHosted connection
What it isa physical port on an AWS router, yours alonea slice of a partner's connection, set up by the partner

For a dedicated connection, AWS gives you a Letter of Authorization (LOA-CFA) that tells the colocation provider which port to connect your cross-connect to. Then the slow part begins: ordering the carrier circuit to your building, the cross-connect, and the router configuration. Plan for one to three months.

14.3Virtual interfaces and the DX gateway

A connection is just a pipe. To use it, you create one or more virtual interfaces (VIFs) over it, each with its own VLAN tag and its own BGP session (Chapter 13 had BGP over tunnels; this is the same idea over a VLAN).

Table 14.2 · Virtual interface types
TypeReachesThrough
Private VIFVPCs, using private addressesa virtual private gateway (one VPC), or a DX gateway to several VPCs' gateways
Transit VIFeverything on one or more transit gatewaysa DX gateway associated with the TGWs
Public VIFAWS public endpoints (S3, other public services) over the private circuitAWS's public network; you need public addresses

A Direct Connect gateway is a global object that links your virtual interfaces to gateways in any AWS region (outside China). One connection in Amsterdam can reach VPCs in Frankfurt and Ireland through it, using AWS's backbone between regions. With a transit gateway, the usual modern design is one transit VIF to a DX gateway, associated with each region's TGW, and the TGW's route tables decide who reaches what (Chapter 11).

BFDBGP alone can take up to its hold time, often 90 seconds on physical links, to notice a dead path. Turn on Bidirectional Forwarding Detection on your router; AWS supports it on Direct Connect, and failover drops to under a second.

14.4Resilience and encryption

One connection is one router, one cross-connect, one circuit, one building: four single points of failure. AWS publishes three recommended designs:

Table 14.3 · Direct Connect resiliency models
ModelDesignSurvivesTarget
Development and testone connection at one locationnothing; add a VPN backupnon-critical
High resiliencyone connection at each of two locationsa device, circuit or whole-location failure99.9%
Maximum resiliencytwo connections, on separate devices, at each of two locationsa location failure and a device failure at the other99.99%

A VPN as backup is a cheap middle ground: a site-to-site VPN to the same transit gateway, with BGP preferring Direct Connect while it's up. Remember the size difference, though: a VPN can't carry 10 Gbps of failover traffic, so decide in advance what's allowed to run slowly during a failure.

Encryption

Traffic on a dedicated connection is isolated but not encrypted. Three ways to add encryption:

  • TLS end to end in your applications (Chapter 6). Usually there already, and enough for many companies.
  • MACsec, link-layer encryption between your router and AWS's, on dedicated 10 and 100 Gbps connections at supported locations. Line-rate, no throughput cost.
  • IPsec VPN over Direct Connect: a site-to-site VPN that runs over the private circuit instead of the internet. Encrypted, with the VPN's per-tunnel bandwidth limits.

14.5What it costs

A Direct Connect bill has several parts: an hourly charge per port (dedicated) or per hosted connection; data transfer out of AWS at a reduced rate (data in is free); and costs outside AWS: the carrier circuit, the cross-connect and rack space at the colocation, and possibly a partner's fee. At the time of writing, data out over Direct Connect in Europe costs around $0.02 per GB, against roughly $0.09 per GB to the internet for the first tiers.

That price difference is where a dedicated connection pays for itself. A rough break-even:

Table 14.4 · An illustrative monthly comparison for a data centre pulling data from AWS
Data out per monthInternet (≈ $0.09/GB)Direct Connect (≈ $0.02/GB + ≈ $1,200 fixed)
2 TB$180$1,240
20 TB$1,800$1,600
100 TB$9,000$3,200

The fixed amount here assumes a 1 Gbps hosted connection, a circuit and colocation fees; your quotes will differ, often a lot. The shape won't: below a few tens of terabytes a month, cost alone rarely justifies a dedicated line.

14.6Do you need one?

Good reasons for a dedicated connection: large, steady traffic between a data centre and the cloud; latency-sensitive systems split across both, such as a factory's control software; regulation or policy that forbids the internet as a path; or a migration that must move hundreds of terabytes. Weak reasons: “the internet feels fragile” for sites that send little data, or many small sites that each would need their own circuit.

At Larder: solved

Eli's decision record was one page. Context: Larder has no data centre. Its traffic to Frankfurt comes from dozens of small stores (a few GB a day each) and an office that mostly uses SaaS tools. Options: a Direct Connect from each store (dozens of circuits, months of lead time, thousands a month each); one Direct Connect into a carrier's network that also serves the stores (an MPLS-style network, a large contract); or keep VPN over fibre and 4G with BGP failover, which already survives a line failure in under a minute (Chapter 13). Decision: keep VPNs. Revisit when: Larder runs a site with constant traffic above about 1 Gbps or 20 TB a month, such as an automated central warehouse, or a store system that can't tolerate the internet's occasional bad minute.

Femke's competitor, it turned out, has a large central warehouse with robots controlled from the cloud. For them, a private line is exactly right.

14.8Lab: find the buildings and do the sums

Lab 14 · Locations, a resilient design and a break-even

You'll list Direct Connect locations, sketch a 99.99% design for a made-up data centre, and compute the break-even for your own numbers. Nobody can order a circuit in a lab, so this one is about reading and deciding. You need the AWS CLI and Python. Cost: nothing; the CLI calls only read.

  1. List locations served from Frankfurt: 
    aws directconnect describe-locations --region eu-central-1 \
  --query 'locations[].[locationCode,locationName,availablePortSpeeds]' --output table
    Pick two locations in different cities. Why would two buildings in the same city be weaker?
  2. Sketch maximum resiliency for a data centre in Rotterdam: two locations, two connections at each, on separate routers on your side too, a transit VIF on each connection to one DX gateway, associated with the Frankfurt TGW. Count the BGP sessions (one per VIF).
  3. Do the break-even for your own quote: 
    fixed = 1200          # $/month: port or hosted fee + circuit + colocation (your quote)
dx, net = 0.02, 0.09  # $/GB out over Direct Connect and over the internet
print("break-even: about", round(fixed / (net - dx) / 1000, 1), "TB a month")
    With these numbers it's about 17 TB. Look up current prices for your region and redo it.
  4. Write the decision. For a company you know (or Larder), write a five-line decision record like Eli's: context, options, decision, and what would make you revisit it.

✓Chapter summary

  • A dedicated connection is a private circuit to the provider at a colocation facility: steady latency, high bandwidth, cheaper data out, not encrypted by default.
  • Dedicated connections are your own port; hosted ones come from a partner, smaller and faster to get.
  • Virtual interfaces (private, transit, public) each carry a VLAN and a BGP session; a DX gateway reaches many regions.
  • Resilience means two locations; 99.99% means two connections at each. Use BFD for fast failover.
  • Encrypt with TLS, MACsec or IPsec over the circuit.
  • It pays off with large, steady traffic or strict requirements, not for many small sites.

Key terms

Dedicated connection
A physical private circuit into a cloud provider's network.
Colocation facility
A building where carriers, providers and customers interconnect.
Cross-connect
A short fibre between two parties' equipment in the same building.
Hosted connection
A share of a partner's Direct Connect capacity.
Virtual interface (VIF)
A VLAN with a BGP session over a Direct Connect connection.
Direct Connect gateway
A global object linking VIFs to gateways in many regions.
BFD
A fast liveness check that lets BGP fail over in under a second.
MACsec
Link-layer encryption between two directly connected routers.

Check your understanding

  1. Which virtual interface type would you use to reach VPCs in two regions through their transit gateways? recall

    Show answer

    A transit VIF to a Direct Connect gateway, with the DX gateway associated with each region's transit gateway.

  2. Why is “two connections at one Direct Connect location” not the high-resiliency design? explain

    Show answer

    Both share the building: a power failure, fire, flood or cut fibre into that location takes both down. High resiliency needs connections at two different locations.

  3. Your company moves 5 TB a month from AWS to its office. A sales rep proposes Direct Connect to save money. Is it likely to? apply

    Show answer

    Unlikely. At about $0.07 per GB saved, 5 TB saves roughly $350 a month, well below the fixed costs of a port or hosted connection, circuit and colocation. Other reasons (latency, policy) might still justify it, but cost alone doesn't.

  4. A regulator requires encryption on all links carrying customer data. Your 10 Gbps dedicated connection runs at 7 Gbps. Which encryption option fits, and why not the others? design

    Show answer

    MACsec, if your location supports it: line-rate encryption with no throughput loss. IPsec over Direct Connect would need many tunnels at about 1.25 Gbps each to carry 7 Gbps. TLS in every application is good practice but may not satisfy a rule about the link itself.

  5. A Direct Connect link with a VPN backup fails over to the VPN, and everything slows to a crawl. What did the design miss? design

    Show answer

    The backup's capacity. A VPN's tunnels carry far less than a dedicated link, so the design needed either more VPN capacity (several connections with ECMP), a second Direct Connect, or an agreed list of what may be paused or slowed during failover.
