Hybrid connectivity I: site-to-site VPN and BGP

Offices, stores, factories and data centres reach the cloud most often through a site-to-site VPN: an encrypted tunnel across the ordinary internet. It's cheap, quick to set up and good enough for most sites. This chapter explains how the tunnels work, how BGP runs inside them to keep routes and failover automatic, and the packet-size problem that catches almost everyone once.

By the end you can

  • Explain what IPsec does and what a VPN tunnel adds to every packet.
  • Set up an AWS site-to-site VPN with two tunnels and BGP.
  • Design redundancy across tunnels, devices and internet links.
  • Recognise and fix an MTU black hole.

At Larder

Tomás, who runs the Noord dark store, reports something strange. Since the new stock-count feature went live, the picker handhelds' end-of-day count upload spins forever and fails. Scanning items, small updates, looking up orders: all fine. Same handhelds, same Wi-Fi, same VPN to Frankfurt. Only the big upload, about 2 MB, never arrives. Eli sees the upload's connection open on the server, then nothing.

Small packets work and big ones vanish. That pattern has one usual cause, and section 13.5 finds it.

13.1The big picture

A site-to-site VPN joins a whole network, like a store's LAN, to a cloud network, through an encrypted tunnel over the internet. Your end is a router or firewall called the customer gateway; the cloud end is a managed gateway, on AWS either a virtual private gateway (for one VPC) or a transit gateway attachment (for many, Chapter 11). Devices behind each end don't know the tunnel exists: they just route to the other side's addresses.

13.2IPsec in brief

IPsec is the family of protocols almost every site-to-site VPN uses. Two parts matter:

  • IKE (Internet Key Exchange, UDP port 500, or 4500 when there's NAT in the way) lets the two gateways prove who they are, usually with a pre-shared key or certificates, and agree on encryption keys. It runs in two phases: first a secure channel for the gateways themselves, then keys for your traffic. Keys are renewed regularly.
  • ESP (Encapsulating Security Payload) carries your traffic. In tunnel mode, each of your packets is encrypted whole and wrapped in a new IP packet between the two gateways' public addresses: encapsulation once more (Chapter 1).

Both ends must agree on the details: IKE version (use IKEv2), encryption and integrity algorithms, key lifetimes, and the pre-shared key. The provider gives you a configuration file for common router brands with all of this filled in; most VPN problems on the first day are a mismatch in one of these settings, and both ends' logs will say which.

Watch out: firewalls in front of the router

The customer gateway needs UDP 500 and 4500 (and protocol ESP, if no NAT is involved) open to and from the AWS tunnel addresses. A site firewall that blocks them gives you tunnels that never come up, with nothing obvious in the logs on the AWS side.

13.3An AWS site-to-site VPN

Three resources describe one VPN on AWS:

Table 13.1 · The pieces of an AWS VPN
ResourceWhat it holds
Customer gatewayYour device's public IP address and its BGP ASN (Chapter 4). A description, not a device.
Virtual private gateway or transit gatewayThe AWS end. A VGW serves one VPC; a TGW serves everything attached to it.
VPN connectionTwo tunnels between them, each with its own AWS public address, inside addresses (169.254.x.x/30), pre-shared key and options.

Each tunnel carries up to about 1.25 Gbps. With a transit gateway, you can run several VPN connections and use ECMP (equal-cost multi-path) to spread traffic across all their tunnels for more bandwidth. A VPN connection costs about $0.05 an hour, plus normal data transfer out. Each Larder store has two VPN connections (13.6), so about $73 a month per store; a few dozen stores come to a couple of thousand dollars a month, still far less than a private line per store.

13.4BGP inside the tunnels

A VPN can use static routing, where you type the ranges on each side, or dynamic routing with BGP. Use BGP. Inside each tunnel, the two ends open a BGP session over the tunnel's inside addresses and tell each other what they can reach:

Table 13.2 · What each side of Noord's VPN advertises
SideASNAdvertisesWhere it comes from
Noord store router6500810.8.3.0/24the store LAN, configured on the router
AWS (Larder's TGW)6451210.20.0.0/16, 10.16.0.0/16the routes in the TGW route table associated with the VPN attachment (Chapter 11)

BGP buys you three things. New ranges appear by themselves: a new store or VPC is learned, not typed, which is how Larder fixed the Rotterdam bug in Chapter 4. Failover is automatic: if a tunnel goes down, its BGP session drops, its routes are withdrawn and traffic moves to the other tunnel within seconds. And you can steer traffic: making a path look longer with AS path prepending (repeating your ASN in the advertisement) makes the other side prefer a different path, which is how you make a backup link a backup.

How fast is failover?AWS detects a dead tunnel with dead peer detection (a few missed keepalives) and BGP's hold timer (30 seconds on AWS). Expect tens of seconds, during which connections through the failed tunnel stall, then continue on the other one.

13.5MTU, MSS and the black hole

Encryption and the extra IP header add bytes to every packet, typically 50 to 80, depending on the algorithms and on NAT. But the internet path between the two gateways still carries at most 1,500-byte packets. So the largest packet that fits inside the tunnel is smaller, around 1,400 bytes. A full-size 1,500-byte packet from a store handheld can't be sent through as it is.

There are three ways a network can deal with an oversized packet. It can fragment it into two pieces, which works but is slow and often blocked. It can drop the packet and send back an ICMP “fragmentation needed” message so the sender lowers its packet size: path MTU discovery. Or, if that ICMP message is itself blocked by a firewall along the way, large packets just disappear and nobody is told. That's an MTU black hole: handshakes and small requests work, because their packets are small, and anything large hangs.

At Larder: solved

The store routers' firewall blocked all inbound ICMP “for security”, so the “fragmentation needed” messages never reached the handhelds. Scans and lookups use small packets and worked; the 2 MB stock count needed full-size packets and fell into the black hole. Eli confirmed it in two minutes from a laptop in the store: ping -M do -s 1372 10.20.17.8 worked and -s 1472 got no reply.

The fix was one line on every store router: clamp TCP's MSS on the tunnel interface to the value in AWS's configuration file. During every TCP handshake the router lowers the maximum segment size both ends agree on, so packets are small enough before they're ever sent. Eli also allowed ICMP “fragmentation needed” through the store firewalls, so path MTU discovery works for anything that isn't TCP.

Key idea

Small things work, big things hang: suspect MTU. Clamp MSS on every tunnel, and never block ICMP “fragmentation needed”.

13.6Designing for failure

Two tunnels protect you from a failure on AWS's side. They don't protect you from your own router, power, or internet line failing. Build redundancy at each level you care about:

Table 13.3 · VPN redundancy levels
DesignSurvivesTypical for
One router, one VPN connection (two tunnels)AWS endpoint failure or maintenancesmall offices
One router, two internet links, a VPN connection on eachan ISP or line failureLarder's stores (fibre plus 4G)
Two routers, two links, two VPN connectionsa router failure as welloffices and data centres
VPN as backup to a dedicated connectiona private line failuredata centres (Chapter 14)

Larder's stores have a fibre line and a 4G modem. Each line has its own VPN connection to the transit gateway, both using BGP. On the 4G connection the store router prepends its ASN twice, so AWS sees a longer path and prefers fibre; the router itself prefers the fibre tunnels for outbound traffic with a higher local preference. When the fibre drops, the fibre tunnels' BGP sessions go down, those routes disappear, and traffic moves to 4G in under a minute. Nobody has to do anything.

Remote users

Site-to-site VPNs connect networks. For individual people, AWS Client VPN and similar services give each laptop its own tunnel. Many companies now prefer identity-aware access proxies instead, which check who you are and what device you're on for each application rather than putting you on the network (Chapter 21).

13.7When a VPN isn't enough

A VPN rides on the public internet, so its latency and throughput vary with the internet's mood, and each tunnel tops out at about 1.25 Gbps. For a few stores and offices that's fine. When you need steady latency, many gigabits, or lower data-transfer prices for large volumes, a dedicated private connection is the next step: Chapter 14. Many companies run both, with the VPN as the cheap backup.

13.9Lab: find your path MTU, then read a real VPN's settings

Lab 13 · MTU by hand, and a VPN connection on paper

You'll measure the largest packet your own internet path carries, then create an AWS VPN connection and read its tunnel and BGP settings. The tunnels will stay down, because there's no router at your end; that's fine for reading the configuration. You need a terminal, the AWS CLI and Terraform. Cost: about $0.05 an hour for the VPN connection.

Part A: your path MTU (free).

  1. Send a packet that must not be fragmented, with 1,472 bytes of data (+ 28 bytes of headers = 1,500): ping -M do -s 1472 -c 2 1.1.1.1 on Linux, ping -D -s 1472 -c 2 1.1.1.1 on macOS, ping -f -l 1472 1.1.1.1 on Windows.
  2. If it fails (“message too long” or “frag needed”), lower the size by 10 until it works. Your path MTU is the size that works plus 28.
  3. If you use a VPN on your laptop, repeat with it on. How much smaller is the path now?

Part B: a VPN connection to read.

main.tfTerraform ≥ 1.5, AWS provider 5.x

provider "aws" { region = "eu-central-1" }

variable "my_public_ip" {}                  # curl https://checkip.amazonaws.com

resource "aws_vpc" "lab" { cidr_block = "10.60.0.0/16" }

resource "aws_vpn_gateway" "vgw" {
  vpc_id = aws_vpc.lab.id                       # the AWS end, for one VPC
}
resource "aws_customer_gateway" "store" {
  bgp_asn    = 65008                            # your end's private ASN
  ip_address = var.my_public_ip
  type       = "ipsec.1"
}
resource "aws_vpn_connection" "store" {
  vpn_gateway_id      = aws_vpn_gateway.vgw.id
  customer_gateway_id = aws_customer_gateway.store.id
  type                = "ipsec.1"
  static_routes_only  = false                   # BGP
}

output "tunnels" {
  value = {
    tunnel1 = [aws_vpn_connection.store.tunnel1_address, aws_vpn_connection.store.tunnel1_inside_cidr,
               aws_vpn_connection.store.tunnel1_vgw_inside_address, aws_vpn_connection.store.tunnel1_cgw_inside_address]
    tunnel2 = [aws_vpn_connection.store.tunnel2_address, aws_vpn_connection.store.tunnel2_inside_cidr,
               aws_vpn_connection.store.tunnel2_vgw_inside_address, aws_vpn_connection.store.tunnel2_cgw_inside_address]
  }
}
  1. Create it: terraform apply -var my_public_ip=$(curl -s https://checkip.amazonaws.com). It takes a few minutes.
  2. Read the tunnels: terraform output tunnels. For each tunnel: AWS's public address, the /30 inside range, and the two inside addresses the BGP session will use.
  3. Check their state: aws ec2 describe-vpn-connections --query 'VpnConnections[0].VgwTelemetry[].[OutsideIpAddress,Status,StatusMessage]' --output table. Both DOWN: no router is answering at your address.
  4. Read a router configuration. In the console, open the VPN connection and download the configuration for any router type (or a “Generic” one). Find the IKE version, the algorithms, the pre-shared keys, the BGP neighbour addresses and ASNs, and the recommended MSS clamp. That's everything a network engineer types into the store router.
  5. Clean up: terraform destroy. The pre-shared keys are stored in your Terraform state; treat state files as secrets.

Stretch goal. Bring a tunnel up for real: run a strongSwan or VyOS instance with an Elastic IP in another VPC or region as the “store router”, using the downloaded configuration. Watch the BGP session establish and the store's range appear in the VPC route table as propagated (turn on route propagation for the VGW).

✓Chapter summary

  • A site-to-site VPN joins a site's network to the cloud through encrypted tunnels over the internet.
  • IPsec: IKE (UDP 500/4500) agrees keys; ESP in tunnel mode encrypts and wraps each packet.
  • On AWS, a VPN connection always has two tunnels, ending on a virtual private gateway or a transit gateway.
  • Run BGP in the tunnels: ranges are learned, failover is automatic, and prepending steers traffic.
  • Tunnels shrink the room for packets. Clamp MSS and don't block ICMP, or large transfers vanish into an MTU black hole.
  • Add redundancy at each level that matters: tunnels, internet links, routers.

Key terms

Site-to-site VPN
An encrypted tunnel connecting two networks over the internet.
Customer gateway
Your router or firewall at the site end, and AWS's record of it.
IPsec
The protocol suite for authenticated, encrypted IP tunnels.
IKE
The protocol that authenticates the gateways and agrees keys.
ESP
The IPsec protocol that encrypts and carries your packets.
AS path prepending
Lengthening a route's AS path to make it less preferred.
MTU
The largest packet a link or path can carry.
MSS
The largest TCP payload per packet, agreed in the handshake.
MSS clamping
A router lowering the MSS in passing handshakes so packets fit.
MTU black hole
Large packets silently dropped because the “too big” message is blocked.

Check your understanding

  1. Why does an AWS VPN connection have two tunnels, and what happens if you configure only one? recall

    Show answer

    The two tunnels end on two separate AWS devices, so one can fail or be taken down for maintenance while the other carries traffic. With only one configured, you lose connectivity whenever that one is down, including during routine AWS maintenance.

  2. SSH sessions over a new VPN work, but scp of a large file stalls right after starting. What do you suspect, and how do you confirm it? apply

    Show answer

    An MTU black hole: the interactive session uses small packets, the file transfer full-size ones. Confirm with a do-not-fragment ping across the tunnel at decreasing sizes (ping -M do -s 1472, then smaller) and find where replies start; fix with MSS clamping and by allowing ICMP “fragmentation needed”.

  3. A store has fibre and 4G, each with a VPN connection. How do you make sure AWS sends traffic over fibre while it's up? apply

    Show answer

    With BGP on both: have the store router prepend its ASN on the routes it advertises over the 4G connection, so AWS sees a longer AS path there and prefers the fibre path. When the fibre's sessions drop, only the 4G routes remain and traffic moves over.

  4. What does BGP give you over static routes in a VPN with many sites? explain

    Show answer

    New ranges are advertised and learned automatically, failed tunnels' routes are withdrawn so traffic fails over without human action, and preferences between paths can be expressed with attributes such as AS path length.

  5. Your company opens a data centre that will send 5 Gbps to AWS around the clock. Is a site-to-site VPN a good fit? design

    Show answer

    Not on its own. One tunnel carries about 1.25 Gbps, so you'd need several connections with ECMP through a transit gateway; you'd depend on the internet's variable latency; and internet data transfer prices for that volume add up. A dedicated connection (Direct Connect) is the better primary path, with the VPN as backup.
