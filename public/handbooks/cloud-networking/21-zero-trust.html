Service mesh and zero-trust networking

Everything so far has used the network to decide who may talk to whom: this subnet may reach that one, this security group may reach that port. Zero trust adds a second question to every connection: who is asking, proven by identity rather than by address. This chapter explains what that means for people reaching internal tools, for services calling each other, and where a service mesh fits, with honest notes on what each costs.

By the end you can

  • Explain the zero-trust model and how it differs from a trusted internal network.
  • Replace broad VPN access with per-application, identity-aware access.
  • Explain workload identity and mutual TLS between services.
  • Decide whether a service mesh, a managed service network, or neither, fits your system.

At Larder

Lena's identity review (ADR 0021) found that a support agent who had left Larder still had helpdesk and billing access three weeks later. It also found something worse in the network: the agent's laptop VPN profile still worked, and anyone on the VPN could reach everything in 10.0.0.0/8, including the internal refund tool, which trusted any request from inside the network. Nothing bad happened. Lena's conclusion was blunt: “Our internal tools are protected by being internal. That isn't protection.”

Section 21.3 shows what replaced the VPN for staff tools.

21.1The big picture

The traditional model is a castle with a moat: a strong perimeter around the network, and trust for anything inside it. It fails in two ways. Once an attacker is inside, through a phished laptop, a leaked VPN profile or a compromised server, everything is reachable. And “inside” has stopped meaning much: people work from home, services run in several clouds, and partners connect in.

Zero trust (described in NIST SP 800-207) drops the idea of a trusted network. Its principles:

  • Verify explicitly: every request is authenticated and authorised, using the identity of the user or service, and the state of the device, not the network it comes from.
  • Least privilege: each identity gets only the access it needs, for as long as it needs it.
  • Assume breach: design so that one compromised laptop or service reaches as little as possible, and is noticed.

Key idea

Zero trust doesn't remove network controls. It stops treating them as proof of who someone is. Keep the segments and security groups; add identity on every request.

What stays: address plans, segmentation with route tables, security groups and network policies, egress control. They limit the blast radius when identity checks fail. Zero trust is a layer on top, not a replacement.

21.3People reaching internal apps

A VPN puts a person's device onto the network; after that, routing and firewalls decide what it can reach, usually far more than the person needs. An identity-aware proxy works per application instead: the person opens the tool's URL, the proxy checks their identity with the company's identity provider (SSO) and the device's posture (managed, encrypted, up to date), applies a policy for that application, and only then forwards the request. The device never joins the network.

At Larder: solved

Larder's 14 staff tools moved behind AWS Verified Access, one application at a time. Each tool's policy names the identity provider group that may use it (“support” for the refund tool, “platform” for the admin dashboard) and requires a managed laptop. Because ADR 0021 connected the identity provider to HR (SCIM), a leaver's access to all 14 tools ends when HR closes their record. The staff VPN was switched off for tools in October; it remains only for the two engineers who need to reach databases during incidents, with short-lived credentials. The refund tool also stopped trusting “inside” requests: it now reads the signed identity header the proxy adds and checks it on every request.

21.4Services calling services

The same idea applies between services: the payments service shouldn't trust a call because it came from sg-app, but because the caller proved it's the orders service. That needs workload identity: a credential issued to each service, automatically, short-lived, that it can present on every call. Common forms:

  • Certificates for mutual TLS (Chapter 6): each service has a certificate naming it, often in the SPIFFE format (spiffe://larder.example/ns/orders/sa/api), and both ends of every connection verify each other.
  • Cloud IAM identities: requests signed with the caller's role, checked against a policy, as AWS's own APIs do. VPC Lattice uses this for service-to-service calls (21.6).
  • Tokens: signed tokens (JWTs) from an identity service, checked by the callee.

Identity enables authorisation policies that read like intent: “orders may call payments POST /charges; nothing else may call payments at all”. Combined with the network controls from earlier chapters, an attacker who takes over one service still can't use it to reach others it was never meant to call.

21.5Service meshes

Adding mTLS, policies, retries and metrics to every service's code is a lot of work in many languages. A service mesh moves that work into the network path. A data plane of proxies sits next to each workload, either a sidecar container in every pod (Envoy with Istio, Linkerd's own proxy) or a shared proxy per node (Istio's ambient mode), and a control plane issues certificates and pushes configuration to them.

What a mesh gives you: mTLS everywhere with automatic certificate rotation; authorisation policies by service identity; traffic control (retries, timeouts, circuit breaking, canary splits); and uniform metrics and traces for every call. What it costs: another distributed system to run and upgrade; extra memory and CPU for proxies; a little latency per hop (usually around a millisecond); and new ways to fail, such as expired certificates or a broken control plane.

Watch out: a mesh for five services

A mesh pays off with dozens of services, several teams and real requirements for encryption and per-call policy. For a handful of services, TLS in the apps, security groups or network policies, and a good HTTP client library usually give most of the benefit at a fraction of the effort.

21.6Managed service networking

Providers now offer some of a mesh's benefits as managed services, without proxies to run:

  • Amazon VPC Lattice connects services across VPCs and accounts through a service network: services are registered by name, callers in associated VPCs reach them through Lattice, and auth policies decide, using IAM, which callers may use which paths. Overlapping address ranges don't matter, and no transit gateway routes are needed for service traffic.
  • ECS Service Connect gives ECS services discovery, retries and metrics with a managed proxy.
  • AWS is retiring App Mesh, its earlier managed mesh; new designs use VPC Lattice, Service Connect, or an open-source mesh on EKS.

Larder chose identity-aware access for staff, mTLS in the payments path (where it was required), network policies on EKS, and no mesh yet: about twenty services and three teams didn't justify running one. The decision record names the trigger to revisit: the first time a second service needs per-call authorisation, or when service count passes about fifty.

21.8Lab: mutual TLS by hand

Lab 21 · A tiny certificate authority and two identities

You'll create a private certificate authority, issue certificates to a “payments” server and an “orders” client, and see a server accept a client because of its identity, and refuse one without it. A mesh does exactly this, automatically, for every pair of services. You need openssl and two terminals. Cost: nothing.

  1. A certificate authority (in an empty folder): 
    openssl req -x509 -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 -nodes \
  -keyout ca.key -out ca.crt -days 1 -subj "/CN=Larder lab CA"
  2. Two workload identities, signed by the CA: 
    for name in payments orders; do
  openssl req -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 -nodes \
    -keyout $name.key -out $name.csr -subj "/CN=$name.larder.internal"
  openssl x509 -req -in $name.csr -CA ca.crt -CAkey ca.key -CAcreateserial \
    -out $name.crt -days 1
done
  3. A server that requires a client certificate. In terminal 1: 
    openssl s_server -accept 8443 -cert payments.crt -key payments.key \
  -CAfile ca.crt -Verify 1 -www
  4. Call it as “orders”. In terminal 2: 
    openssl s_client -connect localhost:8443 -cert orders.crt -key orders.key \
  -CAfile ca.crt -quiet <<< $'GET / HTTP/1.0\r\n\r\n' | grep 'Subject:'
    The server's status page lists the certificate the client presented: Subject: CN=orders.larder.internal. The server knows who is calling, whatever its IP address.
  5. Call it without an identity. Repeat step 4 without -cert and -key. The handshake fails: no certificate, no connection. Then make a certificate signed by a different CA and try again; it fails too.

Stretch goal. Add a check in your head (or a small script around s_server): accept only CN=orders.larder.internal. That's an authorisation policy by identity, the step after authentication.

✓Chapter summary

  • Zero trust: verify identity and device on every request, grant least privilege, assume breach. Network location proves nothing.
  • Keep network controls as a second layer; add identity on top.
  • For people, replace broad VPNs with identity-aware proxies per application, tied to SSO and device checks.
  • For services, use workload identity (certificates, IAM, tokens) and authorisation policies by identity.
  • A service mesh adds mTLS, policy, traffic control and telemetry through proxies, at real operational cost.
  • Managed options such as VPC Lattice cover many needs without running proxies.

Key terms

Zero trust
A model in which no network location is trusted; every request is verified.
Perimeter security
Trusting everything inside a protected network boundary.
Identity-aware proxy
A proxy that checks user identity and device before forwarding to one app.
Device posture
The state of a device (managed, encrypted, patched) used in access decisions.
Workload identity
A credential that proves which service a request comes from.
SPIFFE
A standard format for workload identities in certificates.
Service mesh
Proxies beside each workload, managed by a control plane, handling service-to-service traffic.
Sidecar
A helper container running next to the application in the same pod.
VPC Lattice
AWS's managed service-to-service networking with IAM auth policies.

Check your understanding

  1. In one sentence each: what does a VPN grant, and what does an identity-aware proxy grant? recall

    Show answer

    A VPN grants the device a place on the network, from which routing and firewalls decide what it can reach. An identity-aware proxy grants a verified person on a checked device access to one application, request by request.

  2. Why keep security groups if every service authenticates every call with mTLS? explain

    Show answer

    Defence in depth: if a certificate is stolen, a policy is wrong, or a service has a bug that skips checks, network controls still limit what a compromised workload can even connect to. They also reduce noise and attack surface for things that shouldn't talk at all.

  3. A team of six runs eight services on EKS and wants encryption between them. Do they need a service mesh? design

    Show answer

    Probably not. TLS between services (certificates from a private CA or cert-manager), network policies and security groups for pods cover encryption and reachability at much lower operational cost. Revisit when service count, teams or per-call authorisation needs grow.

  4. What did the lab's failed connection without a client certificate demonstrate? explain

    Show answer

    That the server decided whom to talk to by cryptographic identity, not by network position: a client on the same machine, with full network access, was refused because it couldn't prove an identity signed by the trusted CA.

  5. How does SSO with SCIM fix the leaver problem from Larder's review? apply

    Show answer

    Access to every tool is granted through the identity provider, which HR's system updates automatically. When HR closes a record, SCIM removes the account and groups everywhere, and the identity-aware proxy refuses the next request; there's no separate VPN profile or tool account left behind.
