Part III · Access for people

Single sign-on with IAM Identity Center

This is the chapter where people stop having IAM users. With IAM Identity Center, everyone signs in once, with strong MFA, and picks an account and a job; AWS hands them a role session for exactly that. Access is granted to groups, in one place, across every account. This chapter explains how the pieces fit, how to design permission sets and assignments, and what changes in the console, the CLI and CloudTrail.

By the end you can

  • Explain instances, identity sources, permission sets and account assignments, and how they become IAM roles.
  • Design a small set of permission sets by job, and assign them to groups.
  • Sign in to the console and the CLI through the AWS access portal.
  • Read Identity Center sessions in CloudTrail, and write guardrails that refer to them.
  • Retire IAM users safely.

Before you start

  • Roles and sessions (Chapter 8); access models (Chapter 6); federation (Chapter 5).
  • Guardrails and aws:PrincipalArn (Chapter 10).

At Larder

By January 2027, Larder's AWS access is safer than it was in November, and much more awkward. The eight engineers who use AWS have IAM users in the production account, and most of them a second one in the staging account; Larder's other ten engineers have no AWS access at all, and ask a colleague when they need to look at something. The six on call assume prod-operator with a code from an authenticator app, because the security keys Eli bought in November don't work for the CLI (Chapter 8). A new joiner needs two users, two passwords and two MFA devices before lunch; a leaver needs someone to remember both.

Eli's January goal is simple to state: by the end of the month, nobody at Larder has an IAM user. Section 12.10 shows how it went.

12.1Why single sign-on for the cloud

Everything in Part I points the same way: one identity per person, strong and phishing-resistant authentication, short-lived credentials, access granted to groups and removed in one place. IAM Identity Center is AWS's service for exactly that. It's where people sign in, it's connected to your company directory, and it turns “Ben is on the on-call team” into a one-hour role session in the production account, with nothing long-lived in between.

For people, it replaces IAM users completely. Workloads still use roles directly (Chapter 15); Identity Center is for humans.

12.2The pieces

  • An instance of Identity Center. To give access to AWS accounts you need an organisation instance, which lives in an AWS Organization's management account (or is administered from a delegated member account). An account instance in a standalone account can only serve applications, not AWS account access.
  • An identity source: where users and groups come from (12.4).
  • A permission set: a template for a role: AWS managed policies, references to customer managed policies, an inline policy, an optional permissions boundary, and a session duration (1 to 12 hours).
  • An account assignment: a user or group, a permission set, and an account. For each assignment, Identity Center creates an IAM role named AWSReservedSSO_PermissionSetName_suffix in that account, with a trust policy that only Identity Center can use. You don't edit these roles; you change the permission set, and Identity Center updates them everywhere.
  • The AWS access portal (https://your-alias.awsapps.com/start): where people sign in and choose an account and a permission set.

12.3It needs an organisation

Because account access needs an organisation instance, single sign-on is usually the moment a company creates an AWS Organization. Chapter 20 covers organisations properly; for now, three decisions matter:

  • Create it from a new, empty account. The management account can't be restricted by SCPs and controls every member, so it should run nothing else. Turning an existing production account into the management account means living with that forever.
  • Invite the existing accounts as members. Their resources, IAM users and roles are untouched by joining.
  • Choose Identity Center's region carefully; the instance lives in one region. Pick the one your team works in (Larder: Frankfurt, eu-central-1).

You can start with the built-in directory and switch to an external IdP later; users are matched by user name, and assignments to groups carry over if the groups are recreated with the same names. That's Larder's path: the directory now, the company IdP in May (Chapter 13).

With the built-in directory, set MFA to be required at every sign-in, and allow only FIDO2 authenticators (security keys and passkeys) if you can. Unlike MFA for IAM users' CLI calls (Chapter 8), these work everywhere, CLI included, because the CLI signs in through the browser.

12.5Designing permission sets

Permission sets are roles by job (Chapter 6): describe what a kind of work needs, and let assignments say where. A handful usually covers a company of Larder's size:

Table 12.2 · Larder's permission sets
Permission setPoliciesSessionWho, where
ViewOnlyAWS managed ViewOnlyAccess: see configuration, not data8 hall engineers, prod
DeveloperPowerUserAccess, boundary team-boundary8 hall engineers, dev-staging
ProdOperatorcustomer managed prod-operator (from Chapter 8)1 hon-call group, prod
DbChangecustomer managed db-change1 hthe two database owners, prod
PlatformAdminAdministratorAccess1 hplatform team, every account

Two design notes:

  • ViewOnlyAccess rather than ReadOnlyAccess. ReadOnlyAccess reads data, including every object in every bucket and every secret's metadata; ViewOnlyAccess shows resources and configuration without their contents. For “all engineers in production”, that difference is everything.
  • Customer managed policy references attach a policy by name, which must exist in each account where the permission set is assigned. That lets the policy differ per account (with the right ARNs) while the permission set stays the same. Create the policy first, or provisioning fails.

12.6Assignments: groups, never people

An assignment can name a user or a group. Assign to groups, always. A group assignment is a policy you can review (“the on-call team operates production”); a user assignment is an exception nobody remembers. When someone joins, moves or leaves, you change their group memberships, ideally in the company directory (Chapter 13), and every assignment follows.

Key idea

Permission sets describe jobs; groups describe people; assignments say where. Nobody gets access as an individual.

Together, groups × permission sets × accounts are an access matrix (Chapter 6), and the lab turns them into one so you can read who can do what, where. Review it whenever it changes, and look for two things above all: assignments to individuals, and powerful permission sets held permanently in production. The second is Chapter 14's subject.

12.7Signing in: console, CLI and CloudTrail

For the CLI, configure a profile per account and permission set, sharing one sign-in session:

[sso-session larder]
sso_start_url = https://larder.awsapps.com/start
sso_region = eu-central-1
sso_registration_scopes = sso:account:access

[profile prod-operator]
sso_session = larder
sso_account_id = 111122223333
sso_role_name = ProdOperator
region = eu-central-1

aws sso login --sso-session larder opens the browser (the authorization code flow with PKCE from Chapter 4; --use-device-code for remote machines), and afterwards aws s3 ls --profile prod-operator just works, with credentials that are fetched and refreshed for you and never written to ~/.aws/credentials.

In CloudTrail, a session shows the role and the person:

"userIdentity": {
  "type": "AssumedRole",
  "arn": "arn:aws:sts::111122223333:assumed-role/AWSReservedSSO_ProdOperator_3f9a8c1e2d4b5a60/ben@larder.example",
  "sessionContext": { "sessionIssuer": {
    "arn": "arn:aws:iam::111122223333:role/aws-reserved/sso.amazonaws.com/eu-central-1/AWSReservedSSO_ProdOperator_3f9a8c1e2d4b5a60" } }
}

Watch out: guardrails that name roles

Identity Center's roles have a generated suffix and a path, /aws-reserved/sso.amazonaws.com/, and the suffix changes if a permission set is deleted and recreated. A guardrail exception that names role/db-change (Chapter 10) stops matching once people work through Identity Center. Match on the stable part with a wildcard, arn:aws:iam::111122223333:role/aws-reserved/sso.amazonaws.com/*AWSReservedSSO_DbChange_*, and test it.

12.8Attributes, not just groups

Identity Center can pass attributes from the identity source into every session as session tags (attributes for access control): a person's team, cost centre or department. A policy can then compare aws:PrincipalTag/team with a resource's team tag, which is the ABAC pattern from Chapter 9 (9.6), with the attribute coming from the company directory instead of being set by hand on a role. One permission set can then serve every team, each seeing only its own resources.

On Azure

On Azure, single sign-on isn't an extra service: Entra ID is the sign-in for the portal, CLI and every subscription, and people keep one identity everywhere. The equivalent design rule is the same: assign Azure RBAC roles to Entra ID groups at the narrowest scope, not to users.

On Google Cloud

People sign in with Google accounts managed in Cloud Identity or Workspace, usually federated to the company IdP, and groups are Google Groups, which you bind to roles on the organisation, folders or projects. Workforce identity federation lets people from an external IdP use Google Cloud without Google accounts at all, the closest match to Identity Center with an external IdP.

12.10At Larder: no more IAM users

At Larder: solved

On 5 January 2027, Eli created a new, empty account, larder-management (777788889999), and secured its root user by the checklist in Chapter 7, with the same group email pattern and two more security keys. From it he created Larder's AWS Organization and invited the production and staging accounts, which accepted the same day. No SCPs yet: that's Chapter 20. He enabled IAM Identity Center in Frankfurt with its own directory, required MFA at every sign-in, and allowed only security keys and passkeys.

The permission sets and groups are Table 12.2's. Before go-live, he ran the lab's assignment check: it found one assignment to an individual, Dee's PlatformAdmin in staging, left over from setting up her team's sandbox, which became a time-limited request instead (Chapter 14). It also flagged the platform team's standing PlatformAdmin in production, which went on Chapter 14's list. The guardrail's exceptions were rewritten for Identity Center's role names (12.7) and tested.

Engineers moved over in the week of 11 January. Their IAM users were disabled on 18 January and deleted on 1 February, with ops, the prod-operator role and its authenticator-app MFA devices. The security keys from November finally work for everything. The only IAM user left in either account is deploy-bot, which isn't a person, and whose turn comes in Chapter 17.

12.11Lab: read the access matrix

Lab 12 · Assignments to access, and a real sign-in

You'll turn Larder's groups, permission sets and assignments into a per-person view and find the two kinds of risky assignment; then, in a sandbox organisation, create a permission set, a group and an assignment and sign in to the CLI with them. You need Python 3; for steps 3–6, an AWS Organization sandbox with IAM Identity Center enabled (Appendix C), the AWS CLI v2, and your own Identity Center user. Cost: free.

assignments.pyPython 3.8+

# assignments.py · who can do what, in which account, through IAM Identity Center
ACCOUNTS = {"777788889999": "management", "111122223333": "prod", "444455556666": "dev-staging"}

PERMISSION_SETS = {                       # name: (policies, session hours)
    "ViewOnly":      (["ViewOnlyAccess"], 8),
    "Developer":     (["PowerUserAccess", "boundary: team-boundary"], 8),
    "ProdOperator":  (["prod-operator"], 1),
    "DbChange":      (["db-change"], 1),
    "PlatformAdmin": (["AdministratorAccess"], 1),
    "Billing":       (["Billing"], 8),
}

GROUPS = {
    "engineers": {"ben", "cho", "dee", "eli"},
    "on-call":   {"ben", "cho", "eli"},
    "db-owners": {"ben", "eli"},
    "platform":  {"eli"},
    "finance":   {"marco"},
}

ASSIGNMENTS = [                           # (principal, permission set, account)
    ("group:engineers", "ViewOnly",      "111122223333"),
    ("group:engineers", "Developer",     "444455556666"),
    ("group:on-call",   "ProdOperator",  "111122223333"),
    ("group:db-owners", "DbChange",      "111122223333"),
    ("group:platform",  "PlatformAdmin", "777788889999"),
    ("group:platform",  "PlatformAdmin", "111122223333"),
    ("group:platform",  "PlatformAdmin", "444455556666"),
    ("group:finance",   "Billing",       "777788889999"),
    ("user:dee",        "PlatformAdmin", "444455556666"),   # left over from setting up staging
]

def members(principal):
    kind, name = principal.split(":")
    return GROUPS[name] if kind == "group" else {name}

def effective_access():
    access = {}
    for principal, pset, account in ASSIGNMENTS:
        for person in members(principal):
            access.setdefault(person, {}).setdefault(ACCOUNTS[account], set()).add(pset)
    return access

def findings():
    for principal, pset, account in ASSIGNMENTS:
        if principal.startswith("user:"):
            yield f"direct assignment: {principal} → {pset} in {ACCOUNTS[account]} (use a group)"
        if "AdministratorAccess" in PERMISSION_SETS[pset][0] and ACCOUNTS[account] == "prod":
            yield f"standing admin in prod: {principal} → {pset} (make it just-in-time, Chapter 14)"

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for person, accounts in sorted(effective_access().items()):
        line = "  ".join(f"{acct}: {', '.join(sorted(sets))}" for acct, sets in sorted(accounts.items()))
        print(f"{person:<6} {line}")
    print()
    for f in findings():
        print("!", f)
  1. Run it with python3 assignments.py: 
    ben    dev-staging: Developer  prod: DbChange, ProdOperator, ViewOnly
cho    dev-staging: Developer  prod: ProdOperator, ViewOnly
dee    dev-staging: Developer, PlatformAdmin  prod: ViewOnly
eli    dev-staging: Developer, PlatformAdmin  management: PlatformAdmin  prod: DbChange, PlatformAdmin, ProdOperator, ViewOnly
marco  management: Billing

! standing admin in prod: group:platform → PlatformAdmin (make it just-in-time, Chapter 14)
! direct assignment: user:dee → PlatformAdmin in dev-staging (use a group)
    Nine assignments become one line per person. Eli's line is the one to worry about: four ways into production, one of them unlimited.
  2. Make a change and review it. Move Cho from on-call to db-owners (a “mover”, Chapter 13) and run again: one membership change updates every account at once. Then add a fourth account, "555566667777": "data", and give engineers ViewOnly there.
  3. In your sandbox, find the instance (as an administrator of the management or delegated administrator account): 
    INSTANCE=$(aws sso-admin list-instances --query 'Instances[0].InstanceArn' --output text)
STORE=$(aws sso-admin list-instances --query 'Instances[0].IdentityStoreId' --output text)
ACCOUNT=<a member account ID>
  4. Create a permission set, a group and an assignment. 
    PS=$(aws sso-admin create-permission-set --instance-arn $INSTANCE --name LabViewOnly \
  --session-duration PT1H --query PermissionSet.PermissionSetArn --output text)
aws sso-admin attach-managed-policy-to-permission-set --instance-arn $INSTANCE \
  --permission-set-arn $PS --managed-policy-arn arn:aws:iam::aws:policy/job-function/ViewOnlyAccess
G=$(aws identitystore create-group --identity-store-id $STORE --display-name lab-viewers \
  --query GroupId --output text)
ME=$(aws identitystore get-user-id --identity-store-id $STORE --alternate-identifier \
  '{"UniqueAttribute": {"AttributePath": "userName", "AttributeValue": "YOUR-USER-NAME"}}' \
  --query UserId --output text)
aws identitystore create-group-membership --identity-store-id $STORE --group-id $G --member-id UserId=$ME
aws sso-admin create-account-assignment --instance-arn $INSTANCE --target-id $ACCOUNT \
  --target-type AWS_ACCOUNT --permission-set-arn $PS --principal-type GROUP --principal-id $G
    Within a minute, the access portal shows the new account and LabViewOnly.
  5. Sign in from the CLI. Run aws configure sso, choose the account and LabViewOnly, name the profile lab-view, then: 
    aws sts get-caller-identity --profile lab-view --query Arn --output text
aws s3 ls --profile lab-view                                     # works: ViewOnly can list buckets
aws s3 cp s3://SOME-BUCKET/SOME-KEY - --profile lab-view         # denied: it can't read data
    The ARN ends in assumed-role/AWSReservedSSO_LabViewOnly_…/your user name. As an administrator in the member account, aws iam list-roles --path-prefix /aws-reserved/sso.amazonaws.com/ --query 'Roles[].Arn' shows the role Identity Center created.
  6. Clean up: aws sso-admin delete-account-assignment with the same arguments as the create, then aws sso-admin delete-permission-set --instance-arn $INSTANCE --permission-set-arn $PS, then aws identitystore delete-group --identity-store-id $STORE --group-id $G. The role in the member account disappears with the assignment.

12.12Common mistakes

MistakeWhat happensInstead
Keeping IAM users “just in case” after SSOLong-lived credentials and a second way in nobody reviews.Delete them; keep only designed break-glass access (Chapter 14).
An existing workload account as the management accountA workload compromise controls the organisation; SCPs can't restrict it.A new, empty management account.
Assigning permission sets to individualsExceptions pile up unseen.Groups only; review the matrix.
ReadOnlyAccess for everyoneEveryone can read all data and secrets' metadata.ViewOnlyAccess, plus narrow data access where needed.
Long sessions for powerful permission setsStolen or forgotten sessions stay useful for hours.One hour for admin and production change; longer for viewing.

✓Chapter summary

  • IAM Identity Center gives people one sign-in, strong MFA and short role sessions in every account; it replaces IAM users for humans.
  • Account access needs an organisation instance; create the organisation from a new, empty management account.
  • People come from an identity source: Identity Center's directory, AD, or an external IdP with SAML and SCIM.
  • A permission set describes a job; an account assignment gives it to a group in an account; Identity Center creates AWSReservedSSO_… roles.
  • Assign to groups, never individuals; review the resulting matrix.
  • The console and CLI share one sign-in; CloudTrail shows role and person; guardrails must match SSO role names with wildcards.

Key terms

IAM Identity Center
AWS's workforce sign-in service for accounts and applications.
Organisation instance
An Identity Center instance in an organisation, able to grant AWS account access.
Identity source
Where Identity Center's users and groups come from.
Permission set
A template of policies and session length that becomes a role in each assigned account.
Account assignment
A user or group, a permission set and an account.
AWS access portal
The page where people sign in and choose an account and permission set.
Attributes for access control
Identity attributes passed into sessions as tags, for ABAC.

Check your understanding

  1. What three things does an account assignment connect, and what does Identity Center create as a result? recall

    Show answer

    A user or group, a permission set and an AWS account. It creates (and maintains) an IAM role named AWSReservedSSO_PermissionSet_suffix in that account, trusted only by Identity Center.

  2. Why does single sign-on for AWS accounts usually start with creating an organisation, and why from a new account? explain

    Show answer

    Only an organisation instance of Identity Center can grant access to AWS accounts. The management account can't be restricted by SCPs and controls all members, so it should hold no workloads; a new, empty account is the simplest way to guarantee that.

  3. An engineer needs to read one bucket's objects in production for a week. How do you grant it without changing ViewOnly for everyone? apply

    Show answer

    Create (or reuse) a narrow permission set for that bucket, assign it to a small group in production, add the engineer to the group with an end date, and remove them after the week, ideally through the time-limited process of Chapter 14.

  4. After moving to Identity Center, a guardrail's exception for role/db-change no longer lets the database owners make changes. Why, and what's the fix? apply

    Show answer

    Their sessions now come from AWSReservedSSO_DbChange_suffix under the /aws-reserved/sso.amazonaws.com/ path. Change the exception to match that pattern with wildcards, and test it with the policy simulator or a real session.

  5. Design the permission sets and groups for a company with three product teams, a platform team, finance and support, and four accounts. design

    Show answer

    Roughly: ViewOnly for all engineers in production; Developer (with a boundary) for each team in development and staging; ProdOperator for on-call, time-limited; PlatformAdmin for platform, time-limited in production; Billing for finance in the management account; support gets application tools, not AWS access. All assigned to groups, ideally with a team attribute for ABAC.

  6. Compare how a person gets admin rights in one subscription on Azure with one account on AWS. compare

    Show answer

    Azure: their Entra ID group gets a role assignment (such as Owner or Contributor) at the subscription scope; they use their own identity. AWS: their group gets an account assignment of an admin permission set; they sign in through the portal and receive a session of the role Identity Center created in that account.
