Debugging “access denied”

Every engineer who works with AWS meets “access denied” weekly. Most people respond by adding permissions until it goes away, which is how s3:* on * ends up in production. This chapter gives you a method instead: find out exactly who asked for exactly what, read what AWS tells you about why it said no, confirm it in CloudTrail, and fix the one policy that's actually responsible.

By the end you can

  • Follow a seven-step method from an error message to the policy that caused it.
  • Read the reason in an AWS access-denied message, and decode an encoded one.
  • Find denied calls in CloudTrail, including calls one service made for another.
  • Tell real permission problems from look-alikes: expired tokens, wrong accounts, clock skew.
  • Fix a denial with the smallest correct change.

Before you start

  • The evaluation order (Chapter 10), roles and sessions (Chapter 8), reading policies (Chapter 9).

At Larder

On Tuesday 8 December 2026, Dispatch's new least-privilege policy from Chapter 9 went to staging for its trial evening. At 18:00, Engine A started planning tomorrow's van routes, read its settings from config/ without trouble, and then failed to write a single route plan to routes/: AccessDenied, for every van.

The policy plainly allowed s3:PutObject on routes/*. Ben's first suggestion in the chat was to put s3:* back “just for staging”. Eli asked for twenty minutes instead. Section 11.9 shows what he found, and it wasn't in the policy anyone was looking at.

11.1A method

“Access denied” is a symptom, and like any symptom it has several possible causes. Guessing (adding permissions until it works) usually fixes it by granting far more than needed, and sometimes doesn't fix it at all, because the cause was never a missing permission. Work through it in order:

  1. Who am I? Confirm the exact principal making the call: which role, which session, which account (11.2).
  2. What exactly was asked? The action, the resource ARN, the region. Not “S3”: s3:PutObject on arn:aws:s3:::larder-dispatch-staging/routes/2026-12-09/van-1.json.
  3. Read the error all the way to the end. It increasingly names the type of policy that said no (11.3).
  4. Find it in CloudTrail, including any calls made on your behalf by another service (11.4).
  5. Walk the evaluation order from Chapter 10 for that request: deny, RCP, SCP, resource policy, identity policy, boundary, session policy.
  6. Rule out look-alikes: expired credentials, the wrong account or region, clock skew, a missing object (11.6).
  7. Fix the one responsible policy with the smallest change, validate it, and test the original request again (11.7).

Key idea

Find out exactly who asked for exactly what, then let AWS tell you which policy said no. Don't add a permission until you know which one is missing.

11.2Who am I, really?

A surprising share of permission problems are identity problems: the call wasn't made by who you think. Leftover environment variables from an earlier session, a default profile pointing at another account, a container that picked up the node's role instead of its own (Chapter 8, 8.8). Always start with:

aws sts get-caller-identity
{
    "UserId": "AROAEXAMPLEID:engine-a-7f9c",
    "Account": "444455556666",
    "Arn": "arn:aws:sts::444455556666:assumed-role/dispatch-task/engine-a-7f9c"
}

This call always works, whatever your permissions. For code, log the same thing at start-up: the SDK has the same call. Check the account number as carefully as the role name: production and staging roles often share names.

11.3Read the error

AWS's access-denied messages have become much more helpful. Most services now say who was denied, what they tried, on which resource, and which type of policy is responsible:

Table 11.1 · What the reason says, and where to look

Some services, notably EC2, return an encoded authorization failure message instead of the full detail, because it can reveal information about your policies. Someone with sts:DecodeAuthorizationMessage can decode it:

aws sts decode-authorization-message --encoded-message "$MSG" \
  --query DecodedMessage --output text | python3 -m json.tool

The result shows whether the request was allowed, whether an explicit deny matched, any matched statements, and the request's full context: principal, action, resource and condition keys.

11.4Find it in CloudTrail

CloudTrail records API calls made in your account: who, what, when, from where, and whether it failed. Denied calls are recorded with an errorCode such as AccessDenied or Client.UnauthorizedOperation and, usually, the same errorMessage the caller saw. What to know before you go looking:

  • Management events (creating, changing and deleting resources, and many reads) are recorded by default and searchable for 90 days in the console's Event history or with aws cloudtrail lookup-events, per region.
  • Data events (S3 object reads and writes, Lambda invocations, DynamoDB item calls) are not recorded unless a trail is configured for them, because there are so many. Larder records S3 data events for its production buckets (Chapter 23).
  • Global services such as IAM and STS log in us-east-1 (or the region of the STS endpoint used).
  • Events appear after a delay, typically a few minutes and occasionally up to about 15.

The most useful field for puzzles like Larder's is userIdentity.invokedBy. When S3 needs a KMS key to encrypt an object you upload, S3 calls KMS as you, and CloudTrail records that KMS call with invokedBy: s3.amazonaws.com. If that call is denied, your upload fails with an error from S3, but the cause lives in a different service's event.

11.5Tools that walk the flowchart for you

  • IAM policy simulator (aws iam simulate-principal-policy, or the console): evaluates an existing user's or role's policies for given actions, resources and context keys, and says which statement allowed or denied. It covers identity policies, boundaries and SCPs; resource policies only if you pass them in.
  • IAM Access Analyzer policy validation: catches impossible policies before you blame AWS: misspelled actions, actions that don't support the resource you named (Chapter 9).
  • Last accessed information (in IAM's Access Advisor tab): shows which services a role has actually used, which helps when narrowing a policy without breaking it.

11.7Fixing it well

Once you know which policy said no, the fix is usually one statement. Keep it small:

  • Add the exact action on the exact resource, not a wildcard. If the message says kms:GenerateDataKey on one key, that's what to allow, on that key.
  • Fix it where it belongs. If a guardrail blocked something legitimate, change the guardrail's exception deliberately, or use the approved path (such as Larder's db-change role), rather than removing the guardrail.
  • Sometimes the fix isn't a permission at all. A staging setting that doesn't match production, a request to the wrong region, a missing object.
  • Validate, then repeat the original request. Also try one request that should still fail, so you know you didn't open more than you meant to.
  • Write it down in the commit message or ticket: which call, which reason, which statement changed. The next person will thank you.

Watch out: “just for now” wildcards

A wildcard added to make an error go away under pressure is almost never removed: the error is gone, so nobody looks again. Larder's s3:* on * started exactly that way in April. If you must unblock something urgently, add the narrowest statement you can, with a Sid that says it's temporary and a ticket to replace it.

On Azure

Role assignments can take several minutes to take effect, and a cached token may not show a new group membership until it's refreshed. When a correct-looking assignment “doesn't work”, sign out and in again (or get a new token) before changing anything. For storage data, remember that management roles such as Contributor don't include data access: you need a data role such as Storage Blob Data Reader.

On Google Cloud

Google's errors name the exact permission that was missing, which makes the first step easy. Policy Troubleshooter then shows every binding on the resource and its ancestors that could grant it, any deny policy that blocks it, and whether conditions matched. Like AWS, it can only explain what's in IAM; organisation policy constraints and VPC Service Controls produce their own, differently worded errors.

11.9At Larder: the error was in another service

At Larder: solved

Eli's twenty minutes went like this. Engine A's logs showed the identity was right (dispatch-task, in the staging account). The upload's error ended with the reason in Figure 11.1: the denied action was kms:GenerateDataKey, not anything in S3. CloudTrail in the staging account confirmed it: one denied KMS call per van, each invokedBy: s3.amazonaws.com. The staging bucket, it turned out, had been set in 2025 to encrypt new objects by default with a customer managed KMS key, during an experiment nobody remembered. Production's bucket uses S3-managed keys. The config/ files read fine because the deploy pipeline uploads them with S3-managed encryption explicitly; Engine A's uploads took the bucket's default.

So the policy was right, and staging was wrong. Eli changed the staging bucket's default encryption to match production, the team re-ran the evening's planning, and every route plan was written. Ben's s3:* was never needed. Two follow-ups went on the list: compare staging and production bucket settings automatically, and decide properly whether Larder wants customer managed keys at all (Chapter 18), in which case Dispatch's policy will need kms:GenerateDataKey and kms:Decrypt on that one key.

11.10Lab: from error to cause

Lab 11 · Find, read and decode denials

You'll run a script that finds denied calls in CloudTrail events and says why, first on Larder's staging evening, then on denials you cause yourself; decode an encoded EC2 message; and ask the policy simulator. You need Python 3; for steps 3–7, the AWS CLI v2 signed in to a sandbox account as an administrator, and the role from Lab 8. Cost: free; no instances are created (the EC2 call is a dry run).

denied.pyPython 3.8+

# denied.py · find the denied calls in CloudTrail events and say why
import json, sys
from collections import Counter

def events(path):
    text = open(path).read().strip()
    if text.startswith("["):                             # aws cloudtrail lookup-events ... --output json
        return [json.loads(x) if isinstance(x, str) else x for x in json.loads(text)]
    if '"Records"' in text[:200]:
        return json.loads(text)["Records"]               # a CloudTrail log file from S3
    return [json.loads(line) for line in text.splitlines() if line.strip()]   # one event per line

def who(e):
    u = e["userIdentity"]
    name = u.get("sessionContext", {}).get("sessionIssuer", {}).get("arn") or u.get("arn", u.get("type"))
    return name + (f" (via {u['invokedBy']})" if "invokedBy" in u else "")

def why(e):
    msg = e.get("errorMessage", "")
    if " because " in msg:
        return msg.split(" because ", 1)[1].split(". Encoded")[0]
    if " with an explicit deny " in msg:
        return "explicit deny " + msg.split(" with an explicit deny ", 1)[1]
    if "Encoded authorization failure message" in msg:
        return "encoded: run aws sts decode-authorization-message"
    return msg or "(no message)"

DENIED = ("AccessDenied", "UnauthorizedOperation", "Forbidden")
counts, first = Counter(), {}
for e in events(sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "sample-events.jsonl"):
    if not any(d in e.get("errorCode", "") for d in DENIED):
        continue
    key = (who(e), f"{e['eventSource'].split('.')[0]}:{e['eventName']}", why(e))
    counts[key] += 1
    first.setdefault(key, e["eventTime"])

for (principal, action, reason), n in sorted(counts.items(), key=lambda kv: first[kv[0]]):
    print(f"{first[(principal, action, reason)]}  {n}×  {action}\n    who: {principal}\n    why: {reason}")

✓Chapter summary

  • Work in order: who, what, read the error, CloudTrail, the evaluation order, look-alikes, smallest fix.
  • aws sts get-caller-identity settles “who am I?”, whatever your permissions.
  • Access-denied messages name the principal, action, resource and usually the type of policy responsible; EC2's encoded messages can be decoded.
  • CloudTrail records denials; data events need a trail; invokedBy reveals calls made for you by other services.
  • Expired tokens, wrong accounts or regions, clock skew and missing objects look like permission problems.
  • Fix the one responsible policy with the narrowest change, and test both what should and shouldn't work.

Key terms

CloudTrail
AWS's record of API calls in an account.
Management event
A control-plane call (create, change, delete, many reads); recorded by default.
Data event
A data-plane call such as S3 object access; recorded only if a trail asks for it.
invokedBy
The service that made a call on a principal's behalf.
Encoded authorization message
A denial's details in encoded form, decoded with sts:DecodeAuthorizationMessage.
Policy simulator
AWS's tool for evaluating a principal's policies against chosen requests.
Last accessed information
Which services and actions an identity has actually used, and when.

Check your understanding

  1. What's the first command to run when a script gets “access denied”, and why? recall

    Show answer

    aws sts get-caller-identity (or its SDK equivalent from inside the script): it shows which principal, in which account, is really making the calls, and works regardless of permissions.

  2. An error ends “with an explicit deny in a service control policy”. Will adding the action to the role's policy help? apply

    Show answer

    No. An explicit deny wins over any allow, and an SCP is set by the organisation. The fix is to use an approved path or ask the platform team for a deliberate exception.

  3. An upload to S3 fails with “access denied”, and the role clearly has s3:PutObject on the prefix. Name two causes outside the role's S3 permissions. explain

    Show answer

    The bucket encrypts with a customer managed KMS key, and the role lacks kms:GenerateDataKey on it (or the key policy doesn't allow it); a bucket policy, VPC endpoint policy, SCP or RCP denies the request; or the request comes from a different identity or account than expected.

  4. You can't find a denied S3 GetObject in CloudTrail's Event history. Why might that be? explain

    Show answer

    GetObject is a data event, which isn't in Event history and is recorded only by a trail configured for S3 data events on that bucket. It might also simply not have arrived yet, or be in another region's history.

  5. Design a weekly report that would have caught Larder's staging problem before the trial evening. design

    Show answer

    Run a denial summary like denied.py over each account's CloudTrail events daily and post new principal/action/reason combinations to the team channel; and compare key settings (bucket encryption, public access, versioning) between staging and production buckets, flagging differences.

  6. Compare what you'd look at first for a denied call on AWS and on Google Cloud. compare

    Show answer

    AWS: the error's reason (which policy type) and the CloudTrail event, then the simulator. Google Cloud: the missing permission named in the error, then Policy Troubleshooter for that principal, permission and resource, which shows bindings and deny policies.
