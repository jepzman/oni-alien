Part V · Many accounts

Cross-account access and resource sharing

Accounts are boundaries, and work still has to cross them: your backup account copies production's snapshots, a supplier drops a file into your bucket every night, a partner's system reads an export. This chapter shows the two ways across, roles and resource-based policies, explains the confused deputy problem that every vendor integration must solve, and ends with a checklist for letting another account in, and getting it out again.

By the end you can

  • Choose between assuming a role in another account and granting access in a resource policy.
  • Set up cross-account access with both sides' policies, including KMS keys.
  • Explain the confused deputy problem, and stop it with sts:ExternalId or aws:SourceArn.
  • Review a vendor's integration request, and offboard a vendor cleanly.
  • Share resources with AWS RAM instead of sharing access.

Before you start

  • Cross-account evaluation (Chapter 10, 10.5); trust policies (Chapter 8); key policies (Chapter 18).

At Larder

In March 2027, Larder chose how to connect WMS B, its new warehouse system: webhooks for stock changes, an API for pick lists, and a nightly file for finance by 06:00 (ADR 0018). For the file, WMS B's onboarding guide was simple: “Create an IAM role in your account that trusts our AWS account, give it permission to write to your bucket, and paste the role ARN into our settings.” Ben's team did exactly that in an afternoon.

Eli, reviewing the change, asked one question the guide had marked as optional: where's the external ID? Section 21.4 explains why that one field is the difference between “WMS B can write into Larder's bucket” and “any WMS B customer can”.

21.1Two ways across

To let a principal in account A use a resource in account B, you have two mechanisms, and both need the other side's consent (Chapter 10, 10.5):

21.2Cross-account roles

A cross-account role is an ordinary role (Chapter 8) whose trust policy names a principal in another account. Name the specific role that will call, not the whole account: trusting arn:aws:iam::999911112222:root delegates the choice to every administrator in that account (Chapter 10, 10.4). Then the caller's own account must allow it to call sts:AssumeRole on your role's ARN. Both sides consent, and both sides can revoke.

The session's actions are logged in your account's CloudTrail, with the caller's original identity visible in the AssumeRole event and, if they set one, in the source identity (Chapter 8, 8.5). Inside one organisation, people rarely need cross-account roles at all: Identity Center gives them sessions directly in each account (Chapter 12).

21.3Resource-based policies across accounts

A resource policy can name principals in other accounts, or a whole organisation with aws:PrincipalOrgID (Chapter 22). Three details catch people out:

  • Encryption keys travel too. If the bucket or secret uses a customer managed KMS key, the key policy must also let the other account use the key (Chapter 18). Data encrypted with an AWS managed key, such as aws/s3, can't be shared across accounts at all; that's one reason to use a customer managed key.
  • Object ownership. Objects another account uploads into your bucket should become yours: keep S3 Object Ownership at “bucket owner enforced” (the default for new buckets), so ACLs can't give the uploader control.
  • Both sides still apply: the caller's account must allow the action in its identity policy, and its SCPs and your RCPs must not deny it.

21.4The confused deputy

A confused deputy is a service with legitimate power that's tricked into using it for someone who shouldn't have it. Vendors that act in customers' AWS accounts are perfect deputies: WMS B's service assumes a role in each customer's account, using whatever role ARN that customer typed into its settings. Now imagine another WMS B customer, Mallory, who types in Larder's role ARN. ARNs aren't secret; they're in logs, screenshots and support tickets. WMS B's service assumes the role, because Larder's trust policy trusts WMS B, and delivers Mallory's file into Larder's bucket, or, if the role could read, Larder's data to Mallory.

The fix is an external ID: a value the vendor generates, unique to each customer, shows in that customer's settings, and passes on every AssumeRole call it makes for them. The customer requires it in the trust policy:

wmsb-nightly-file trust policyproduction account

{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [{
    "Effect": "Allow",
    "Principal": { "AWS": "arn:aws:iam::999911112222:role/wmsb-file-delivery" },
    "Action": "sts:AssumeRole",
    "Condition": { "StringEquals": { "sts:ExternalId": "wmsb-3c9e51a07d2b4f18" } }
  }]
}

The external ID isn't a password; it can appear in a screenshot. What makes it work is that the vendor chooses it per tenant and never lets a tenant choose or change it, so Mallory can't make WMS B send Larder's ID. If a vendor lets customers type in their own external ID, it protects nothing; the lab shows why.

Key idea

When a vendor acts for many customers, trusting the vendor isn't enough. You must also know which customer it's acting for: that's what the external ID tells you.

AWS services are deputies too. When S3 sends events to your SNS topic, or a service assumes a role you created for it, condition the topic policy or trust policy on aws:SourceArn (the exact bucket or resource) or aws:SourceAccount (your account), so the service can't be pointed at your resource on someone else's behalf.

21.5Letting a vendor in, and out

  1. Trust the vendor's specific role if they publish one, not their whole account.
  2. Require their external ID, and check it's generated by them, per customer.
  3. Grant only the job: write to one prefix, read one export, never *, never IAM.
  4. Prefer push over pull where you can: a vendor writing into an inbound bucket you then process exposes less than a vendor reading from your systems.
  5. Tag and record each external access with an owner and the contract it belongs to; IAM Access Analyzer will list it as external access (Chapter 23).
  6. Offboard deliberately when the contract ends: delete the role or bucket policy statement, check CloudTrail for its last use, and resolve the Access Analyzer finding.

21.6Sharing resources with AWS RAM

Some things are better shared than accessed. AWS Resource Access Manager (RAM) shares a resource itself with other accounts or with OUs in your organisation: VPC subnets, transit gateways, Route 53 Resolver rules, IPAM pools (Handbook 1, Chapters 10–11). The other account sees the resource as if it were nearly its own, but the owner keeps control and can unshare it. Inside an organisation, shares can be accepted automatically. Use RAM where it exists for a resource type; reach for cross-account roles and resource policies for everything else.

On Azure

Azure Lighthouse lets a service provider's users manage resources in a customer's tenant with role assignments the customer approves and can remove, without guest accounts. Multi-tenant applications work differently: the customer's administrator consents to the app, which creates a service principal in the customer's tenant that the customer controls.

On Google Cloud

IAM bindings can name principals from anywhere, including other organisations' service accounts, which makes cross-organisation access a one-line grant, and a one-line mistake. The domain-restricted sharing organisation policy limits which domains' identities may appear in your allow policies.

21.8At Larder: three crossings

At Larder: solved

WMS B's nightly file. WMS B did generate an external ID per customer; its guide just called it optional. Ben's team added it to the trust policy, narrowed the principal from WMS B's account to the delivery role WMS B publishes, and narrowed the permissions from the whole bucket to s3:PutObject on larder-wmsb-inbound/nightly/*. WMS B can write the file and nothing else: not read it back, not list, not delete. The first file arrived at 05:12 on 20 April, the morning after Zuid moved to WMS B.

The backup account. Since June (Chapter 20), AWS Backup copies production's database snapshots every night into a vault in the backup account, whose access policy accepts copies only from Larder's organisation, and whose vault lock means nobody, including Larder's own administrators, can delete a copy before its retention ends. The snapshots' customer managed key (Chapter 18) lets the backup account use it for exactly that. A compromised production account can no longer take the backups with it.

StockPilot's goodbye. The old warehouse system read stock exports through a bucket policy naming its role. On 15 September 2027, the day StockPilot was switched off, the statement was deleted, CloudTrail confirmed its last read was on 14 September, and the external access finding in Access Analyzer was closed with a note pointing to ADR 0016.

21.9Lab: confuse a deputy

Lab 21 · A vendor, two tenants, one role

You'll simulate a vendor that assumes roles in customers' accounts, let another customer point it at Larder's role, and see which trust designs stop it. Optionally, you'll require an external ID on a real role. You need Python 3; for the optional part, the AWS CLI in a sandbox account. Cost: free.

deputy.pyPython 3.8+

# deputy.py · the confused deputy: a vendor that assumes roles in its customers' accounts
import secrets

VENDOR = "arn:aws:iam::999911112222:root"                     # WMS B's AWS account (example ID)
LARDER_ROLE = "arn:aws:iam::111122223333:role/wmsb-nightly-file"

def trust_allows(trust, caller, external_id):
    """STS's check of the role's trust policy for sts:AssumeRole."""
    if caller != trust["principal"]:
        return False
    want = trust.get("external_id")
    return want is None or external_id == want

class WMSB:
    """The vendor's service. Customers configure a role ARN; the vendor assumes it nightly."""
    def __init__(self, customer_may_choose_id=False):
        self.tenants, self.free = {}, customer_may_choose_id

    def onboard(self, tenant, chosen_id=None):
        generated = "wmsb-" + secrets.token_hex(8)                  # vendor-generated, per tenant
        self.tenants[tenant] = {"external_id": chosen_id if (self.free and chosen_id) else generated}
        return self.tenants[tenant]["external_id"]

    def set_role(self, tenant, role_arn):
        self.tenants[tenant]["role_arn"] = role_arn

    def nightly_job(self, tenant, roles):
        t = self.tenants[tenant]
        ok = trust_allows(roles[t["role_arn"]], VENDOR, t["external_id"])
        return f"{tenant:<8} → {t['role_arn'].split('/')[-1]}: " + ("ASSUMED" if ok else "denied")

def scenario(title, trust_for, free=False):
    print(title)
    wmsb = WMSB(customer_may_choose_id=free)
    larder_id = wmsb.onboard("larder", chosen_id="larder")
    wmsb.onboard("mallory", chosen_id="larder" if free else None)   # Mallory is another WMS B customer
    roles = {LARDER_ROLE: trust_for(larder_id)}
    wmsb.set_role("larder", LARDER_ROLE)
    wmsb.set_role("mallory", LARDER_ROLE)                          # ...who points her tenant at Larder's role
    for tenant in ("larder", "mallory"):
        print("   ", wmsb.nightly_job(tenant, roles))

scenario("1. trust WMS B's account, no external ID",
         lambda _: {"principal": VENDOR})
scenario("2. trust WMS B's account + the external ID WMS B generated for Larder",
         lambda larder_id: {"principal": VENDOR, "external_id": larder_id})
scenario("3. same, but WMS B lets customers type their own external ID",
         lambda larder_id: {"principal": VENDOR, "external_id": larder_id}, free=True)
  1. Run it with python3 deputy.py: 
    1. trust WMS B's account, no external ID
    larder   → wmsb-nightly-file: ASSUMED
    mallory  → wmsb-nightly-file: ASSUMED

  2. Think like Mallory. In scenario 2, what would she need to get into Larder's account? (Larder's external ID, and a way to make WMS B use it for her tenant. The first might leak; the second is WMS B's job to prevent.)

21.10Common mistakes

MistakeWhat happensInstead

✓Chapter summary

  • Cross the boundary by assuming a role in the other account, or with a resource-based policy naming the other account's principal. Both sides must allow.
  • Trust specific roles, not whole accounts; customer managed KMS keys must allow the other account too.
  • A confused deputy is a trusted service tricked into acting for the wrong party. Stop vendors with a vendor-generated external ID, and AWS services with aws:SourceArn / aws:SourceAccount.
  • Let vendors in narrowly, prefer push over pull, record each external access, and offboard on time.
  • Share resources themselves with AWS RAM where it supports them.

Key terms

Cross-account role
A role whose trust policy names a principal in another account.
Confused deputy
A privileged service tricked into acting for someone without that privilege.
External ID
A vendor-generated, per-customer value required in a trust policy.
aws:SourceArn / aws:SourceAccount
Condition keys naming the resource or account a service acts for.
AWS RAM
Resource Access Manager: shares resources across accounts and OUs.
Vault lock
An AWS Backup setting that stops anyone deleting backups before their retention ends.

Check your understanding

  1. Name the two ways to give account A's role access to a bucket in account B, and what each side must allow. recall

    Show answer

    Assume a role in B (A allows sts:AssumeRole on it; B's role trusts A's role and grants the bucket access), or a bucket policy in B naming A's role (B's bucket policy allows it; A's identity policy allows the S3 actions).

  2. Explain why an external ID works even though it isn't secret. explain

    Show answer

    The protection comes from who chooses it: the vendor generates it per customer and always sends the ID belonging to the tenant it's acting for. Another customer can't make the vendor send yours, so knowing it doesn't help them.

  3. A bucket in the production account, encrypted with aws/s3, must be readable by the analytics account. What's in the way, and what do you change? apply

    Show answer

    Objects encrypted with an AWS managed key can't be decrypted from another account. Switch the bucket to a customer managed key whose key policy allows the analytics role to decrypt (via S3), then grant it in the bucket policy and in the analytics role's identity policy.

  4. A vendor's onboarding guide says: trust our account, attach ReadOnlyAccess. Review it. design

    Show answer

    Trust their specific role, require their generated external ID, replace ReadOnlyAccess (which reads all data) with the few actions and resources the integration needs, tag the role with owner and contract, and plan the offboarding.

  5. Why put backup copies in a separate account with a vault lock, rather than just another region? explain

    Show answer

    An attacker with administrator access to the production account can delete backups in any of its regions. A separate account with its own access, and a vault lock that forbids early deletion even by administrators, survives the production account's compromise.

  6. Compare how a service provider gets access to a customer's environment on Azure and on AWS. compare

    Show answer

    Azure: Azure Lighthouse delegates role assignments on the customer's subscriptions to the provider's tenant, approved and removable by the customer. AWS: the customer creates a role trusting the provider's role, with the provider's external ID, and narrow permissions.
