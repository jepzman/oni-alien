Part II · IAM on AWS

8

Users, groups and roles

AWS IAM gives you three kinds of identity. Users are the oldest and easiest to understand, and the one to avoid. Roles are the one to build everything on: an identity with no password and no permanent key, which people, services and other accounts borrow for an hour at a time. This chapter explains how a role is put together, what happens when you assume one, and how to write the trust policy that decides who can.

By the end you can

  • Explain why IAM users are a legacy pattern, and when one is still acceptable.
  • Describe a role as a trust policy plus permission policies.
  • Assume a role and read the temporary credentials and session it produces.
  • Write trust policies for people, AWS services and other accounts, with conditions.
  • Control who can hand a role to a service, with iam:PassRole.

Before you start

  • Short-lived credentials (Chapter 3); the account and root user (Chapter 7).
  • Reading policies (Chapter 9). This chapter writes the other half: trust policies.

8.1IAM users

An IAM user is a long-lived identity inside one account. It can have a console password and up to two access keys for the CLI and SDKs. An access key is a pair: an access key ID starting with AKIA, and a secret access key, shown once when it's created. Two keys are allowed so you can rotate: create the new one, switch everything to it, then delete the old.

Users get permissions from policies attached to them directly or, better, to groups they belong to.

8.2Groups

An IAM group is a set of users that share policies: attach a policy to developers, and every user in it gets those permissions. Groups are simple, with three limits worth knowing: a group isn't a principal (you can't name it in a trust or resource policy, or sign in as it); groups can't contain other groups; and they only hold IAM users, not roles or federated people. Groups in your identity provider are a different thing, and much more useful (Chapter 12).

8.3Why users are a legacy pattern

IAM users were AWS's original answer to “how do people get in?”, and they still work. But they carry every problem from Part I:

  • Long-lived credentials. Passwords and access keys last until someone deletes them. Larder's credential report shows the result: a key two and a half years old, and three more belonging to people who've left (Chapter 3).
  • One account at a time. A user exists in one account. With ten accounts, a person needs ten users, ten passwords and ten MFA devices, or you build cross-account roles anyway.
  • A separate directory. Joiners and leavers in your company's directory don't reach IAM. Someone must remember to create and delete users by hand (Chapter 13).

The modern answer is: people sign in through IAM Identity Center or another identity provider and get temporary role sessions (Chapter 12); workloads on AWS use roles provided by the platform (Chapter 15); workloads outside AWS exchange their own platform's tokens for role sessions (Chapter 17). An IAM user is acceptable only where nothing else works: a third-party tool that can only take an access key, or an emergency break-glass account (Chapter 14), and then with a narrow policy, monitoring and rotation.

8.4Roles

An IAM role is an identity with permissions but no credentials of its own: no password, no access keys. Instead, trusted principals assume it and receive temporary credentials for a session. A role is defined by two kinds of policy, and keeping them apart is most of the battle:

  • The trust policy is a resource-based policy on the role itself. Its Principal names who may call sts:AssumeRole (or its web identity and SAML variants) on this role, and its conditions say under what circumstances.
  • The permission policies are ordinary identity-based policies (Chapter 9): what a session of this role may do.

Roles serve every kind of principal from Chapter 1: people (through single sign-on or, as at Larder this month, from IAM users), AWS services acting for you (an ECS task, a Lambda function), pipelines outside AWS, and principals in other accounts.

8.5Assuming a role

Assuming a role is an API call to AWS STS, the Security Token Service:

aws sts assume-role \
  --role-arn arn:aws:iam::111122223333:role/prod-operator \
  --role-session-name eli \
  --source-identity eli

STS checks the trust policy and the caller's own permissions (Chapter 10), and returns temporary credentials:

{
  "Credentials": {
    "AccessKeyId": "ASIAQEXAMPLE7RTHUNA",
    "SecretAccessKey": "…",
    "SessionToken": "IQoJb3JpZ2luX2VjE…",
    "Expiration": "2026-12-07T10:42:13+00:00"
  },
  "AssumedRoleUser": {
    "Arn": "arn:aws:sts::111122223333:assumed-role/prod-operator/eli"
  },
  "SourceIdentity": "eli"
}

Things to notice:

  • Temporary keys start with ASIA, long-lived ones with AKIA. Seeing AKIA in a log or a leak tells you a long-lived key is involved.
  • All three parts are needed: key ID, secret and session token. Tools pass them as AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID, AWS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY and AWS_SESSION_TOKEN.
  • The session expires: by default after one hour; you can ask for 15 minutes up to the role's maximum session duration, which you set between 1 and 12 hours.
  • The session has its own ARN: assumed-role/role-name/session-name. That's what CloudTrail records, so choose session names that identify the person or job.
  • Source identity is a value set when the role is first assumed, which can't be changed afterwards and is recorded in CloudTrail for every action in the session, even across further role assumptions. It's how you keep Chapter 1's accountability when people share a role.
Role chainingUsing a role session to assume another role is called role chaining. AWS limits chained sessions to one hour, whatever the maximum session duration says.

8.6Writing trust policies

A trust policy has the same grammar as any policy (Chapter 9), with a Principal and an action from STS. The principal says what kind of thing may assume the role:

Table 8.1 · Who can be trusted to assume a role
PrincipalExampleMeaning
A specific user or role{"AWS": "arn:aws:iam::111122223333:user/eli"}exactly that identity
An account{"AWS": "arn:aws:iam::111122223333:root"}any principal in that account whose own policies allow it; the account's administrators decide who
An AWS service{"Service": "ecs-tasks.amazonaws.com"}that service, acting for you (8.7)
A federated provider{"Federated": "arn:aws:iam::111122223333:oidc-provider/token.actions.githubusercontent.com"}people or workloads authenticated by that SAML or OIDC provider (Chapter 5, Chapter 17)

Despite the name, arn:aws:iam::111122223333:root in a principal doesn't mean the root user. It means “the account”, handing the decision to that account's IAM policies. Conditions then narrow the trust:

prod-operator-trust.jsontrust policy

{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [{
    "Sid": "EngineersWithMfaAndTheirOwnName",
    "Effect": "Allow",
    "Principal": { "AWS": [
      "arn:aws:iam::111122223333:user/ben",
      "arn:aws:iam::111122223333:user/eli"
    ] },
    "Action": ["sts:AssumeRole", "sts:SetSourceIdentity"],
    "Condition": {
      "Bool": { "aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent": "true" },
      "StringEquals": { "sts:SourceIdentity": "${aws:username}" }
    }
  }]
}

Read aloud: “Allow Ben's and Eli's users to assume this role, only if they signed in with MFA, and only if they set the source identity to their own user name.” The last condition stops anyone from recording a colleague's name. Other conditions you'll meet: sts:ExternalId for third parties (Chapter 21), aws:SourceAccount and aws:SourceArn for services (8.7), and the sub and aud claims of OIDC tokens (Chapter 17).

Watch out: trust that's too wide

"Principal": {"AWS": "*"} without strong conditions lets any AWS account in the world try to assume your role. Trusting another company's whole account trusts every administrator they have. And trusting a federated provider without conditions on which identities (such as which GitHub repository) lets anyone that provider authenticates in. A role's permissions are only as safe as its trust policy.

8.7Roles for AWS services, and iam:PassRole

Many services act for you: an ECS task reads from S3, a Lambda function writes to DynamoDB, CloudFormation creates resources. Each does it by assuming a role you give it:

  • A service role is an ordinary role whose trust policy names the service (ecs-tasks.amazonaws.com, lambda.amazonaws.com). You write its permissions.
  • An instance profile is the wrapper that lets an EC2 instance use a role (Chapter 15).
  • A service-linked role is created and managed by a service for its own needs, with a fixed policy. You can see it but not edit it.

Handing a role to a service is itself a permission: iam:PassRole. Whoever can launch a task and pass any role can run code with that role's permissions, including an administrator role. That's one of the classic ways to escalate privileges in AWS. Limit it to the roles a person or pipeline actually needs to pass, and to the service that should receive them:

{
  "Effect": "Allow",
  "Action": "iam:PassRole",
  "Resource": "arn:aws:iam::111122223333:role/dispatch-task",
  "Condition": { "StringEquals": { "iam:PassedToService": "ecs-tasks.amazonaws.com" } }
}

Service role trust policies should also say which of your resources may use them, with aws:SourceAccount or aws:SourceArn, so a service acting for someone else can't be tricked into using your role (the “confused deputy” of Chapter 21).

8.8How tools find credentials

The AWS CLI and SDKs look for credentials in a fixed order, the credential provider chain, and use the first they find. Knowing the order explains most “why is it using those credentials?” puzzles:

Table 8.2 · Where the CLI and SDKs look, roughly in order
SourceExampleTypical use
Command-line options--profile prodchoosing a profile per command
Environment variablesAWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID, AWS_SESSION_TOKEN, AWS_PROFILEscripts, CI, temporary sessions
Web identity token fileAWS_WEB_IDENTITY_TOKEN_FILE + AWS_ROLE_ARNKubernetes pods (Chapter 16)
Config and credentials files~/.aws/config: SSO profiles, role_arn + source_profile, credential_processpeople on laptops
Container credentialsAWS_CONTAINER_CREDENTIALS_RELATIVE_URIECS tasks
Instance metadataIMDSv2 on EC2EC2 instances (Chapter 15)

When in doubt, aws sts get-caller-identity tells you who you are right now, and works whatever your permissions.

8.9Changing roles from the console

People with IAM users can also switch role in the AWS console: the console assumes the role for them, with MFA if the trust policy requires it, and shows the role's name in the top bar. A CLI profile does the same:

[profile prod-operator]
role_arn = arn:aws:iam::111122223333:role/prod-operator
source_profile = eli
mfa_serial = arn:aws:iam::111122223333:mfa/eli-phone
role_session_name = eli
duration_seconds = 3600

The CLI then asks for a six-digit code before assuming the role. One limit to know: MFA for API and CLI calls accepts only one-time-code devices (an authenticator app or a hardware TOTP token), not FIDO2 security keys or passkeys, which work for console sign-in only. With single sign-on (Chapter 12), this whole step disappears: people sign in once, with whatever factor the identity provider requires, choose an account and a permission set, and get a role session directly.

On Azure

Azure has no “assume role”: you assign roles directly to a user, group or managed identity at a scope. Temporary elevation comes from Privileged Identity Management (PIM), where an eligible person activates a role for a few hours, with justification and optional approval (Chapter 14).

On Google Cloud

The closest thing to assuming a role is impersonating a service account: a principal with the Token Creator role on a service account can get short-lived tokens for it, for example with gcloud --impersonate-service-account. Avoid downloading service account keys, which are long-lived like IAM user access keys; organisations can forbid them with an organisation policy.

8.11At Larder: ops becomes a role

At Larder: solved

Eli did it in three steps in the first week of December. First, the leavers: he deactivated the access keys of Jasper's and the two contractors' users, removed their console passwords, waited a week to see whether anything broke (nothing did), and deleted the users. Second, he created a role, prod-operator, with ops's old powers minus IAM changes, a one-hour maximum session, and the trust policy from 8.6, listing the six engineers on the on-call rota (8.6 shows two of them). Each of them registered two MFA devices on their own IAM user, their new security key for the console and an authenticator app for the CLI, and got a CLI profile like the one in 8.9. Third, he removed ops's password and detached its policies; the user itself goes in Chapter 12, along with every other IAM user.

The difference shows in CloudTrail. A production change now reads assumed-role/prod-operator/ben, with sourceIdentity: ben, and Ben had to pass MFA to make it. Had the backup change from Chapter 1 happened this way, the first line of the investigation would have named a person.

8.12Lab: create, assume and inspect a role

Lab 8 · A role, a session and a source identity

You'll create a read-only role, assume it with a source identity, look at the temporary credentials and the session's ARN, find the limits of what the session can do, and see the assumption in CloudTrail. You need the AWS CLI v2, signed in to a sandbox account as an administrator (Appendix C), and a Unix shell. Cost: free.

  1. Create the role. It trusts your account (so your administrator permissions decide whether you may assume it) and allows setting a source identity. 
    ACCOUNT=$(aws sts get-caller-identity --query Account --output text)
cat > trust.json <<EOF
{ "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [{ "Effect": "Allow",
                  "Principal": { "AWS": "arn:aws:iam::$ACCOUNT:root" },
                  "Action": ["sts:AssumeRole", "sts:SetSourceIdentity"] }] }
EOF
aws iam create-role --role-name iam-lab-reader --assume-role-policy-document file://trust.json
aws iam attach-role-policy --role-name iam-lab-reader \
  --policy-arn arn:aws:iam::aws:policy/AmazonS3ReadOnlyAccess
    Wait about ten seconds for IAM to catch up.
  2. Assume it for 15 minutes and load the session into your shell: 
    read -r AK SK ST EXP <<< "$(aws sts assume-role \
  --role-arn arn:aws:iam::$ACCOUNT:role/iam-lab-reader \
  --role-session-name lab-$USER --source-identity $USER --duration-seconds 900 \
  --query 'Credentials.[AccessKeyId,SecretAccessKey,SessionToken,Expiration]' --output text)"
export AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID=$AK AWS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY=$SK AWS_SESSION_TOKEN=$ST
echo "$AK expires $EXP"
aws sts get-caller-identity --query Arn --output text
    The key starts with ASIA, expires in 15 minutes, and you are now arn:aws:sts::…:assumed-role/iam-lab-reader/lab-you.
  3. Find the session's edges. Predict each result first: 
    aws s3 ls                                                      # a
aws iam list-users                                             # b
aws sts assume-role --role-arn arn:aws:iam::$ACCOUNT:role/iam-lab-reader \
  --role-session-name again                                    # c
    a works (the role reads S3). b is denied: nothing in the role's permission policies allows iam:ListUsers, and the error message says so. c is denied too: the trust policy would allow it, but the session's own permissions don't include sts:AssumeRole. Both sides must agree, which is Chapter 10's subject.
  4. Return to yourself with unset AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID AWS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY AWS_SESSION_TOKEN, and check with get-caller-identity.
  5. Find it in CloudTrail. After a few minutes (CloudTrail can take up to about 15 to show events): 
    aws cloudtrail lookup-events --max-results 5 \
  --lookup-attributes AttributeKey=EventName,AttributeValue=AssumeRole \
  --query 'Events[].CloudTrailEvent' --output text |
python3 -c 'import sys, json
for line in sys.stdin:
    e = json.loads(line); p = e.get("requestParameters") or {}
    print(e["eventTime"], e["userIdentity"].get("arn"), "→", p.get("roleSessionName"), p.get("sourceIdentity"))'
    You'll see who assumed the role, the session name and the source identity.
  6. Clean up. 
    aws iam detach-role-policy --role-name iam-lab-reader \
  --policy-arn arn:aws:iam::aws:policy/AmazonS3ReadOnlyAccess
aws iam delete-role --role-name iam-lab-reader
rm trust.json

Stretch goals. (1) Add the MFA condition from 8.6 to the trust policy, then try step 2 again: it fails until you pass --serial-number and --token-code. (2) Set the role's maximum session duration to two hours with aws iam update-role --max-session-duration 7200 and assume it with --duration-seconds 7200.

8.13Common mistakes

MistakeWhat happensInstead
IAM users for peopleLong-lived passwords and keys; leavers keep access.Single sign-on with role sessions.
Access keys for workloads on AWSKeys in config files, images and Git history.Service roles (Chapter 15).
Wide trust ("AWS": "*", whole accounts, unconditioned providers)Strangers or partners' admins can assume the role.Named principals plus conditions.
iam:PassRole on *Anyone who can launch a service gets any role's permissions.Specific role ARNs and iam:PassedToService.
Meaningless session namesCloudTrail can't tell sessions apart.Session names and source identity that name the person or job.

✓Chapter summary

  • IAM users have long-lived passwords and keys (AKIA…) in one account; they're a legacy pattern. Groups bundle users' policies and aren't principals.
  • A role has no credentials; trusted principals assume it and get temporary ones (ASIA…) from STS.
  • A role = a trust policy (who may assume it, when) + permission policies (what a session may do).
  • Sessions last 15 minutes to 12 hours (chained ones, one hour); session names and source identity keep accountability.
  • Trust specific principals with conditions; an account principal delegates to that account's policies.
  • Services act through service roles; iam:PassRole decides who may hand them one, so restrict it.
  • The CLI and SDKs use the first credentials in the provider chain; get-caller-identity tells you which.

Key terms

IAM user
A long-lived identity with a password and/or access keys in one account.
IAM group
A set of IAM users sharing policies; not a principal.
IAM role
An identity without credentials, assumed for temporary sessions.
Trust policy
The resource-based policy on a role that says who may assume it.
STS
AWS Security Token Service, which issues temporary credentials.
Role session
A period of use of a role, with its own ARN and temporary credentials.
Source identity
An unchangeable value recorded for every action in a session and its chained sessions.
Service role
A role an AWS service assumes to act for you.
iam:PassRole
The permission to give a role to an AWS service.
Credential provider chain
The order in which tools look for credentials.

Check your understanding

  1. You find AKIAQEXAMPLE… in a public repository. What kind of credential is it, and why does that make it worse than ASIA…? recall

    Show answer

    A long-lived IAM user (or root) access key. It works until deleted, whereas an ASIA key is part of a temporary session that expires within hours. Deactivate it now, then investigate its use in CloudTrail.

  2. A trust policy names arn:aws:iam::111122223333:root. Can every user in that account assume the role? explain

    Show answer

    Only those whose own identity policies allow sts:AssumeRole on this role. Naming the account delegates the choice to that account's administrators; it doesn't mean the root user, and it doesn't mean everyone.

  3. Write a trust policy for a role that ECS tasks in your account use. apply

    Show answer

    {"Effect": "Allow", "Principal": {"Service": "ecs-tasks.amazonaws.com"}, "Action": "sts:AssumeRole", "Condition": {"StringEquals": {"aws:SourceAccount": "111122223333"}}}, optionally with aws:SourceArn limited to your cluster's tasks.

  4. A developer may create Lambda functions and has iam:PassRole on *. Explain how they could become an administrator. explain

    Show answer

    They create a function, pass it an existing administrator role, and invoke it: the function's code runs with administrator permissions and can, for example, attach AdministratorAccess to the developer. Restrict PassRole to specific roles.

  5. Six engineers share a powerful role. Design it so that CloudTrail still shows which of them did what, and nobody can pretend to be another. design

    Show answer

    Require sts:SetSourceIdentity with sts:SourceIdentity equal to ${aws:username} (or the IdP's user attribute with SSO), require MFA, and keep sessions to an hour. Every event then carries a source identity that the person couldn't choose freely.

  6. Compare how a person gets temporary admin rights on AWS and on Azure. compare

    Show answer

    AWS: they assume a role (directly or through an SSO permission set) and get a time-limited session. Azure: they're made eligible for a role in Privileged Identity Management and activate it for a few hours, with justification or approval; there's no separate identity to assume.
