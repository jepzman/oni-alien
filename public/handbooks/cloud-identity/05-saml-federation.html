SAML and federation

Federation means trusting someone else's login: your partner's staff sign in with their own company accounts, and your employees reach a hundred SaaS tools and your cloud consoles with one. Much of that still runs on SAML 2.0, an XML standard from 2005 that every enterprise identity provider speaks. This chapter explains how SAML works, what an assertion says, how to check one, and how to decide how far to trust the identity provider that sent it.

By the end you can

  • Explain identity providers, service providers and the metadata that connects them.
  • Follow an SP-initiated SAML sign-in, and say why IdP-initiated sign-in is riskier.
  • Read a SAML assertion and list every check a service provider must make.
  • Choose between SAML and OpenID Connect for a new integration.
  • Limit what a federated identity provider can vouch for.

At Larder

Korenwolf's three offices start their Larder for Business service in January 2027, and Korenwolf IT's 40-section solution design template has a firm line in it: all supplier portals used by staff must use single sign-on with the Korenwolf identity provider, via SAML 2.0. Their office managers will sign in to Larder with their Korenwolf accounts, and when someone leaves Korenwolf, their access to Larder must end with their Korenwolf account. Eli has until 18 December to make it work.

The protocol is the easy part. The question that takes Eli a week is a different one: if Larder trusts Korenwolf's identity provider to say who someone is, what stops it saying something Larder shouldn't believe? Section 5.7 answers it.

5.1What federation is for

Federation is an agreement between two systems: one authenticates people, the other accepts its word. The person signs in once, with the account their organisation manages, and gets into applications run by someone else. Everyone gains something:

  • The person has one login and one set of factors, instead of a password per supplier.
  • Their organisation keeps control: it decides who may use the app, enforces its MFA rules, and ends access by disabling one account.
  • The application stores no passwords for these people, and doesn't run their resets.

The same idea runs inside your own company: employees sign in to the AWS console, Slack and Jira through one identity provider (Chapter 12). And it works between machines, when a CI system's token is accepted by a cloud (Chapter 17). This chapter is about people and SAML; the principles carry over.

5.2Identity providers, service providers and metadata

SAML has two roles. The identity provider (IdP) authenticates the person and issues a signed statement about them: Korenwolf's Microsoft Entra ID. The service provider (SP) is the application that relies on it: Larder's login service. (OpenID Connect calls the SP a relying party.)

Before anyone signs in, the two exchange metadata, an XML document each publishes about itself. It's how trust is written down (Chapter 1, 1.6):

Table 5.1 · What each side's metadata tells the other
FromFieldLarder–Korenwolf example

IdPsingle sign-on URL: where to send peoplehttps://login.korenwolf.example/saml2
IdPsigning certificate: the public key that checks its signaturesan X.509 certificate, valid three years

The certificate is the heart of it: the SP will believe any assertion signed with the matching private key. Certificates expire, and an unnoticed expiry stops every sign-in at once, so put the date in a calendar and agree how rollovers will work (most IdPs can publish the next certificate in advance).

5.3A sign-in, step by step

SAML moves messages through the user's browser, using redirects and auto-submitting HTML forms; the IdP and SP never talk directly during a sign-in. The usual flow starts at the application, so it's called SP-initiated:

Step ① has a name: home realm discovery, working out which IdP a person belongs to, here from the email domain. In step ② the SP remembers the request's ID; in step ⑥ it checks that the response answers that request (InResponseTo).

SAML can also start at the IdP: the person clicks a Larder tile in Korenwolf's app portal, and the IdP posts an unsolicited response straight to Larder. This IdP-initiated sign-in is convenient, but there's no request to match it to, so the SP can't tell a fresh sign-in from a response captured and replayed, or injected into someone else's browser. Prefer SP-initiated; if you must accept IdP-initiated, keep validity windows short and keep a replay cache.

5.4Inside an assertion

The SAML response carries an assertion: the IdP's signed statement about the person. Strip away the namespaces and it reads quite naturally:

Two details deserve attention:

  • The NameID identifies the person to this SP. Prefer a persistent format, an opaque ID that never changes for this person and this SP. An email address as NameID breaks when someone marries and changes their name, and can be reassigned to a new employee later. Together with the issuer, the NameID is the key for the account, like iss + sub in OIDC.
  • Attributes are whatever the IdP is configured to release: names, email, department, group memberships. The SP should ask for the minimum, and treat each one as a claim made by the IdP, which is only as trustworthy as the IdP's own data.

5.5Checking an assertion

A SAML response arrives as a form post from a browser, which anyone can make. Before trusting it, the SP must check:

  1. Signature: the assertion (or the whole response) is signed, the signature is valid, and it was made with the certificate from this IdP's metadata, not one embedded in the message.
  2. Issuer: exactly the IdP this connection trusts.
  3. Audience: your SP's entity ID.
  4. Recipient and destination: your ACS URL for this connection.
  5. Time: now is between NotBefore and NotOnOrAfter, with a small allowance for clock differences.
  6. InResponseTo: answers a request you sent and haven't seen answered.
  7. One use: the assertion ID hasn't been seen before (keep a cache until its expiry).
  8. Then your own rules about what this IdP may say (5.7), and map the attributes to the account.

Watch out: never write your own SAML validation

XML signatures sign a part of a document, identified by an ID. In signature wrapping attacks, the attacker keeps the validly signed part where the signature check finds it, and adds a second, unsigned assertion where the application reads its data. Code that checks one element and reads another is fooled. These bugs keep reappearing, even in mature libraries. Use a well-maintained SAML library or, better, a managed identity service, and keep it updated.

5.6SAML or OpenID Connect?

Both protocols federate sign-in, and most identity providers speak both. They differ in age and in what they're good at:

Table 5.2 · SAML 2.0 and OpenID Connect side by side
SAML 2.0OpenID Connect
Since20052014
FormatXML, XML signaturesJSON, JWTs
Transportthrough the browser (redirects and form posts)browser redirects plus a back channel for tokens
Mobile and native appsawkwarddesigned for them (Chapter 4)
Calling APIs afterwardsnot part of the standardOAuth access tokens, in the same flow
Where it's strongenterprise SaaS, older IdPs, cloud console federationnew apps, mobile, APIs, workload federation

For a new integration where both sides can choose, choose OpenID Connect: it's simpler to implement correctly and covers apps and APIs. Support SAML when your customers' identity providers require it, as Korenwolf's does: in enterprise sales it's often a checkbox you can't skip.

5.7How far to trust a partner's IdP

Here is the question that kept Eli busy. Once Larder trusts assertions signed by Korenwolf's IdP, those assertions can say anything: any email address, any group, any office. A misconfiguration at Korenwolf, a malicious administrator there, or an attacker who takes over their IdP could produce a perfectly signed assertion for eli@larder.example, or for an office manager at a different Larder business customer. The signature proves who said it, not that it's true.

Key idea

A federated IdP should only be able to vouch for its own people, inside its own organisation's account, with the attributes you need, and nothing more.

In practice, that means four rules on the SP side:

  1. Bind the connection to one tenant. Accounts created through Korenwolf's connection belong to Korenwolf's business account, always. No attribute in an assertion can move someone into another customer's account, or into Larder's staff tools.
  2. Bind it to the organisation's domains. Only email addresses at domains Korenwolf has proved it owns (korenwolf.example) are accepted from it. An assertion naming any other domain is rejected, however well signed.
  3. Key accounts on issuer + NameID, never on email alone, so an account can't be claimed by another IdP that asserts the same address.
  4. Ask the IdP to filter, and check anyway. Korenwolf assigns the Larder app only to its office managers' group, so other staff never get an assertion. Larder also requires that group in the assertion, so a mistake on one side isn't enough.

Then decide how accounts appear and disappear. With just-in-time provisioning, the SP creates the account at the first successful sign-in from the assertion's attributes; simple, but the SP never hears that someone left, only that they stopped signing in. With SCIM, the IdP pushes creations, changes and removals to the SP as they happen (Chapter 13). Either way, keep the SP's sessions short, because disabling someone at the IdP doesn't end a session they already have at the SP.

5.8Across providers

The clouds appear on both sides of federation: as IdPs for the apps you build, and as SPs that accept your company's IdP for console and CLI access.

On Azure

Inviting a partner's staff as B2B guests is Entra ID's own form of federation: the guest signs in with their home organisation's account, and lives in your tenant as a guest user you can add to groups and remove. Cross-tenant access settings decide which external tenants are allowed, and whether you trust their MFA.

On Google Cloud

Workforce identity federation lets people from an external IdP (SAML or OIDC) use the Google Cloud console and APIs without Google accounts at all; IAM policies grant roles to attributes from the assertion, such as a group. It's the closest equivalent to AWS's AssumeRoleWithSAML.

5.9At Larder: Korenwolf's office managers sign in

At Larder: solved

Larder's login service could already act as a SAML SP for each business customer, so the work was configuration and rules. Eli and Korenwolf IT exchanged metadata on 7 December; Korenwolf assigned the Larder app to a new group, Larder office managers, with nine people from the three offices; and Korenwolf's IdP releases four things: a persistent NameID, email, office and that group.

On Larder's side, the connection is bound to Korenwolf's business account and to korenwolf.example; accounts are keyed on issuer + NameID; the group is required; and the office attribute picks which delivery address an office manager orders for. Accounts are created just in time for now, with sessions of eight hours; SCIM is on the plan for when Korenwolf's portal launches in spring (Chapter 13). The first test sign-in failed because the audience in Korenwolf's configuration had a trailing slash; the second worked. It went live on 18 December, as planned.

The certificate in Korenwolf's metadata expires in December 2029. It's in Larder's on-call calendar for November 2029.

5.10Lab: be the service provider

Lab 5 · Read and check a SAML assertion

You'll parse a SAML response like Korenwolf's, run the service provider's checks from 5.5 and 5.7, and watch it reject a replay, a late response, the wrong audience and an IdP vouching for someone it shouldn't. You need Python 3. Cost: nothing. Important: the lab doesn't check the XML signature, which needs a dedicated library (such as python3-saml or pysaml2, built on xmlsec); 5.5 explains why you shouldn't write that yourself.

saml.pyPython 3.8+

# saml.py · read a SAML assertion and run a service provider's checks on it
# Teaching only: it does NOT verify the XML signature. Real code uses a library that does.
import xml.etree.ElementTree as ET
from datetime import datetime

NS = {"samlp": "urn:oasis:names:tc:SAML:2.0:protocol",
      "saml": "urn:oasis:names:tc:SAML:2.0:assertion"}

RESPONSE = """<samlp:Response xmlns:samlp="urn:oasis:names:tc:SAML:2.0:protocol"
    xmlns:saml="urn:oasis:names:tc:SAML:2.0:assertion" ID="_r1" Version="2.0"
    IssueInstant="2026-12-18T08:30:00Z" InResponseTo="_req-7f3a"
    Destination="https://login.larder.example/saml/acs/korenwolf">
  <saml:Issuer>https://sts.korenwolf.example/7c1d</saml:Issuer>
  <samlp:Status><samlp:StatusCode Value="urn:oasis:names:tc:SAML:2.0:status:Success"/></samlp:Status>
  <saml:Assertion ID="_a-91c2" Version="2.0" IssueInstant="2026-12-18T08:30:00Z">
    <saml:Issuer>https://sts.korenwolf.example/7c1d</saml:Issuer>
    <!-- <ds:Signature> over this Assertion goes here -->
    <saml:Subject>
      <saml:NameID Format="urn:oasis:names:tc:SAML:2.0:nameid-format:persistent">kw-48d1a0</saml:NameID>
      <saml:SubjectConfirmation Method="urn:oasis:names:tc:SAML:2.0:cm:bearer">
        <saml:SubjectConfirmationData InResponseTo="_req-7f3a" NotOnOrAfter="2026-12-18T08:35:00Z"
            Recipient="https://login.larder.example/saml/acs/korenwolf"/>
      </saml:SubjectConfirmation>
    </saml:Subject>
    <saml:Conditions NotBefore="2026-12-18T08:29:00Z" NotOnOrAfter="2026-12-18T08:35:00Z">
      <saml:AudienceRestriction><saml:Audience>https://login.larder.example/saml</saml:Audience></saml:AudienceRestriction>
    </saml:Conditions>
    <saml:AuthnStatement AuthnInstant="2026-12-18T08:29:58Z">
      <saml:AuthnContext><saml:AuthnContextClassRef>urn:oasis:names:tc:SAML:2.0:ac:classes:PasswordProtectedTransport</saml:AuthnContextClassRef></saml:AuthnContext>
    </saml:AuthnStatement>
    <saml:AttributeStatement>
      <saml:Attribute Name="email"><saml:AttributeValue>j.devries@korenwolf.example</saml:AttributeValue></saml:Attribute>
      <saml:Attribute Name="office"><saml:AttributeValue>Rotterdam</saml:AttributeValue></saml:Attribute>
      <saml:Attribute Name="groups"><saml:AttributeValue>Larder office managers</saml:AttributeValue></saml:Attribute>
    </saml:AttributeStatement>
  </saml:Assertion>
</samlp:Response>"""

# What Larder configured for this connection (from Korenwolf's metadata, and its own)
TRUST = {"issuer": "https://sts.korenwolf.example/7c1d",
         "audience": "https://login.larder.example/saml",
         "acs": "https://login.larder.example/saml/acs/korenwolf",
         "email_domain": "korenwolf.example",
         "required_group": "Larder office managers"}
PENDING = {"_req-7f3a"}            # AuthnRequest IDs we sent and haven't seen answered
SEEN = set()                       # assertion IDs already used (replay cache)

def t(text):
    return datetime.fromisoformat(text.replace("Z", "+00:00"))

def check(xml, now, skew=60):
    r = ET.fromstring(xml)
    a = r.find("saml:Assertion", NS)
    cond = a.find("saml:Conditions", NS)
    scd = a.find(".//saml:SubjectConfirmationData", NS)
    attrs = {x.get("Name"): x.findtext("saml:AttributeValue", namespaces=NS)
             for x in a.findall(".//saml:Attribute", NS)}
    problems = []
    if a.findtext("saml:Issuer", namespaces=NS) != TRUST["issuer"]:
        problems.append("issuer")
    if cond.findtext(".//saml:Audience", namespaces=NS) != TRUST["audience"]:
        problems.append("audience")
    if scd.get("Recipient") != TRUST["acs"] or r.get("Destination") != TRUST["acs"]:
        problems.append("recipient")
    if not (t(cond.get("NotBefore")).timestamp() - skew <= now <= t(cond.get("NotOnOrAfter")).timestamp() + skew):
        problems.append("time window")
    if scd.get("InResponseTo") not in PENDING:
        problems.append("not a response to our request")
    if a.get("ID") in SEEN:
        problems.append("replayed assertion")
    if not attrs.get("email", "").endswith("@" + TRUST["email_domain"]):
        problems.append("email outside korenwolf.example")
    if attrs.get("groups") != TRUST["required_group"]:
        problems.append("not in the Larder group")
    if problems:
        return "rejected: " + ", ".join(problems)
    SEEN.add(a.get("ID"))
    PENDING.discard(scd.get("InResponseTo"))
    name_id = a.findtext(".//saml:NameID", namespaces=NS)
    return f"ok: {TRUST['issuer']} | {name_id} | {attrs['email']} | office={attrs['office']}"

if __name__ == "__main__":
    NOW = t("2026-12-18T08:30:02Z").timestamp()
    print("as received     ", check(RESPONSE, NOW))
    print("same one again  ", check(RESPONSE, NOW + 10))
    PENDING.add("_req-7f3a"); SEEN.clear()
    print("ten minutes late", check(RESPONSE, NOW + 600))
    print("other audience  ", check(RESPONSE.replace("login.larder.example/saml<", "app.other.example/saml<"), NOW))
    print("a Larder email  ", check(RESPONSE.replace("j.devries@korenwolf.example", "eli@larder.example"), NOW))
  1. Run it with python3 saml.py: 
    as received      ok: https://sts.korenwolf.example/7c1d | kw-48d1a0 | j.devries@korenwolf.example | office=Rotterdam
same one again   rejected: not a response to our request, replayed assertion
ten minutes late rejected: time window
other audience   rejected: audience
a Larder email   rejected: email outside korenwolf.example
    Match each line to a check in 5.5 or 5.7. The last one is the important one: even if Korenwolf's IdP signed an assertion for eli@larder.example, Larder wouldn't accept it from this connection.
  2. Read the account key. The success line shows issuer and NameID first. Change the email in RESPONSE to a different @korenwolf.example address and run again: it's still the same account (kw-48d1a0), with a changed email, which is what should happen when someone changes their name.
  3. Break it on purpose. Comment out the domain check and run again: the assertion for a Larder employee is accepted. Then delete PENDING.add("_req-7f3a"); SEEN.clear() and run again: every later test now also fails with “not a response to our request”, because once a request has been answered, it can't be answered again.

Stretch goal. Look at a real IdP's metadata: in Microsoft Entra ID, every tenant publishes it at https://login.microsoftonline.com/<tenant-id>/federationmetadata/2007-06/federationmetadata.xml. Find the entity ID, the sign-in URL and the signing certificate's expiry (decode the certificate with openssl x509 -noout -enddate).

✓Chapter summary

  • Federation: an SP accepts an IdP's word about who someone is. The person's own organisation keeps control of their account.
  • Trust is set up with metadata: entity IDs, URLs and the IdP's signing certificate.
  • SAML sign-in passes through the browser; SP-initiated flows can be matched to a request, IdP-initiated ones can't.
  • An assertion says who vouches, about whom, for which service, when, how they signed in, and some attributes.
  • Check signature (with the metadata's certificate), issuer, audience, recipient, time, InResponseTo and one-time use, with a library.
  • Limit what each IdP can vouch for: its own tenant, its own domains, required groups. Key accounts on issuer + NameID.
  • Prefer OpenID Connect for new integrations; support SAML where customers need it.

Key terms

Federation
Accepting another system's authentication of a person.
Identity provider (IdP)
Authenticates people and issues signed assertions about them.
Service provider (SP)
The application that relies on the IdP's assertions.
Metadata
An XML description of an IdP or SP: entity ID, endpoints and certificates.
Assertion
A signed SAML statement about a subject's authentication and attributes.
ACS URL
Assertion consumer service: the SP endpoint that receives SAML responses.
NameID
The identifier for the subject; persistent NameIDs never change for a person and SP.
Home realm discovery
Working out which IdP a person belongs to.
JIT provisioning
Creating an account at a person's first federated sign-in.

Check your understanding

  1. In the Larder–Korenwolf setup, which is the IdP, which is the SP, and where is the person's password checked? recall

    Show answer

    Korenwolf's Entra ID is the IdP and checks the password and MFA. Larder's login service is the SP; it never sees the password.

  2. Why does an SP keep the IDs of the AuthnRequests it sends, and of the assertions it has accepted? explain

    Show answer

    Request IDs let it check that a response answers a sign-in this SP started (InResponseTo). Assertion IDs let it reject a captured response that's replayed while still within its time window.

  3. An assertion is correctly signed by a partner's IdP and names finance@yourcompany.example. What should happen, and why? apply

    Show answer

    Reject it. The partner's connection may only vouch for its own verified domains and its own tenant; a valid signature proves who made the statement, not that the partner is entitled to make it.

  4. Korenwolf disables an office manager's account at 10:00. They had signed in to Larder at 09:00. When does their Larder access end, and how would you make it sooner? apply

    Show answer

    With just-in-time accounts, when Larder's session ends: up to eight hours after 09:00. Sooner: shorter sessions, and SCIM deprovisioning so Larder hears about the disabled account and ends its sessions at once.

  5. A start-up is adding enterprise SSO to its new web and mobile app. Should it start with SAML or OIDC? design

    Show answer

    Start with OpenID Connect, which suits mobile and APIs and is easier to get right; add SAML when an enterprise customer's IdP requires it, ideally through an identity service that handles both.

  6. Compare how a partner's staff could be given access to your app on Azure and on AWS. compare

    Show answer

    Azure: invite them as B2B guests in your Entra ID tenant, signing in with their home organisation, governed by cross-tenant access settings; or add their IdP to Entra External ID. AWS: add their IdP (SAML or OIDC) to a Cognito user pool, one IdP per partner, mapped to that partner's account in your app.
