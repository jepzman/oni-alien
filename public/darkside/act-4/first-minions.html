Your First Minions

Your first team. You were promoted for doing the work. Now you mustn't.

Threat
Habitat
first 1:1s, first feedback, the first time you redo someone's work at midnight
Weakness
learning that your job is now their success, not your output

IA sighting

Thursday 1 April 2027 · first day as a manager

Pip became analytics lead in Customer Ops: his first team, Asha and Tomasz. He bought a management book. On his first morning he held it, briefly, upside down. Nobody noticed except the Annotator, who later wrote this entry.

April · the midnight redo

Tomasz's weekly report wasn't how Pip would have done it. Pip redid it at midnight. Then he redid Asha's dashboard. By the end of April he was working more hours than ever and his team had learned nothing, except that Pip would redo things.

May · the avoided conversation

Tomasz's summaries kept burying the main finding. Pip didn't say anything, because Tomasz had eight years at MetroVolt and Pip had eight weeks as a manager. The summaries kept burying the finding.

June · the turn

Priya, in a 1:1, asked how many hours he was working. Pip said 55. She asked how many hours Asha and Tomasz were learning from him. Pip didn't have an answer. That was the day he stopped redoing things.

— the AnnotatorYou were promoted for being good at the work. Now your work is making them good at it.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow MetroVolt makes first-time managers

  1. Always · the promotion

    At MetroVolt, as almost everywhere, the best analyst gets promoted to manage the analysts. Skill at the work is the main qualification for leading it.

  2. Always · the training

    There's a two-day course, usually six months after you start. By then the habits are formed.

  3. Always · the reflex

    New managers do what made them successful: the work itself. They redo, check and hover. Some become All-Seeing Eyes (Act III).

  4. 2027 · Pip

    Pip, a brilliant analyst, becomes a manager on a Thursday, with a book and no training. He does exactly what everyone does. Then he learns.

The turning point. Promoting people for doing the work, into a job that's mostly not doing it, with no training until it's too late.

What might have saved them. Training before the first day. Or a manager who asks, in week two, “how much are they learning from you?”

— the AnnotatorEvery bad habit in Act III starts here, in someone's first month as a manager. This is where you stop it.

IIIKnow thy enemy

First-time managers are usually promoted for being good at the work, and then struggle because the new job is different: enabling others, giving feedback, protecting the team. The commonest traps are doing the work yourself, avoiding feedback, and wanting to be liked more than wanting people to grow.

What your team wants

Clear goals, honest feedback, protection from chaos above, and room to do the work their way.

Why new managers struggle

Because the habits that made them great individuals (doing, checking, perfecting) make them poor managers if they don't change.

What makes a good first year

  • Delegating outcomes, not tasks.
  • Specific feedback, early.
  • Real 1:1s.
  • Shielding the team.

Three kinds of new manager

The Doer

Keeps doing the work. Needs to let go.

The Friend

Avoids hard conversations. Needs to give feedback.

The Checker

Hovers and checks. Needs to trust. (Don't be Victor.)

— the AnnotatorIf you're working more hours than before you were promoted, you're probably still doing your old job.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • You still do the work yourself “because it's faster”.
  • You avoid giving feedback.
  • You want to be liked more than you want them to grow.
  • You're working twice as many hours as before.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Letting Go

    Delegate whole outcomes, not tasks. Agree what good looks like, then step back.

    incantation“Asha, the regulator dashboard is yours now. Here's what good looks like. Shout if you're stuck; I won't check in daily.”

  2. 2The Clear Word

    Give specific feedback early: what you saw, why it matters, what you'd like instead.

    incantation“The report was solid. The summary buried the main finding. Next time, lead with it.”

  3. 3The Shield

    Protect your team from noise above: priorities, pressure, the Seagull's visits.

  4. 4The Real 1:1

    Hold weekly 1:1s that are about them (see Act III, the Ritual Circle).

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Shadow Doer

    Redoing their work at night. They learn nothing, and you burn out.

  • The Best Friend

    Avoiding hard conversations to stay liked. See Act III, the Conflict Phantom.

  • The Mini-Eye

    Checking everything because you know how you'd do it. See Act III, the All-Seeing Eye. Don't be Victor.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

A new manager with a good team.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your team is capable.
  • You're tempted to do things yourself.
  • Your manager supports you.

Do

  1. Delegate whole outcomes.
  2. Start weekly 1:1s about them.
  3. Give one piece of specific feedback a week.

Don’t: The shadow doer.

If it gets worse: If you're avoiding hard feedback, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

Managing people more experienced than you.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • They've been here longer.
  • Feedback feels awkward.
  • Problems are going unsaid.

Do

  1. Give feedback on outcomes, respectfully and specifically.
  2. Ask for their advice; it's not weakness.
  3. Set clear expectations together.
  4. Protect them from noise above.

Don’t: The best friend: avoiding hard conversations.

If it gets worse: If you're burning out and the team isn't growing, you're at Hell.

Hell

Drowning: doing two jobs, the team stuck.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • You work far more hours than before.
  • Your team waits for you to decide everything.
  • Nobody's growing.

Do

  1. Stop doing the work. Hand it over, with support.
  2. Ask your manager for coaching or training.
  3. Agree what good looks like with each person.
  4. Read Act III to see what not to become.

Don’t: The mini-eye: checking everything.

If it gets worse: If you hate managing, read the Peter Principle. It might not be for you, and that's fine.

VIIIAre you still doing your old job?

If you manage anyone, tick honestly.

  • You've redone someone's work this month.
  • You work more hours than before you were promoted.
  • You've avoided giving feedback to someone more experienced.
  • Your team waits for you before deciding things.
  • Your 1:1s are mostly status updates.

The cure, if you ticked two: Pick one thing you do that someone in your team could own. Hand it over this week, with what good looks like. Then don't touch it.

IXThe rehearsal

The Letting Go and the Clear Word.

Scene: Monday 7 June 2027, Pip and Asha.

PipAsha, from today the regulator dashboard is yours. Here's what good looks like: on time, checked, and the main change on the first line. I won't check it daily.

AshaYou redid my last one.

PipI did, and I shouldn't have. It was fine. If something's wrong, I'll tell you, and you'll fix it.

Asha…OK. What would you have changed?

PipThe summary buried the main finding. Lead with it. That's the only thing.

Asha owns the dashboard for the next year. It's never late. In December it's in her objectives, and in calibration.

— the AnnotatorHanding it over cost Pip a small, perfectionist pang. It gave Asha a year of growth and him forty evenings back.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your analyst's work isn't how you'd do it. Do you redo it?

No. Give feedback on what matters, let the rest go, and let them own it.

Your old peers are now your team. How do you change?

Name it, set new boundaries (see Act III, the Friend Who Became Your Boss), and be fair to everyone.

What's your job now?

Their success: clear goals, feedback, protection and growth. Your output is their output.
