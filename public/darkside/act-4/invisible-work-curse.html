The Invisible Work Curse

Everyone relies on it. Nobody rewards it. It's called glue work, and it sticks to the same people.

Threat
Habitat
onboarding, documentation, meeting notes, team rotas, the birthday card
Weakness
naming it, sharing it, and counting it in reviews

IA sighting

Every week · Asha

Asha onboarded every new joiner. She kept the data dictionary up to date. She ran the team rota, took notes in every meeting, and remembered birthdays. In 2027 she onboarded four people and answered, by her own count, 312 “quick questions” about the data.

Friday 10 December · her review

Asha's review, written by Pip, said: “Great team player. Solid analysis.” It did not mention onboarding, the dictionary or the 312 questions. Pip had written it in twenty minutes. He had not noticed what he hadn't written.

Monday 13 December

Asha asked, quietly, why the onboarding wasn't in her review. Pip opened his mouth and realised he had no idea how much of the team's smoothness came from her. He'd thought it just… happened.

That week

Pip counted. Asha spent roughly a day a week on glue work. Without it, the four new joiners would have taken six weeks each to get productive instead of two. Nobody had ever written that number down.

— the AnnotatorGlue holds everything together. That's exactly why it's invisible. Make it visible.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the glue came to stick to Asha

  1. 2025 · her first week

    Asha joins Customer Ops. The data dictionary is three years out of date. She fixes it, because she needs it. People thank her.

  2. 2026 · the default

    When someone new arrives, people say “ask Asha”. She's helpful, so she helps. Onboarding becomes hers without anyone deciding it.

  3. 2027 · the weight

    Asha does a day a week of glue work. Her objectives, written by a manager who never saw it, list only analysis.

  4. December 2027 · the review

    Her review praises her as a “team player”, which is how reviews describe glue work they can't see.

The turning point. Fixing one thing because it needed fixing, and becoming the person who fixes everything, without it ever being agreed.

What might have saved them. One sentence from a manager in 2026: “You're doing the onboarding now; let's put it in your objectives.”

— the AnnotatorGlue work often falls on the most helpful people, and disproportionately on women. If you manage a team, go and look for it.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Glue work is the essential, unglamorous work that keeps a team running: onboarding, documentation, notes, rotas, coordination. Everyone depends on it, but it rarely appears in objectives or reviews, so the people doing it get “team player” instead of promotions.

What it is

Work that makes everyone else's work possible: knowledge, coordination, care. Remove it and things quietly stop working.

Why it stays invisible

Because it prevents problems instead of producing outputs. Nobody notices a crisis that didn't happen, or a new joiner who got productive fast.

What makes it count

  • Writing it down with the time it takes.
  • Measuring its outcome.
  • Putting it in objectives.
  • Sharing it on a rota.

Three kinds of glue

The Knowledge Glue

Documentation, dictionaries, answers. Measure questions answered and time saved.

The People Glue

Onboarding, mentoring, morale. Measure time-to-productive.

The Admin Glue

Notes, rotas, organising. Share it on a rota.

— the Annotator“Team player” in a review often means “did lots of things nobody measured”. Measure them.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • You onboard every new joiner, and it's not in your objectives.
  • You maintain the documents everyone uses.
  • You take the notes in every meeting.
  • Your review mentions “team player” and nothing else.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Naming

    Write down the glue work you do and the time it takes. Make it visible to your manager.

    incantation“I spend about a day a week on onboarding, the data dictionary and the rota. Can we talk about whether that's in my objectives?”

  2. 2The Rota

    Share glue work across the team on a rota, so it isn't always the same people.

    incantation“Shall we rotate meeting notes? It's been me for a year.”

  3. 3The Count

    Ask for glue work that matters to be in your objectives and your review, with outcomes.

    incantation“Could the onboarding guide be an objective? New joiners now get productive in two weeks, not six.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Silent Saint

    Doing it all, saying nothing, and resenting it. Nobody can reward what they can't see.

  • The Sudden Strike

    Stopping all of it at once, without warning. Things break, and you get blamed for the break.

  • The Martyr's Ledger

    Reminding everyone constantly how much you do. See Act II, the Martyr Banshee.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

Glue work that's noticed but not counted.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • People thank you.
  • It's not in your objectives.
  • Your manager would add it if asked.

Do

  1. Write it down with the time it takes.
  2. Ask for it in your objectives.
  3. Measure one outcome.

Don’t: The silent saint.

If it gets worse: If it's invisible and growing, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

Glue work that's invisible and growing.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • It takes a day a week or more.
  • Your review says “team player”.
  • Others have stopped doing any.

Do

  1. Do the Naming with your manager.
  2. Propose a rota.
  3. Ask for the most valuable glue to count in your review.
  4. Drop or share the least valuable.

Don’t: The sudden strike.

If it gets worse: If it's holding back your promotion, you're at Hell.

Hell

Glue work blocking your career.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Peers with less impact are promoted.
  • You're described as “supportive”, not “strong”.
  • The glue keeps landing on you.

Do

  1. Raise it formally in your review, with numbers.
  2. Hand over glue work explicitly, with dates.
  3. Ask your sponsor to describe its value in calibration.
  4. Consider whether the culture can change.

Don’t: The martyr's ledger: reminding everyone constantly.

If it gets worse: If nothing changes, take your skills where they'll be counted.

VIIIDo you see the glue?

Especially if you manage people. Tick honestly.

  • You don't know who onboards new joiners in your team.
  • Someone always takes the notes, and it's the same person.
  • You've written “team player” in a review without saying what it meant.
  • You've never asked anyone how long their glue work takes.
  • Things run smoothly and you're not sure why.

The cure, if you ticked two: Ask your team: who does the work that keeps us running? Then make sure it's in someone's objectives, or on a rota.

IXThe rehearsal

The Naming and the Count, from the manager's side.

Scene: Monday 13 December, Pip and Asha.

AshaWhy isn't the onboarding in my review?

PipBecause I didn't see it, and that's on me. I'm sorry. How much time does the glue work take?

AshaAbout a day a week. Onboarding, the dictionary, the rota, notes.

PipAnd the four new joiners got productive in two weeks instead of six. That's twenty weeks of time saved. I'm rewriting the review with that number in it.

Asha…Thank you.

PipAnd from January, notes and the rota go on a rotation. The onboarding stays yours, as an objective.

— the AnnotatorOne honest apology and one number turned “team player” into evidence for promotion.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

You do most of your team's glue work. First step?

Write it down with the time it takes, and talk to your manager.

Your manager says “but you're so good at it”. What do you say?

“Thank you. Can it count, or can we share it?” Both are fair asks.

If you manage a team, how do you spot glue work?

Ask: who onboards, documents, takes notes, organises? Then check whether those people's reviews mention it.
