The Raise Duel

Asking for more money, with evidence, without apology.

Threat
Habitat
annual reviews, promotion conversations, the HR business partner's calendar
Weakness
market data, a specific number, and a calm silence after you say it

IA sighting

November · the acting role

Pip had been acting Head of Customer Insight since November, leading nine people, on his level-5 salary of £54,000. The acting allowance he'd asked for “was being looked at”.

January · the research

Pip did what he'd never done: he researched his market. Salary surveys, six job adverts, two recruiters and one honest conversation with Jules. Heads of insight at similar utilities earned £68,000 to £78,000. Analytics leads, his substantive role, earned £58,000 to £64,000.

Thursday 10 February 2028 · 14:00

Pip met Priya and Karen from HR. He had a single page: his role, the market range, his results, and a number. His hands were cold. He said the number. Then he said nothing. It was the longest eleven seconds of his career.

14:00:11

Karen said, “There's no budget for base pay changes this year.” Pip had prepared for that sentence too.

— the AnnotatorSay the number. Then be quiet. The silence is not your problem.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the Annotator learned to duel

  1. 2020 · the Annotator's first offer

    Joining MetroVolt, the Annotator accepts the first salary offered, gratefully, without asking. She learns later she was offered £8,000 less than the man hired the same week.

  2. 2021 · the apology

    Asking for a raise, she opens with “sorry, I know it's a bad time”. She gets 2%. The ask had negotiated against itself before it started.

  3. 2023 · freelance

    Self-employed, she has to name her price every week. She learns to say a number, explain it and stop talking.

  4. 2027 · the margins

    She writes this entry so Pip's first duel goes better than hers.

The turning point. Accepting the first number, and then apologising for asking for a second one.

What might have saved them. Market data and one sentence: “Based on the role and the market, I'm asking for £X.”

— the AnnotatorNobody ever paid me more for being grateful. They paid me more when I showed them what the work was worth.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Pay negotiation is a normal business conversation, not a favour. Organisations expect it, budget for it, and often pay more to those who ask well. Asking well means: market data, evidence of value, a specific number, and a plan B if base pay can't move.

What HR wants

To keep pay within bands and budgets, avoid unfairness between people, and keep good people. Help them do all three.

Why people don't ask

Fear, gratitude, and the belief that good work will be noticed and rewarded automatically. It's usually noticed. It's rarely rewarded automatically.

What wins the duel

  • A market range from several sources.
  • Your results, in numbers.
  • One specific number.
  • Silence after you say it.

Three kinds of duel

The Review Duel

Annual pay review. Bring market data early.

The Promotion Duel

New role, new band. Ask before accepting.

The Retention Duel

You have an offer elsewhere. Only if you'd really go.

— the Annotator“No budget” usually means “no budget for base pay this year”. Ask about everything else.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • You haven't had a meaningful raise in two years.
  • Your role has grown; your pay hasn't.
  • New joiners are paid more than you for similar work.
  • You've never asked.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Market Scroll

    Know your market rate: salary surveys, job adverts, recruiters, peers.

  2. 2The Number

    Ask for a specific number, explain why, and then stop talking.

    incantation“Based on the role and the market, I'm asking for £62,000. Here's the evidence.” (Then silence.)

  3. 3The Plan B

    If there's no budget, negotiate other things or a date: an allowance, a review in six months, training, title, flexibility.

    incantation“If the salary can't move now, could we agree an acting allowance and a review in July, in writing?”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Apology

    “Sorry, I know it's a bad time, I just wondered if maybe…” You've negotiated against yourself before you started.

  • The Bluff

    Threatening to leave without meaning it. They may call it.

  • The Comparison

    “Dorian earns more than me.” Even if true, it makes it about him, not your value.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Review Duel, with a fair manager.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Pay reviews happen yearly.
  • Your manager supports you.
  • Bands are published.

Do

  1. Research the market.
  2. Bring a one-page case and a number.
  3. Ask early, before budgets are set.

Don’t: The apology.

If it gets worse: If you hear “there's no budget”, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

“There's no budget.”

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Base pay is frozen.
  • Your role has grown.
  • HR is sympathetic but stuck.

Do

  1. Plan B: allowance, bonus, title, training, flexibility.
  2. Ask for a dated review in writing.
  3. Ask where you sit in the band and why.
  4. Keep your market data current.

Don’t: The comparison with named colleagues.

If it gets worse: If you're significantly underpaid and nothing moves, you're at Hell.

Hell

Significantly underpaid, nothing moves.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • You're well below market.
  • Requests are ignored for years.
  • New joiners earn more for the same work.

Do

  1. Put it in writing, with evidence.
  2. If it could be discriminatory, ask about pay equality formally.
  3. Test the market seriously.
  4. Decide your walk-away point calmly.

Don’t: The bluff.

If it gets worse: If you get a real offer, read the Escape Portal before deciding.

VIIIHave you ever duelled?

Tick honestly.

  • You've never asked for a raise.
  • You don't know your market rate.
  • You accepted your current salary without negotiating.
  • You'd start the conversation with “sorry”.
  • You believe good work is rewarded automatically.

The cure, if you ticked two: Spend an hour finding three salary data points for your role. Write one sentence with a number. You don't have to use it yet. Just have it ready.

IXThe rehearsal

The Number, the silence, and Plan B.

Scene: Thursday 10 February, 14:00. Pip, Priya and Karen from HR.

PipI've been acting head for three months. Heads of insight at peer utilities earn £68,000 to £78,000. My results are on this page. I'm asking for an acting allowance of £10,000 a year, backdated to November.

Eleven seconds of silence. Pip counts them.

KarenThere's no budget for base pay changes this year.

PipUnderstood. The allowance isn't base pay. It's for the extra role, until it's filled.

Karen…That's possible. Not £10,000. Perhaps £7,500.

Pip£7,500, backdated to November, in writing, and a review of my base pay in July against the analytics lead band?

PriyaAgreed. Karen?

KarenAgreed. I'll send it today.

— the AnnotatorPip got 75% of his ask, backdated, plus a dated review. He'd have got none of it by being grateful.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

You want a raise. What do you bring?

Market data, your Book of Deeds summary, and one specific number.

They say “there's no budget this year”. What now?

Plan B: an allowance, a one-off bonus, a dated review in writing, or other benefits.

Should you mention a competing offer?

Only if you have one and would take it. Bluffs get called.

The Annotator's last wordSay the number. Then be quiet. The silence is not your problem.
