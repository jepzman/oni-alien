Passed Over: The Resurrection

You didn't get it. What you do in the next month decides what you get next.

Threat
Habitat
rejection emails, welcome balloons, the first 1:1 with your new boss
Weakness
feedback, gratitude, and a plan, in that order

IA sighting

Monday 6 March 2028 · 08:30 · the email

“We're delighted to announce that Ruth Calloway, currently Head of Insight at Northgate Energy, will join us as Head of Customer Insight on Monday.” Pip read it twice, then a third time in case the name changed.

08:31

Pip's phone buzzed: Priya, asking for a call at nine. Dorian, a message: “Gutted for both of us. Coffee? Not that kind.”

Monday 13 March · 09:00 · the welcome

There were balloons by the lift on floor 9. Pip stood on the other side of the glass wall for a moment, in the rain-grey light, watching people welcome Ruth. Then he walked in, held out his hand, and said, “Welcome. Let me know how I can help in your first month.” His voice did not shake. Afterwards he went to the bathroom on floor 9, and did not stare at the hand dryer.

That evening

Pip drew a small bird rising in the corner of his notebook. He didn't know what it meant yet. The Annotator, when he told her, said it was a phoenix, and that phoenixes need a fire first.

— the AnnotatorBeing passed over is a moment. How you behave the day after is a reputation.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow MetroVolt came to look outside

  1. 2019 · the CEO's view

    Dame Helena believes MetroVolt is too inward-looking: the same people, the same ideas. She asks for every senior role to consider external candidates.

  2. 2021 to 2026 · the outsiders

    Several senior roles go to outsiders. Some succeed; some leave in a year. Internal candidates learn that “strong field” often means “someone from Northgate”.

  3. 2027 · Ruth

    Ottoline has followed Ruth Calloway's work at Northgate for two years. When Head of Customer Insight appears, the prophecy includes an outsider before the advert.

  4. March 2028 · the decision

    The panel picks Ruth: ten years leading insight teams, against Pip's five months acting. It's defensible. It still hurts.

The turning point. A sensible push for fresh ideas that sometimes meant internal candidates were measured against people they never had a chance to become.

What might have saved them. Nothing unfair happened. What matters is what Pip does next.

— the AnnotatorSometimes you're passed over because someone better applied. That's the hardest and the most useful kind: it tells you exactly what to learn.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Being passed over hurts, especially if you acted in the role. The reasons are often reasonable (experience, an external strategy, a stronger candidate), but it still feels personal. What happens next depends mostly on you: how you behave publicly, what you learn, and whether your next step is here or elsewhere.

What the organisation watches

Whether you're gracious. People who handle disappointment well are remembered for the next role.

Why it hurts more than it should

Because you invested hope and evidence. Because colleagues know. Because, often, you were close.

What helps you rise

  • A dignified first day.
  • Specific feedback, a week later.
  • A plan with dates.
  • Deciding calmly whether to stay.

Three kinds of “no”

The Not Yet

Close, missing one thing. Learn it; you're next.

The Not Here

The organisation wanted something else. Consider other doors.

The Not This

The role wasn't right for you anyway. See the Peter Principle.

— the AnnotatorThe phoenix needs a fire first. Make sure the fire teaches you something.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • The job went to someone else, possibly from outside.
  • You were acting in the role.
  • Colleagues are watching how you'll react.
  • You're tempted to update your CV out of anger.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Dignified Day

    On the day: congratulate the winner, thank the panel, and say nothing else in public.

    incantation“Congratulations, Ruth. Welcome. Let me know how I can help in your first month.”

  2. 2The Feedback Ask

    A week later, ask for specific feedback: what would have made the difference?

    incantation“What would have made the difference for me? I'd like to be ready next time.”

  3. 3The Rising Plan

    Agree a development plan with your manager or sponsor, with dates. Decide whether the next step is here or elsewhere, calmly.

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Public Sulk

    Visible bitterness. It confirms they made the right choice.

  • The Sabotage Welcome

    Making the winner's start harder. They'll know, and so will everyone else.

  • The Rage Application

    Applying for any job elsewhere the same week. Leave if you should, but decide on a good day.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Not Yet: close, missing one thing.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Feedback is specific.
  • Your sponsor still backs you.
  • There's a clear gap.

Do

  1. Congratulate the winner.
  2. Ask for specific feedback.
  3. Close the gap with a dated plan.

Don’t: The public sulk.

If it gets worse: If the feedback is vague and the reasons unclear, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Not Here: an outsider won, or reasons are vague.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • The winner came from outside.
  • Feedback is “strong field”.
  • You acted in the role.

Do

  1. Ask a precise question: what would have made the difference?
  2. Help the winner succeed; it's noticed.
  3. Talk to your sponsor about your next step.
  4. Give it a month before deciding anything.

Don’t: The rage application.

If it gets worse: If you're passed over repeatedly, you're at Hell.

Hell

Passed over again and again.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • This is the second or third time.
  • The feedback changes each time.
  • Others with less evidence move up.

Do

  1. Ask, in writing, what specifically you need.
  2. If it might be unfair or discriminatory, raise it formally.
  3. Test the market: your evidence is valuable elsewhere.
  4. Use the Escape Portal (Act III) calmly.

Don’t: The sabotage welcome.

If it gets worse: Sometimes the resurrection happens somewhere else. That's still a resurrection.

VIIIHow would you take the fall?

Tick honestly.

  • You'd be visibly upset in front of colleagues.
  • You'd avoid the person who got the job.
  • You'd apply elsewhere the same week.
  • You wouldn't ask for feedback because it would hurt.
  • You'd quietly stop trying.

The cure, if you ticked two: Write your congratulations sentence now, for a role you want. If the day comes, you'll be glad it was already written.

IXThe rehearsal

The Feedback Ask and the Rising Plan, with the person who got the job.

Scene: Monday 20 March, Pip's first 1:1 with Ruth.

RuthI know you wanted this role. I'd understand if this is awkward.

PipIt's a bit awkward. I'm also glad you're here; your Northgate model is brilliant. What do you think I'd need to be ready for a role like yours?

RuthHonestly? You're a better analyst than I am. You haven't yet led people through a bad year. And I'm not sure you want to.

Pip…What makes you say that?

RuthYour best Friday lines are all about models. None of them are about 1:1s.

Pip goes home and reads his Book of Deeds from the start. Ruth is right. Next entry: the Peter Principle.

— the AnnotatorThe most useful feedback Pip ever got came from the person who beat him. It only happened because he welcomed her.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

You weren't selected. What do you say to the person who was?

Congratulations, welcome, and an offer of help. Mean it, or at least say it well.

When do you ask for feedback?

After a few days, when you can listen. Ask what would have made the difference.

Should you leave?

Maybe. Decide in a month, after feedback, with a clear head.
