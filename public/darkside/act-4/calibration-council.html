The Calibration Council

Your year, judged in four minutes, by people who've never seen your work.

Threat
Habitat
December meeting rooms, nine-box grids, rating distributions
Weakness
a manager armed with evidence, and senior people who already know your name

IA sighting

Tuesday 14 December · 09:00 · room 27A

Twelve managers, one HR partner, Ottoline at the end of the table, and a nine-box grid on the screen. Sixty names. Four hours. Four minutes a person, if nobody argued. Everybody argued.

10:40 · Asha

A manager from Network Ops said Asha seemed “quiet in meetings”. Pip, who had rewritten her review, said she'd onboarded four people in two weeks each and answered 312 data questions. Asha's card stayed in its box.

11:15 · Pip

Priya read four lines about Pip, from his Book of Deeds. Gareth said Pip had been “very helpful on the meter fix”. Ottoline said nothing, and wrote something down. Pip's card moved up one box.

11:22 · Dorian

Dorian's manager said he'd made “various strategic contributions”. Someone asked for an example. There was a pause long enough to be noticed. Dorian's card moved down one box. Nobody in the room had seen his work. Several had seen his slides.

— the AnnotatorYou won't be in the room. Make sure your evidence is, and that someone there has seen you.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the council came to sit

  1. 2015 · the inflation

    Every manager at MetroVolt rates their team highly. 70% of staff are “exceeds expectations”. The ratings mean nothing.

  2. 2016 · calibration

    HR introduces calibration: managers compare ratings across teams and agree a distribution. It's fairer in theory; nobody can inflate alone.

  3. 2017 to 2026 · the theatre

    In practice, the people who do well are those whose managers argue well, and whom senior people have seen. Quiet people and absent managers lose ground.

  4. 2027 · the council

    Sixty names, four hours, a grid. The judgement of a year depends on four minutes and who's in the room.

The turning point. A fix for inflated ratings that made ratings depend on advocacy and visibility as much as on work.

What might have saved them. Calibration based on written evidence, prepared in advance, rather than on who argues loudest in the room.

— the AnnotatorCalibration isn't random. It's a debate, and debates are won with evidence and witnesses.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Calibration is a meeting where managers compare their team's ratings and agree a final distribution. It's meant to make ratings fair across teams. In practice, your outcome depends on your manager's evidence, how well they argue, and whether senior people in the room know your work.

What the council wants

A defensible distribution: not everyone can be top-rated. Clear examples make a rating easy to defend.

Why good people lose ground

Because their managers are vague, absent or bad at arguing, and nobody senior has seen them. “Quiet” is often said, and rarely challenged.

What wins in the room

  • Specific examples with numbers.
  • Senior people who've seen your work.
  • A manager who speaks up.
  • Corroboration from other managers.

Three kinds of council

The Fair

Evidence-based, well-run. Prepare your manager and trust it.

The Loud

Wins go to the best arguers. Arm your manager; find corroborators.

The Fixed

Outcomes decided by a few. Build relationships with the few.

— the AnnotatorIf anyone in calibration might say “quiet”, make sure someone else is ready to say what you actually did.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Ratings are “subject to calibration”.
  • There's a target distribution (only so many top ratings).
  • Your manager can't tell you what was said about you.
  • Your rating changed between your review and the final result.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Armed Advocate

    Before calibration, give your manager the evidence they need: your Book of Deeds summary, in three bullet points they can say out loud.

    incantation“For calibration: three things. The complaint model (£410k), the Horizon submission, and onboarding four people. Anything else you need?”

  2. 2The Known Name

    Make sure at least two senior people in the room know your work directly.

  3. 3The Debrief

    Afterwards, ask your manager what was said and what you'd need to change next year.

    incantation“What were the concerns in calibration, and what would move me up next year?”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Rating Rage

    Fighting your rating angrily after calibration. It's usually locked; you'll just lose goodwill.

  • The Lobby

    Messaging calibration members to campaign for yourself. They'll mention it, and not kindly.

  • The Shrug

    Assuming calibration is random and preparing nothing. It's not random. It favours the prepared.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Fair council.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Calibration uses written evidence.
  • Your manager prepares well.
  • Outcomes are explained.

Do

  1. Give your manager three bullets with evidence.
  2. Ask for a debrief afterwards.
  3. Act on the feedback.

Don’t: The shrug.

If it gets worse: If the loudest arguers win, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Loud council.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Ratings depend on who argues.
  • Quiet people drift down.
  • Your manager isn't a strong advocate.

Do

  1. Arm your manager with sentences they can say out loud.
  2. Make sure two senior people have seen your work.
  3. Ask a peer manager who knows your work to corroborate.
  4. Get a sponsor (see the Sponsor's Mark).

Don’t: The lobby: messaging council members yourself.

If it gets worse: If outcomes are fixed by a few, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Fixed council.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Ratings mirror a few people's opinions.
  • Evidence doesn't change outcomes.
  • The same names rise every year.

Do

  1. Build direct relationships with the few, openly.
  2. Keep your evidence for appeals and CVs.
  3. Ask for written reasons for your rating.
  4. Consider where your work would be judged fairly.

Don’t: The rating rage.

If it gets worse: If it's unfair in a way linked to protected characteristics, raise it formally.

VIIIWould your name survive four minutes?

Imagine the council reaching your card. Tick honestly.

  • Your manager couldn't name three things you did this year, with numbers.
  • No senior person in calibration has seen your work.
  • Someone might describe you as “quiet”.
  • You've never asked what was said about you.
  • You've never given your manager anything before calibration.

The cure, if you ticked two: Before the next calibration, give your manager three sentences with evidence, and make sure one more senior person has seen your work this quarter.

IXThe rehearsal

The Armed Advocate, from the manager's chair.

Scene: Tuesday 14 December, 10:40. Room 27A. Pip speaks for Asha.

ManagerAsha seems quite quiet in meetings. Meets expectations?

PipShe's quiet in meetings and loud in results. She onboarded four people this year, each productive in two weeks instead of six. She answered 312 data questions. And she owns the regulator dashboard.

GarethI can back that. Her dictionary saved my team a month on the meter fix.

HRSo: exceeds?

OttolineExceeds. And let's stop using “quiet” as a rating.

Asha's card moves up one box. Pip tells her the next day exactly what was said, including Gareth's sentence.

— the AnnotatorTwo specific sentences and one corroborator beat “quiet”. That's calibration: evidence and witnesses.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Calibration is next week. What do you give your manager?

Three bullet points with evidence, short enough to say out loud in four minutes.

Your rating drops after calibration. What do you ask?

What was said, what concerns came up, and what would change it next year.

Why does it matter who knows your name?

Because in calibration, people defend those they've seen. A name nobody recognises is easy to move down.

The Annotator's last wordYou won't be in the room. Make sure your evidence is, and that someone there has seen you.
