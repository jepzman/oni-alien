The Title Without the Gold

Promoted! New title, new responsibilities, same salary.

Threat
Habitat
promotion emails, off-cycle promotions, “we'll fix pay in the next round”
Weakness
asking, before you accept, what the pay is, in writing

IA sighting

Friday 5 November 2027 · 16:40 · the email

Jules was promoted to Principal Analyst, Grid Analytics. The email from Victor's successor praised Jules's “strategic impact” and listed four new responsibilities. It did not mention pay.

16:42

Jules replied: “Thank you so much! Delighted to accept!” with two exclamation marks. Jules had wanted this for two years.

Monday 8 November

Jules asked about the salary. “It'll be reviewed in the next cycle,” said HR. The next cycle was in April. Principal sat in a pay band that started £9,000 above Jules's salary.

April 2028

In the April cycle, Jules got 3%, the same as everyone. Jules was now a Principal paid below the Principal band, with four new responsibilities. Jules told Pip over coffee, angry mostly at themself: “I said yes before I asked.”

— the AnnotatorAsk about the gold before you put on the crown.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow titles came loose from their gold

  1. 2020 · the frozen budget

    During the hiring freeze (Act V), MetroVolt can't raise pay but wants to keep people. It starts promoting “in title”, with pay to follow.

  2. 2021 · the habit

    Title promotions are cheap and popular. Pay rarely catches up, because the next cycle has its own budget limits.

  3. 2024 · the gap

    HR notices dozens of staff paid below their own band. Fixing it would cost millions. It goes on a list.

  4. 2027 · Jules

    Jules is promoted in title, off-cycle, by email. The pay is “next cycle”. The list gets one name longer.

The turning point. Using titles as a cheaper substitute for pay, and promising the pay later.

What might have saved them. One question before accepting: “What's the salary?” Jules's leverage disappeared with the word “delighted”.

— the AnnotatorThe moment of greatest leverage is between the offer and your yes. Spend it wisely.

IIIKnow thy enemy

A promotion without a raise is common, especially when budgets are tight. Sometimes it's a genuine stepping stone. Often it's a cheaper way to keep you. Your leverage is highest before you accept; after that, pay depends on the next cycle's budget and everyone else's needs.

What the organisation wants

To recognise and keep you, at the lowest cost this year.

Why the gold doesn't follow

Because “next cycle” has its own budget, and you're no longer a retention risk once you've said yes.

What gets you the gold

  • Asking about pay before accepting.
  • Knowing the band for the new role.
  • A dated, written promise.
  • Raising it promptly if forgotten.

Three kinds of crown

The Stepping Stone

A title now, pay agreed in writing for a date. Fine.

The Paper Crown

Title only, vague “next cycle”. Negotiate before yes.

The Crown of Thorns

Title, more work, no pay, no promise. Think carefully.

— the Annotator“Delighted to accept” is a lovely sentence. Say it after “what's the salary?”

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • A promotion is offered “off-cycle”.
  • The email mentions responsibilities, not pay.
  • “Pay will be reviewed in the next cycle.”
  • Your new title is in the next pay band; your salary isn't.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Question Before Yes

    Before accepting, ask what the new salary is, and get it in writing.

    incantation“Thank you, I'm delighted. Before I accept: what's the salary for the new role?”

  2. 2The Band Check

    Ask what the pay band for the new role is, and where you'd sit in it.

    incantation“What's the band for Principal, and where would I be in it?”

  3. 3The Dated Promise

    If pay can't move now, get a date and an amount in writing.

    incantation“If it can't change now, can we agree in writing that it moves to at least £X by July?”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Grateful Yes

    Accepting on the spot, thrilled, and asking about pay later. Later, you have no leverage.

  • The Silent Seethe

    Accepting, then resenting it for a year. Your manager doesn't know you're upset.

  • The Title Strike

    Refusing the new responsibilities until pay changes. It looks petty, even when you're right.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Stepping Stone.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Pay is mentioned, with a date.
  • The band is clear.
  • Your manager supports it.

Do

  1. Get the date and amount in writing.
  2. Accept.
  3. Diary the date.

Don’t: The grateful yes before the details.

If it gets worse: If pay is vague (“next cycle”), you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Paper Crown.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Pay isn't mentioned.
  • “Next cycle” is the only promise.
  • The new band starts above your salary.

Do

  1. Ask the salary and band before accepting.
  2. Ask for a dated minimum in writing.
  3. If needed, accept with a written review date.
  4. Raise it the day after the review if forgotten.

Don’t: The silent seethe.

If it gets worse: If you've accepted and pay never follows, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Crown of Thorns.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • You're below your own band.
  • Reviews pass with nothing.
  • Your responsibilities keep growing.

Do

  1. Raise it formally with the band as evidence.
  2. Ask HR how being below band is being addressed.
  3. Test the market: your new title helps.
  4. Decide your timeline calmly.

Don’t: The title strike: refusing the new work.

If it gets worse: If nothing changes, your title is now a strong line on your CV.

VIIIWould you take the crown without the gold?

Tick honestly.

  • You'd accept a promotion before asking about pay.
  • You don't know the pay band for your current role.
  • You think asking about pay at promotion is ungrateful.
  • You've been promised a raise “next cycle” before.
  • You're paid below your band and haven't said so.

The cure, if you ticked two: Find out your current band and where you sit. Then write the sentence you'll use at your next promotion: “Thank you. Before I accept, what's the salary?”

IXThe rehearsal

The Question Before Yes, and the Dated Promise.

Scene: Friday 5 November, 16:40. Jules, again, if Jules had read this entry.

Subject: RE: Promotion to Principal Analyst

JulesThank you, I'm delighted. Before I accept, could you confirm the salary for the Principal role, and the band?

HRThe band is £63k to £72k. Pay will be reviewed in the next cycle.

JulesThank you. Could we agree in writing that I'll move to at least the band minimum, £63k, by 1 January? Then I'm happy to accept today.

HR…I'll check with Finance.

In this version, Jules gets £63k from January. In the real one, Jules got 3% in April. Same person, same promotion, one email.

— the AnnotatorThe only difference between the two endings is the order of two sentences.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

You're offered a promotion by email. What do you reply first?

Thanks, and a question: what's the salary and band? Accept once you know.

Pay will be “reviewed next cycle”. What do you ask for?

A date and a minimum amount, in writing.

You already accepted without a raise. Is it too late?

No. Ask for a pay review now, with the band and your new responsibilities as evidence.
