The Rival for the Throne

Two candidates, one role, and a team watching how you both behave.

Threat
Habitat
internal recruitment, shared teams, the corridor after the interviews
Weakness
competing on results only, in the open, and staying gracious either way

IA sighting

Monday 10 January 2028 · the advert

Head of Customer Insight was advertised. Pip applied that morning. Dorian applied that afternoon. By lunch the next day, the whole floor knew.

January · the careful weeks

Conversations between them became very polite. Dorian started bringing two coffees again, and Pip, who knew exactly what that meant from Act II, felt the old tension rise. Colleagues began, quietly, to pick sides: Pip's analysts, Dorian's planners.

Friday 21 January · the corridor

Pip stopped Dorian by the lifts. “We both want this,” he said. “Let's both go for it properly, and whoever doesn't get it, we stay on good terms. Deal?” Dorian looked at him for a long moment. “Deal,” he said. “And Pip? I'm not doing the coffee thing. I just like coffee now.”

February · the interviews

Asked about Dorian's weaknesses, Pip said, “He presents better than I do, and he'd be good at it. Here's what I'd bring.” Asked about Pip, Dorian said, “He builds things. I'd make sure he's on slide one.” The panel looked at each other.

— the AnnotatorCompete like you'll have to work together afterwards. You will.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow Pip and Dorian came to want the same chair

  1. March 2026 · the theft

    Dorian presents Pip's forecast as his own (Act II, the Credit Vampire). Pip stares at a hand dryer.

  2. 2026 to 2027 · the distance

    Pip learns to work in daylight. Dorian's department sinks under him (Act V, the Ghost Ship).

  3. July 2027 · the lifeboat

    Pip offers Dorian a way into Customer Ops, on one condition: name the builders. Dorian accepts, and keeps the condition.

  4. January 2028 · the chair

    Both want Head of Customer Insight. Both deserve to be considered. Their history makes the rivalry dangerous, and also makes a clean contest possible.

The turning point. Two people with a bad history choosing, out loud, to compete cleanly.

What might have saved them. Nothing needed saving here. The open conversation in the corridor was the saving.

— the AnnotatorThe rivalry with the most history can be the cleanest, if someone names it first.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Competing with a colleague for a promotion is normal and can be healthy. It turns toxic when it goes underground: undermining, side-taking, quiet sabotage. The cure is to compete openly, on your own results, and to decide in advance how you'll behave whatever happens.

What both rivals want

The role, a fair contest, and not to be humiliated. Underneath, usually, to keep working together.

Why rivalries go bad

Because nobody names them. Silence breeds suspicion, teams pick sides, and every interaction becomes a move.

What keeps it clean

  • Naming the rivalry openly.
  • Your own case, never their weaknesses.
  • Graciousness planned in advance.
  • Keeping your teams out of it.

Three kinds of rival

The Sporting

Competes fairly. Match them; you'll both be stronger.

The Anxious

Competes nervously, may slip into whispering. Name it early.

The Ruthless

Will undermine. Stay in daylight; see Act II, the Doppelgänger.

— the AnnotatorWhatever happens, one of you will report to the winner, or work beside them. Behave accordingly.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • You and a peer are both clearly in the running.
  • Conversations between you have become careful.
  • Colleagues start picking sides.
  • You catch yourself hoping their project fails.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Open Rivalry

    Acknowledge the competition openly with your rival. It removes the tension of pretending.

    incantation“We both want this. Let's both go for it properly, and whoever doesn't get it, we stay on good terms. Deal?”

  2. 2The Own Case

    Make your case entirely about your results and your plan, never about their weaknesses.

  3. 3The Graceful Outcome

    Decide in advance how you'll behave if you lose, and if you win.

    incantation“Congratulations. Genuinely. How can I help you succeed in it?”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Shadow Campaign

    Undermining your rival behind their back. It's the fastest way to lose, and to deserve to.

  • The Sabotage

    Making their work harder: late data, missed invites. People notice patterns.

  • The Sore Winner

    Gloating, or making the loser's life hard afterwards. You'll need them.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Sporting rival.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • You get on well.
  • Both of you are open about applying.
  • The process is fair.

Do

  1. Name it and agree to stay friends.
  2. Make your own case.
  3. Plan your graceful outcome.

Don’t: The sore winner.

If it gets worse: If tension grows and teams pick sides, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Anxious rival, and a split team.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Conversations are careful.
  • Colleagues are taking sides.
  • You hear second-hand comments.

Do

  1. Have the open conversation in private.
  2. Tell your team to stay out of it.
  3. Keep collaborating visibly.
  4. Talk about your plan, not their gaps, in interviews.

Don’t: The shadow campaign.

If it gets worse: If your rival is actively undermining you, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Ruthless rival.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your work is being undermined.
  • Comments about you reach the panel.
  • Your rival controls something you need.

Do

  1. Stay in daylight: record, collaborate in writing.
  2. Raise specific interference with your manager.
  3. Make your case on evidence.
  4. Read Act II, the Doppelgänger.

Don’t: Sabotage in return.

If it gets worse: If the contest is unfair, ask HR about the process before the decision.

VIIIHow do you compete?

Tick honestly.

  • You've hoped a rival's project would fail.
  • You've mentioned a rival's weakness to someone deciding.
  • You avoid your rival since the role was advertised.
  • Your team has picked a side and you haven't stopped them.
  • You haven't thought about how you'd behave if you lost.

The cure, if you ticked two: Say it out loud to your rival: we both want this, let's compete fairly. Then plan your congratulations speech, for either outcome.

IXThe rehearsal

The Open Rivalry.

Scene: Friday 21 January, by the lifts on floor 9.

PipDorian. We both want this. Let's both go for it properly, and whoever doesn't get it, we stay on good terms. Deal?

Dorian…Deal. Honestly, I've been dreading this.

PipMe too. And let's tell our teams to stay out of it.

DorianAgreed. And Pip? I'm not doing the coffee thing. I just like coffee now.

PipI know. I've seen the slides. My name's on them.

Neither gets the job (see Passed Over). Both congratulate Ruth on the same morning, standing side by side.

— the AnnotatorThe rivalry with the worst history became the cleanest contest on floor 9, because one sentence was said in a corridor.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

You and a peer are both applying. What do you say to them?

Name it: we both want it, let's compete fairly and stay friends either way.

You're asked in interview about your rival's weaknesses. What do you say?

Talk about your strengths and plan. “Dorian would be good at it too. Here's what I'd bring.”

You win. What do you do in week one?

Talk to your rival first, privately. Ask what they'd like to own. Give them something real.
