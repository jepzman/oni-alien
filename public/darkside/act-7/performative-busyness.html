Act VII · The New Plagues · entry 11 of 12

The Performative Busy-ness Dance

An endless dance of early emails, packed calendars and loud sighs. Very tiring to watch. Produces almost nothing.

Threat
Habitat
calendars, early-morning emails, “crazy week” small talk, meetings about meetings
Weakness
measuring outcomes, protecting focus time, and not joining the dance

IA sighting

November 2029 · the dance

Hugo's week: 54 meetings. Emails timed to send at 06:12. A green dot (honest now, after the fire drill) from 07:00 to 22:00. Every conversation began with “crazy week”. He sighed audibly at every desk he passed. People said he was the hardest-working person on floor 14.

The quiet corner

Asha, at GridSight, had shipped Northshire's phase-two forecasts three days early, run the client workshop, and left at 16:30 every day to collect her son. Nobody said she was hard-working. Nobody said she wasn't. Nobody noticed.

Thursday 12 December · calibration

Rupert praised Hugo's “extraordinary commitment”. Ruth, in the room, asked one question: which of the programme's eleven milestones had been delivered? Three. Asha's GridSight work had hit all of its, early. Ruth, who had read the Calibration Council in Act IV, said so, with numbers.

Tuesday 14 January 2030 · Hugo

Hugo found Pip in the kitchen and said, quietly, that he was exhausted, and didn't know how to stop. Pip showed him his Outcome Ledger: one line per week, what got finished. Hugo cancelled twenty meetings the next day. Two of his milestones moved in a fortnight. He still says “crazy week”, but now he sounds surprised by it.

— the AnnotatorThe dance is exhausting to do and to watch. Sit it out. Ship something.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow Hugo learned to dance

  1. 2015 · his first boss

    Hugo's first manager judged people by who was in earliest and left latest. Hugo learned to be visible first, effective second.

  2. 2021 · the remote years

    With nobody to see him, Hugo fills his calendar and his chat with proof of life.

  3. 2026 · Lumen

    Act II's Hugo: always on Slack, never sleeping. It worked; he was promoted. The dance was rewarded.

  4. 2029 · the peak

    Fifty-four meetings a week. Three milestones of eleven. And, by January, a man who can't stop.

The turning point. A first boss who rewarded being seen, and every boss since who didn't ask what was delivered.

What might have saved them. One manager, at any point, asking “What did you finish this month?”

— the AnnotatorMost performative busy-ness isn't fakery. It's anxiety, trained by bosses who measured the wrong thing.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Performative busy-ness is the visible display of being busy (full calendars, early emails, constant availability, talk about how busy you are) as a substitute for, or distraction from, results. It's often rewarded, because it's easy to see, and it's contagious, because nobody wants to look less busy than the person next to them.

What it wants

To be seen as committed. Usually from fear of being seen as lazy.

Why it's rewarded

Because effort is visible and outcomes take effort to check. Busy people look valuable until someone counts.

What beats it

  • Counting outcomes.
  • Protected focus time.
  • Leaders who leave on time.
  • Not joining the competition.

Three kinds of dancer

The Anxious Dancer

Afraid to look idle. Show them the Outcome Ledger.

The Competitive Dancer

Out-busies everyone. Don't compete; ship.

The Exhausted Dancer

Can't stop. Needs help, not judgment. See Act VIII.

— the AnnotatorIf you manage people, the most powerful thing you can do is leave on time, visibly, having finished something.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • People compete to say how busy they are.
  • Emails sent at 06:00 are praised.
  • Calendars are full, outcomes are thin.
  • Leaving on time feels like a confession.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Outcome Ledger

    Track what you finished, not how hard you worked. Share it.

  2. 2The Focus Ward

    Block focus time in your calendar and defend it like a meeting.

    incantation“I've got focus time till eleven. Can we talk after, or by message?”

  3. 3The Calm Answer

    When someone asks how you are, don't say “so busy”. Say what you're working on.

    incantation“Good, thanks. Finishing the churn forecast this week.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Scheduled Send at 06:12

    Timing emails to look early. Everyone with the same trick knows.

  • The Busy Competition

    Answering “I'm busy” with “I'm busier”. Nobody wins. Everybody's tired.

  • The Meeting Hoard

    Accepting every invite to look important. Your calendar is not your CV.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Anxious Dancer, nearby.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • A colleague performs busy-ness.
  • It doesn't affect you much.
  • Your outcomes are visible.

Do

  1. Keep your Outcome Ledger.
  2. Use the Calm Answer.
  3. Protect your focus time.

Don’t: The busy competition.

If it gets worse: If busy-ness is what gets rewarded, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Rewarded Dance.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Leaders praise long hours.
  • Quiet effective people are overlooked.
  • You feel pressure to dance.

Do

  1. Make your outcomes visible weekly.
  2. Ask your manager to judge on outcomes.
  3. Find a sponsor (Act IV).
  4. Keep your hours sane.

Don’t: The scheduled send at 06:12.

If it gets worse: If the dancer is you, and you can't stop, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Exhausted Dancer is you.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • You can't remember your last free evening.
  • Your calendar runs your life.
  • You're not sure what you've achieved.

Do

  1. Write down what you finished last month.
  2. Cancel a third of your meetings.
  3. Tell someone you trust.
  4. Read Act VIII, the Slow Hex of Burnout.

Don’t: The meeting hoard.

If it gets worse: If you can't stop on your own, get help. That's not weakness.

VIIIAre you dancing?

Tick honestly.

  • You've said “crazy week” more than twice this week.
  • You've scheduled an email to send early to look keen.
  • You've accepted a meeting just to be seen in it.
  • You feel guilty leaving on time.
  • You can list your meetings but not your outcomes.

The cure, if you ticked two: Write down three things you finished this month. If it's hard, cancel three meetings next week and finish something.

IXThe rehearsal

The Outcome Ledger, offered kindly.

Scene: Tuesday 14 January 2030, the kitchen on floor 14.

HugoCan I ask you something? How do you leave at half five?

PipI finish things. I keep a list: one line a week, what got done. When the line's there, I go home.

HugoI've got fifty-four meetings this week. I don't know what I've finished.

PipWhich three would you miss if they vanished?

Hugo…Three. Out of fifty-four.

PipThen try keeping those, and finishing one thing by Friday.

— the AnnotatorHugo cancelled twenty meetings the next day. Nobody complained. Three people thanked him.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

A colleague always seems busier than you. Should you worry?

Only if they deliver more. Compare outcomes, not calendars.

You leave on time and feel guilty. Why?

Because the dance is loud. Your Outcome Ledger is the antidote.

You manage someone who performs busy-ness. What do you do?

Talk about outcomes and priorities. Kindly. They may not know another way.

The Annotator's last wordThe dance is exhausting to do and to watch. Sit it out. Ship something.
