Act VII · The New Plagues · entry 2 of 12

The Green Dot of Doom

A small green light that says you exist. Remote workers worship it. Managers watch it. It measures nothing.

Threat
Habitat
status lights, “last seen” times, cameras-on rules, instant replies at 22:00
Weakness
visible outcomes, regular written updates, and managers who judge work instead of dots

IA sighting

Tuesday 17 July 2029 · the remark

In a programme review, Hugo said, lightly: “I never see Asha online on Mondays.” Asha, remote on Mondays and Fridays, was not in the room. She was finishing GridSight's Northshire forecast, offline, so she could think.

That week · Hugo's dot

Hugo's dot was always green. At 06:12, at 23:40, on Sundays. People admired it. What they didn't know was that on his desk sat a small grey box, rocking his mouse gently back and forth, all day and all night.

Friday 20 July · the Friday Lantern

Pip told Asha what Hugo had said. She didn't argue. She started a Friday note to Pip, Ruth and Hugo: finished, next, stuck. Three lines. By September Hugo was forwarding them to Rupert as an example of “great visibility”.

Thursday 11 October · the fire drill

During the fire drill, someone knocked Hugo's desk. The grey box fell off and lay on the carpet, rocking the mouse in the empty office, keeping his dot green for nobody. Thirty people walked past it on the way back in. Hugo now works with his dot honestly set to “focus time”.

— the AnnotatorA green dot proves the computer is on. Show people what you did with it.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the green dot became a god

  1. 2014 · chat arrives

    MetroVolt adopts Slack. Status dots are a small courtesy: is someone around to answer?

  2. 2020 · the scattering

    With everyone at home, the dot is the only sign a colleague exists. Managers start glancing at it.

  3. 2023 · the watching

    A manager in Billing mentions dots in a review. Word spreads. People keep their dots green to be safe.

  4. 2029 · the jiggler

    Hugo, anxious since the Lumen days, buys a mouse jiggler. His dot never goes out. Asha's does, because she's working.

The turning point. A courtesy signal turning into a performance measure, because it was the only thing managers could see.

What might have saved them. Managers agreeing outcomes with remote people, so the dot never had to mean anything.

— the AnnotatorWhen people can't see work, they watch whatever they can see. Make the work visible and the dot stops mattering.

IIIKnow thy enemy

The green dot is a status indicator in chat tools. It was meant to show whether someone was available. In remote and hybrid work it's become a proxy for presence, effort and even commitment, which it measures badly. People game it; managers misread it; good remote workers get judged by it.

What it wants

Nothing. It's a light. The people watching it want reassurance that work is happening.

Why it's watched

Because work is invisible when you can't see someone, and the dot is the one thing that isn't.

What replaces it

  • A weekly written note.
  • Agreed outcomes.
  • Honest statuses.
  • Managers who ask, not watch.

Three kinds of dot-watcher

The Anxious Watcher

A manager who doesn't know what you're doing. The Friday Lantern cures it.

The Gossip Watcher

A colleague who comments on dots. Ignore, kindly.

The Tracking Watcher

A company that monitors activity formally. Know the policy and your rights.

— the AnnotatorIf your company formally monitors activity, it should tell you how. Ask for the policy; it's your data too.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Someone says “I never see you online”.
  • People reply instantly to look present.
  • Cameras-on becomes a rule, not a choice.
  • Your manager can't say what you delivered, but knows when you were away.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Friday Lantern

    Send a short weekly note: what you finished, what's next, what's stuck. It replaces the dot as evidence.

    incantation“This week: finished the churn forecast, started the tariff study. Stuck on data from Billing; I've asked Hamid.”

  2. 2The Honest Status

    Use your status to say what you're doing (“focus time till 11”) rather than pretending to be always on.

  3. 3The Outcome Conversation

    Ask your manager how they'd like to see your work, and agree it. Then the dot stops mattering.

    incantation“What would help you see what I'm working on, without watching my status?”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Mouse Jiggler

    Faking presence with a gadget. It's the least productive thing ever invented, and it gets found.

  • The Instant-Reply Reflex

    Answering everything within a minute to prove you're there. You'll never do deep work again.

  • Dot-Watching Others

    Commenting on a colleague's status. You don't know what they're doing. Neither does their dot.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Anxious Watcher.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your manager mentions your status.
  • They mean well.
  • They don't know what you're working on.

Do

  1. Start the Friday Lantern.
  2. Use honest statuses.
  3. Have the Outcome Conversation.

Don’t: The instant-reply reflex.

If it gets worse: If colleagues use your dot against you, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Gossip Watcher, in rooms you're not in.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Comments about your availability, in meetings.
  • You're remote; they're not.
  • It's affecting how you're seen.

Do

  1. Keep the Friday Lantern going, to a wider group.
  2. Ask your manager to vouch for your outcomes.
  3. Be visibly present at key moments.
  4. Don't argue about the dot.

Don’t: The mouse jiggler.

If it gets worse: If your company tracks activity formally, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Tracking Watcher.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Activity is monitored formally.
  • Keystrokes or screens are recorded.
  • It's used in reviews.

Do

  1. Ask for the monitoring policy in writing.
  2. Know your rights about workplace monitoring.
  3. Keep your outcome record.
  4. Decide whether you can work like this.

Don’t: Gaming the system. It's usually a disciplinary matter.

If it gets worse: If it's making you ill, read Act VIII.

VIIIAre you a dot-watcher?

Tick honestly.

  • You've mentioned someone's status in a meeting.
  • You've judged a colleague's effort by when they're online.
  • You keep your own dot green when you're not working.
  • You reply instantly to show you're present.
  • As a manager, you know when people log on but not what they delivered.

The cure, if you ticked two: Next time you wonder whether someone's working, ask them what they're working on. Then believe the answer.

IXThe rehearsal

The Friday Lantern.

Scene: Friday 20 July 2029, Pip calls Asha.

PipI want to tell you something Hugo said, so you hear it from me. He said he never sees you online on Mondays.

AshaBecause I'm working. Offline. On his programme's forecast, actually.

PipI know. I don't think arguing about the dot will help. What if you sent a three-line note every Friday?

AshaFinished, next, stuck?

PipExactly. To me, Ruth and Hugo. Then nobody needs to look at your dot.

AshaFine. But if Hugo starts sending one, I want to read it.

— the AnnotatorHugo started sending one in November. It was mostly meetings. By February it wasn't.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your manager says they “never see you online”. What do you do?

Start the Friday Lantern and have the Outcome Conversation.

A colleague's dot is always green. Are they working harder?

No idea. Neither do you. Look at what they deliver.

You manage remote people. How do you know they're working?

Agree outcomes and look at them. If you need a dot to know, the problem is the outcomes.
