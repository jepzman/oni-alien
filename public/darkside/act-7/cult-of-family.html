The Cult of Family

“We're a family here.” Lovely, until the family restructures you.

Threat
Habitat
values posters, onboarding decks, leadership speeches, requests for unpaid weekends
Weakness
remembering it's a team, not a family, and keeping your boundaries warm but firm

IA sighting

Monday 10 September 2029 · the banner

A banner went up in the atrium, three storeys high: “WE ARE THE METROVOLT FAMILY.” Imogen launched the campaign with a video: colleagues hugging, a cake, a line about “looking after each other like family”. Mugs followed.

Friday 14 September · the weekend

Ascend 2030's first systems went live on Saturday 22 September. Hugo asked Pip's team to “pull together like family” and work the weekend, unpaid, “because that's what families do”. Pip offered Thursday evening until eight, and Monday first thing. He declined Saturday. Hugo looked wounded. Pip felt guilty for a day. Then he read the next entry in the diary.

Tuesday 6 November · the restructure

Retail announced a restructure: 120 roles. Calendar invites went out at 08:00. In the atrium, under the banner, people from Retail walked to their meetings. The Chronicler, who'd seen this in 2009 under a banner that said “ONE BIG FAMILY”, stood beside Pip and said, quietly: “Families don't send calendar invites.”

January 2030 · the quiet replacement

The banner came down. A new one went up: “ONE METROVOLT TEAM.” Nobody announced the change. Imogen, asked about it by Pip at a skip-level, said: “Teams can be honest about change. Families can't.” She had, it turned out, argued for that wording in August, and lost.

— the AnnotatorLove your colleagues. Remember that the company can restructure them, and you.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow MetroVolt became a “family” (again)

  1. 1998 · the real family firm

    MetroVolt's founders really did know everyone's name. The word “family” started there, and was nearly true.

  2. 2009 · the first banner

    After the financial crisis, “ONE BIG FAMILY” goes up during a cost-cutting year. People remember the irony for a decade.

  3. 2027 · after Horizon

    Engagement scores fall after the Famine. Leadership wants warmth back. “Family” tests well in a focus group.

  4. 2029 · the campaign

    Imogen proposes “team”; the executive committee prefers “family”. The banner goes up two months before Retail's restructure.

The turning point. Using the warmest possible word to fix trust, during a year when trust was about to be tested again.

What might have saved them. Imogen's original wording: “team”. Warm, true, and honest about change.

— the Annotator“Family” at work usually means someone wants loyalty that a contract doesn't require. Give your care freely; keep your boundaries.

IIIKnow thy enemy

The cult of family is when an organisation describes itself as a family, usually sincerely, sometimes strategically. The feeling it describes (care, belonging, loyalty) is often real between colleagues. The problem is that a company can't be a family: it pays you, can restructure you, and expects you to leave one day. Calling it a family blurs those facts, and makes boundaries feel like betrayal.

What it wants

Loyalty and extra effort, freely given. Sometimes just warmth. Sometimes unpaid weekends.

Why it's dangerous

Because it makes normal things (saying no, asking for pay, leaving) feel like disloyalty, and makes restructures feel like betrayal.

What's healthier

  • Team, not family.
  • Care with boundaries.
  • Honesty about change.
  • Leaving well, without guilt.

Three kinds of family cult

The Sincere Family

Leaders really mean it. Enjoy the warmth; keep your boundaries anyway.

The Weekend Family

“Family” is used to get unpaid work. Warm boundary, every time.

The Guilt Family

Leaving is called betrayal. Leave well, without apology.

— the AnnotatorA good team cares about you and still lets you go. That's not less than family. It's more honest.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Leaders say “family” about the company.
  • Asking for overtime pay feels disloyal.
  • Weekend work is called “going above and beyond”.
  • Leaving is described as “betrayal”.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Team Lens

    Think of work as a sports team: committed, caring, and everyone knows people get traded.

  2. 2The Warm Boundary

    Care about your colleagues and still say no to unpaid extra work.

    incantation“I'd love to help with the launch. I can't do Saturday, but I can do Thursday evening till eight.”

  3. 3The Real Family Check

    Ask yourself: would a family do this? If not, it's not family. It's a job, which is fine.

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Total Devotion

    Giving the company everything because it called you family. It won't give everything back.

  • The Cynic's Sneer

    Mocking colleagues who genuinely care about each other. The caring is real. The family isn't.

  • Staying Out of Guilt

    Not leaving a job that's wrong for you because it would “let the family down”.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Sincere Family.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Leaders mean it warmly.
  • No unpaid extra work is expected.
  • People do look after each other.

Do

  1. Enjoy the warmth.
  2. Keep the Team Lens.
  3. Look after colleagues on your own terms.

Don’t: The cynic's sneer.

If it gets worse: If “family” is used to ask for unpaid work, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Weekend Family.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Weekend and evening asks.
  • Refusing feels disloyal.
  • “Family” appears in the ask.

Do

  1. Use the Warm Boundary.
  2. Offer what you can, clearly.
  3. Track your extra hours.
  4. Talk to your manager if it becomes regular.

Don’t: The total devotion.

If it gets worse: If leaving or saying no is called betrayal, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Guilt Family.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Leavers are spoken of badly.
  • Boundaries are treated as disloyalty.
  • Restructures happen anyway.

Do

  1. Keep your boundaries.
  2. Keep your CV current.
  3. Find colleagues who see it too.
  4. Leave well if you need to.

Don’t: Staying out of guilt.

If it gets worse: Read Act VIII, the Inner Sanctum.

VIIIAre you running the family cult?

Especially if you manage people. Tick honestly.

  • You've said “we're a family” to your team.
  • You've asked for weekend work by appealing to loyalty.
  • You've been hurt when someone resigned, and shown it.
  • You've described a leaver as “disloyal”.
  • You feel guilty when you take all your holiday.

The cure, if you ticked two: Replace “family” with “team” in your vocabulary for a month. Notice what you can say honestly that you couldn't before.

IXThe rehearsal

The Warm Boundary.

Scene: Friday 14 September 2029, Hugo asks Pip about the weekend.

HugoGo-live's Saturday. We need everyone. Families pull together, right?

PipI want it to go well too. I can do Thursday evening until eight, and be on first thing Monday.

HugoBut Saturday's the big day.

PipI can't do Saturday. If something breaks that's mine, ring me and I'll help by phone for an hour.

Hugo…Okay. That's actually helpful. Thanks.

— the AnnotatorNothing of Pip's broke on Saturday. Hugo worked fourteen hours. In January he asked Pip how he'd said no so easily.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your boss says “we're a family” and asks you to work the weekend. What do you do?

The Warm Boundary: offer what you can, decline what you can't.

Is it wrong to love your team?

No. Love your team. Just don't confuse it with the company.

The company calls leaving “betrayal”. How do you leave?

Politely, professionally, with good notice. Teams expect trades.

The Annotator's last wordLove your colleagues. Remember that the company can restructure them, and you.
