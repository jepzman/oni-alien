The Engagement Survey Oracle

It promises your answers are anonymous. Your team has five people. Your manager can read.

Threat
Habitat
the annual survey, pulse checks, “you said, we did” posters
Weakness
knowing what's really anonymous, writing what's useful, and following up in person

IA sighting

Thursday 1 November 2029 · the oracle speaks

Pulse, MetroVolt's annual engagement survey, opened: forty questions, “two minutes”, “completely anonymous”. It asked for team, grade, location and length of service. Results would be reported for any group of five or more. Ruth's team had six people.

November · the comments

Pip wrote one comment, the kind he'd say in person: “We've been covering two roles since March. Agreeing priorities would help more than anything.” Someone else in the team wrote four hundred furious words about Ascend, including a phrase they used in every meeting.

Monday 3 December · the reading

Ruth read her team's results twice. She recognised every voice; with six people, how could she not? She did not try to work out who wrote what. She told Pip later it took real effort not to. Hugo, in another team, asked two people directly whether they'd written a comment. Imogen heard about it within the day and stopped it.

Tuesday 4 December · the Manager's Rite

Ruth shared the team's results in full. “Three of you said priorities are unclear. You're right. From January we'll agree the top three each month, and I'll say no to anything else.” She also said: “Someone's very angry about Ascend. I hear it. If you want to talk, my door's open; if not, that's fine.” The angry writer came to see her a week later.

— the AnnotatorWrite what you'd say out loud. Then, if it's safe, say it out loud.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the oracle came to ask so much

  1. 2012 · the first survey

    MetroVolt runs its first engagement survey. Results are shared company-wide. Nothing changes. Response rates fall.

  2. 2018 · the drill-down

    To make results actionable, the survey starts reporting by team. Groups get smaller. Anonymity gets thinner.

  3. 2024 · the threshold

    The minimum reporting group is lowered from ten to five, “so managers have something to act on”.

  4. 2029 · the oracle

    Pulse is useful and dangerous at once: actionable for managers, and recognisable for small teams.

The turning point. Making results small enough to act on, without thinking about whether they were still anonymous.

What might have saved them. A reporting threshold of ten, and clear guidance for managers on what not to do with comments.

— the AnnotatorAn anonymous survey is only as anonymous as its smallest group and its most curious manager.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Engagement surveys ask employees how they feel about their work, team and leaders. They can drive real change when results are shared and acted on. But “anonymous” often means “not named”, not “unidentifiable”: in small teams, managers can often guess, and comments in someone's usual phrasing are easy to recognise.

What it wants

Honest data. And, often, a good score to show leadership.

Why it's mistrusted

Because previous surveys changed nothing, and because people suspect (often rightly) that small teams aren't really anonymous.

What makes it useful

  • Specific, kind comments.
  • Managers who share and act.
  • No author hunts.
  • One visible change.

Three kinds of oracle

The True Oracle

Large groups, real action. Be honest.

The Thin Veil

Small teams, recognisable comments. Write what you'd say in person.

The Hollow Oracle

Nothing ever changes. Answer briefly, and say the important things directly.

— the AnnotatorIf you manage people: the worst thing you can do with survey results is hunt for authors. The second worst is nothing.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • The survey says “completely anonymous” and asks your team, grade and location.
  • Last year's “you said, we did” was a new coffee machine.
  • Your manager says “please be honest” a bit too hard.
  • Results are shared by team, and your team is tiny.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Anonymity Check

    Find out the minimum group size for reporting. Under ten, assume your manager can guess.

  2. 2The Useful Comment

    Write what you'd be happy to say in person, specifically and kindly. It's more likely to change something.

    incantation“Our team is asked to cover two roles since March. Agreeing priorities would help more than anything else.”

  3. 3The Manager's Rite (if you manage)

    Share your results with the team, say what you heard, and pick one thing to change. Never hunt for authors.

    incantation“Three of you said priorities are unclear. You're right. Here's what I'm changing.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Venting Essay

    Writing a furious anonymous essay. It's recognisable, and fury is easy to dismiss.

  • The All-Fives Lie

    Ticking everything “strongly agree” to be safe. Then nothing changes.

  • The Author Hunt

    If you're a manager: trying to work out who wrote what. It destroys trust instantly.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The True Oracle.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Large reporting groups.
  • Last year's results were acted on.
  • Managers share results.

Do

  1. Answer honestly.
  2. Write one useful comment.
  3. Join the follow-up conversation.

Don’t: The all-fives lie.

If it gets worse: If your team is small, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Thin Veil.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your team is under ten.
  • Comments are shown to your manager.
  • Your phrasing is recognisable.

Do

  1. Do the Anonymity Check.
  2. Write the Useful Comment.
  3. Say important things in person too.
  4. Keep it kind.

Don’t: The venting essay.

If it gets worse: If your manager hunts for authors, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Author Hunt.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your manager asks who wrote what.
  • People are treated differently after.
  • Trust is gone.

Do

  1. Don't confirm or deny.
  2. Tell HR; author hunts are usually against policy.
  3. Talk to your skip-level.
  4. Keep notes.

Don’t: Writing anything you'd regret being linked to.

If it gets worse: If it continues, read the Escape Portal in Act III.

VIIIAre you misusing the oracle?

As a respondent or a manager. Tick honestly.

  • You've written survey comments you'd never say in person.
  • You've ticked everything positive to be safe.
  • As a manager, you've tried to guess who wrote a comment.
  • You've never shared your team's results.
  • You've complained nothing changes but never said what should.

The cure, if you ticked two: Write one comment this year that you'd be happy to sign. Then, if it's safe, sign it, by saying it in your next 1:1.

IXThe rehearsal

The Manager's Rite.

Scene: Tuesday 4 December 2029, Ruth's team meeting.

RuthHere are our full Pulse results. I'm not hiding any of it.

RuthThree of you said priorities are unclear. You're right. That's on me.

PipWhat changes?

RuthFrom January we agree the top three each month. Anything else, I say no to, on your behalf.

RuthAnd someone's very angry about Ascend. I hear it. My door's open. If you'd rather not, that's fine too.

— the AnnotatorEngagement in Ruth's team rose eleven points the following year. She credits one sentence: “That's on me.”

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your team has five people. Is the survey anonymous?

Technically, maybe. Practically, assume not. Write what you'd say in person.

What should you write?

One specific, useful point, kindly said.

You're a manager with bad results. What do you do?

The Manager's Rite: share, acknowledge, change one thing. Never hunt.
