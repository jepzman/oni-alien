The All-Hands Mass

Once a quarter the faithful gather to hear the liturgy: exciting times, doubling down, our journey. Nobody knows what it means.

Threat
Habitat
town halls, quarterly all-hands, CEO videos, pre-submitted Q&A
Weakness
a decoder, attention to what isn't said, and one well-asked question

IA sighting

Thursday 11 October 2029 · the questions

Staff were invited to submit questions for the quarterly all-hands, “to make the best use of time”. Tamsin, on floor 27, read all 214 of them a week early. Forty-one asked about Ascend and jobs. Six were chosen. None of the forty-one.

Thursday 18 October · the mass

The atrium, chairs in rows, a lectern, a screen of rising arrows. Dame Helena spoke for thirty-five minutes. “Exciting times.” “Doubling down on networks.” “Our journey to 2030.” “Right-sizing our cost base.” Retail wasn't mentioned once. Customer was mentioned twice, both times as “our customers”, not the teams.

18 October · the Q&A

Five pre-submitted questions were answered (one about parking). Then, unexpectedly, two live questions. Pip, who'd prepared one in case, put his hand up: “Thanks, Helena. You mentioned doubling down on networks. What does that mean for Customer teams this year?” A pause. Then a real answer: Customer headcount stable this year, AI tools to free capacity, no further restructures planned in Customer “that I'm aware of today”.

Tuesday 6 November · the silence explained

Retail's restructure was announced. It had not been mentioned at the all-hands. The Chronicler, who'd noted the silence on a napkin (“Retail: 0 mentions”), had told their friend in Retail to update her CV a fortnight earlier.

— the AnnotatorThe liturgy is mostly noise. The silences are the sermon.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the all-hands became a mass

  1. 2005 · the old town hall

    MetroVolt's CEO takes open questions in the canteen. Some are angry. Some answers are honest. People remember it fondly.

  2. 2016 · the comms team

    An angry question goes viral internally. All-hands get scripted. Questions are submitted in advance.

  3. 2022 · the arrows

    Slides become strategy art. Nobody presents a number that could be wrong. The language becomes safe and empty.

  4. 2029 · the mass

    Dame Helena genuinely wants to connect. But the format, built to avoid risk, turns her sincerity into liturgy.

The turning point. One bad moment in 2016, which made every all-hands since a performance designed to avoid another.

What might have saved them. Keeping some live questions, and a CEO willing to say “I don't know yet”.

— the AnnotatorMost leaders hate the liturgy too. They're trapped in it. A good question is a key.

IIIKnow thy enemy

The all-hands is a regular meeting where leadership speaks to everyone. It's often heavily scripted and full of leadership language that sounds meaningful and says little. But it isn't useless. What's said, what's emphasised and especially what isn't mentioned can tell you a lot.

The decoder ring

Exciting times: change is coming. Doubling down on X: less of everything else. Right-sizing: fewer people. Our journey: no end date. No plans at this time: not yet announced. Efficiencies: cuts.

Why it's scripted

Because one bad answer can travel the whole company in an hour. Leaders protect themselves with vagueness.

What to listen for

  • Areas not mentioned.
  • Promises from last quarter, missing.
  • New words repeated often.
  • Answers that change the subject.

Three kinds of mass

The Real Town Hall

Live questions, honest answers. Ask well; it's rare.

The Liturgy

Scripted, safe, vague. Decode it and read the silences.

The Pre-Announcement

Unusually vague, moved at short notice. Something's coming; read Act V.

— the AnnotatorAsk your manager after every all-hands: “What did you hear that I might have missed?” They often know.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • The all-hands is moved forward a week.
  • Questions must be submitted in advance.
  • The slides have more arrows than numbers.
  • The word “journey” appears in the title.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Decoder Ring

    Translate the liturgy. “Exciting times” means change. “Doubling down” means cutting the rest. “Rightsizing” means fewer people.

  2. 2The Silence Reading

    Listen for what isn't mentioned: your area, last quarter's promise, the thing everyone's talking about.

  3. 3The Good Question

    Ask a specific, kind, answerable question. Vague angry questions get vague answers.

    incantation“Thanks, Helena. You mentioned doubling down on networks. What does that mean for Customer teams this year?”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Ambush

    Asking a hostile question to make a point. You'll get applause and a reputation.

  • The Total Tune-Out

    Ignoring every all-hands. Occasionally, between the arrows, they tell you something important.

  • Taking the Liturgy Literally

    Believing “no plans for large-scale changes” means no changes. See Act V.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Real Town Hall.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Live questions are taken.
  • Leaders answer directly.
  • Numbers are shown.

Do

  1. Listen properly.
  2. Ask a specific question if you have one.
  3. Share what you learned with your team.

Don’t: The total tune-out.

If it gets worse: If it's all script and arrows, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Liturgy.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Pre-submitted questions only.
  • Lots of jargon, few facts.
  • Your area isn't mentioned.

Do

  1. Use the Decoder Ring.
  2. Note the silences.
  3. Ask your manager what they heard.
  4. Prepare one good question anyway.

Don’t: Taking the liturgy literally.

If it gets worse: If it's moved at short notice and unusually vague, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Pre-Announcement.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • It was moved forward suddenly.
  • Words like “right-sizing” appear.
  • Leaders look tired.

Do

  1. Read Act V, Reading the Omens.
  2. Update your Go-Bag Scroll.
  3. Talk to your manager privately.
  4. Don't panic publicly.

Don’t: The ambush.

If it gets worse: If invites arrive, read the Day of the Calendar Invite.

VIIIDo you preach the liturgy?

If you ever present to a team. Tick honestly.

  • You've said “exciting times” instead of saying what's changing.
  • You've avoided mentioning a team because the news was bad.
  • You only take questions you've seen in advance.
  • Your slides have more arrows than numbers.
  • You've said “no plans at this time” knowing plans existed.

The cure, if you ticked two: In your next team update, replace one phrase with the plain fact. “We're cutting the budget by 10%” is scarier to say and kinder to hear.

IXThe rehearsal

The Good Question.

HostWe have time for two live questions.

PipThanks, Helena. You mentioned doubling down on networks. What does that mean for Customer teams this year?

— the Annotator“That I'm aware of today” was, the Chronicler noted, the most honest phrase of the afternoon. Customer was not restructured that year.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

The CEO says “exciting times ahead”. What does it mean?

Change is coming. Listen for where.

Your area isn't mentioned at all. Good or bad?

Could be either. Ask your manager what they heard about it.

You have a real question. How do you ask it?

Specific, kind and answerable. If it's sensitive, ask your manager or skip-level first.
