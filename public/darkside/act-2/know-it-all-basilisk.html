The Know-It-All Basilisk

One glance at your idea and it turns to stone.

Threat
☠☠☠☠☠
Habitat
architecture reviews, technical forums, the comments on your doc
Weakness
data, questions, and being invited to own part of the answer

IA sighting

Dr. Lionel Ashe, principal engineer, was right in 2011 about the transformer replacement programme, and has been reminding people ever since. When Pip presented a simple machine-learning model for predicting transformer failures, Lionel did not look up from his laptop. “That won't work. We've got thirty years of physics for this. Next.”

The idea was turned to stone in four seconds. It had taken Pip three months.

IIKnow thy enemy

The Basilisk is usually genuinely expert, and that's the problem. Its identity rests on being the one who knows. New ideas from junior people are a threat to that identity, so it kills them on sight, before they can grow.

The trick: don't look it in the eye. Don't challenge its expertise; use it. A Basilisk invited to improve an idea will often defend it fiercely, because now it's partly theirs.

— the AnnotatorThe Basilisk is right more often than you'd like. Listen for the 20% that's true before you dismiss the 80% that's rude.

IIISigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • “We tried that.” “That won't scale.” “Next.”
  • They answer questions nobody asked, at length.
  • Juniors stop proposing anything in their meetings.
  • They reply to your 12-page doc after reading the title.

IVCounter-spells

  1. 1The Mirror Shield

    Answer with data, not opinion. Ask what evidence would change their mind.

    incantation“Fair challenge. On the 2018–2024 data the model caught 31 of 38 failures. What would you need to see to trust it?”

  2. 2The Apprentice's Question

    Ask for their expertise before the meeting. People rarely destroy what they helped build.

    incantation“Lionel, before I present, could you poke holes in this? You know the physics better than anyone.”

  3. 3The Small Pilot

    Propose a small, safe test instead of a big change. Basilisks hate risk more than they hate new ideas.

    incantation“Could we run it in shadow mode on twenty transformers for three months? No decisions on it, just a comparison.”

VForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Stare-Down

    Challenging their expertise in public. “Actually, physics models are outdated.” They have a doctorate and twenty years. You have a notebook.

  • The Workaround

    Building it anyway behind their back. When it's discovered, the fight is no longer about the idea, it's about trust.

  • Turning to Stone

    Giving up on all ideas. You'll be safe and invisible, which at MetroVolt is how people become Neville.

VIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

A confident expert having a rushed day. Ask for specifics and offer data later. Usually fine on the second try.

Nightmare

Kills every new idea on sight. The Apprentice's Question before every proposal, and Small Pilots to build a track record.

Hell

The Basilisk owns the decision and won't move. Find a sponsor above them who cares about the outcome, run the safest possible pilot, and let the results argue for you.

VIIILoot drop

⚿The Pre-Review Ask

Send to the expert a week before you present. It turns a judge into a co-author.

Hi Lionel,

I'm presenting a transformer-failure model at the asset review on
the 14th. Before I do, I'd really value your eyes on it:

  1. Is there a failure mode the data can't see? (you'll know)
  2. Where would you not trust it?
  3. What test would convince you?

20 minutes any day next week? Coffee's on me.

IXThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

The Basilisk says “that won't work” and moves on. What do you say?

“Fair. What's the main reason?” Then write it down. Either you learn something real, or they have to say something specific you can test.

You have a new idea in their area. Who do you talk to first?

The Basilisk, privately, with the Pre-Review Ask. Never let them see it for the first time in front of an audience.

They're partly right: the model misses one failure type. What now?

Say so, credit them, and add it. “Lionel spotted the moisture failure mode, so we've added it.” Your idea just got their name on it, and their defence.

The Annotator's last wordDon't fight the expert. Recruit them. Then they fight for you.
