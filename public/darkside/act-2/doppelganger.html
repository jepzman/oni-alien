The Doppelgänger

Lovely to your face. A different creature in rooms you're not in.

Threat
☠☠☠☠☠
Habitat
1:1s with your manager, calibration meetings, the pub after work
Weakness
witnesses, consistency, and your own relationships

IA sighting

Saskia is Pip's peer and favourite person in Grid Planning. She brings him cardamom buns. She says his forecast work is “honestly the best in the team”. On the 11th of September, Pip's manager mentioned, gently, that “some people” felt Pip “didn't really collaborate” and was “a bit precious about his models”.

Pip had collaborated with exactly one person that quarter. She had brought buns.

IIKnow thy enemy

The Doppelgänger is competing with you, usually for the same promotion, budget or reputation, and has decided that open competition is too risky. So it is warm in public, where warmth is rewarded, and undermining in private, where nobody checks.

Two cautions. First, don't jump to it: feedback that hurts is not proof of a traitor, and most colleagues are exactly what they seem. Second, if it's real, you will not beat it by being a better spy. You beat it by having your own relationships, so that when your name comes up, other people already know you.

— the AnnotatorThe cure for whispering is not a louder whisper. It's people who know you well enough not to believe it.

IIISigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Feedback about you arrives vague and second-hand: “some people feel…”.
  • Your manager seems to know things you only told one person.
  • Their praise is always private, their concerns always in groups.
  • They are often in the room when your work is discussed and you are not.

IVCounter-spells

  1. 1The Clarifying Question

    When vague feedback arrives, ask for specifics. It tests whether it's real and whether it's fair.

    incantation“Thanks for telling me. Can you give me an example, so I can fix it? Which project was it?”

  2. 2The Wide Web

    Build direct relationships with your manager, skip-level and the people who use your work. Then nobody's opinion of you comes only through one voice.

    incantation“I'd love a monthly half-hour with you to hear how the forecasts are landing for Ops.”

  3. 3The Open Hand

    Bring collaboration into the open: invite them into your work visibly, credit them in writing. It's hard to call you precious when there's an email thread of you sharing.

    incantation“Saskia, want to co-present the model method at the team review? You know the data well.”

  4. 4The Quiet Test

    Tell them something harmless and specific, then see where it reappears. Use once. It's a test, not a hobby.

VForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Counter-Whisper

    Undermining them back. Now there are two Doppelgängers and your manager trusts neither.

  • The Confrontation Ambush

    Accusing them in front of others with no evidence. They will be shocked and hurt, convincingly. You will look paranoid.

  • The Bunker

    Trusting nobody, sharing nothing. You will become exactly what they said you were: precious and unco-operative.

VIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

A misunderstanding: a peer gave honest, clumsy feedback. Ask for examples, fix what's fair, move on.

Nightmare

A rival with a habit. Widen your web, collaborate in writing, keep your work in daylight. Over a few months the whispers stop matching what people see.

Hell

A skilled operator who has your manager's ear. Build your skip-level relationship, ask your manager directly for specific examples, keep a record of your collaboration, and consider whether this team can be won.

IXThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your manager says “some people” find you hard to work with. What do you say first?

Thank them, then ask for a specific example. Don't guess who said it out loud; it makes you look defensive and is often wrong.

You're fairly sure who it is. Do you confront them?

Not with an accusation. You can say: “I heard there's a concern about how we work together. If anything bugs you, I'd rather hear it from you.” It tells them you know, without a scene.

What's the long-term cure?

The Wide Web. When your manager, skip-level and users know you directly, one voice behind your back loses most of its power.
