Act VI · The Outer Realms · entry 8 of 12

The Frenemy Pact

Your partner on Monday, your competitor on Tuesday, and very interested in what you said on Monday.

Threat
Habitat
joint bids, partnerships, industry groups, “let's collaborate”
Weakness
a written agreement, clear walls around what's shared, and no illusions

IA sighting

October 2028 · the partnership

The regulator opened a regional flexibility competition: pay utilities to shift demand away from peak hours. Neither GridSight nor Northgate Energy could win it alone. Theo Blake, Northgate's partnerships lead, proposed a joint bid. Ruth, who used to work at Northgate, said yes, and then said: “Write everything down.”

The Written Pact

Pip drafted one page: the joint work, what each side shared (demand data for three regions), what each kept (pricing, client lists, other bids), and who owned the model they built together. Theo signed it cheerfully. “Very thorough!”

Thursday 18 January 2029 · the other hand

At the end of a joint workshop, Theo asked Pip, casually, how GridSight priced Wexley's phase two. Pip smiled and said that was outside the pact. Theo laughed: “Worth a try!” In February, Northgate bid against GridSight for Wexley's phase three. Pip had known they would. It was in the Open Rivalry conversation, in October.

March 2029 · both outcomes

The joint flexibility bid won: £1.2 million across both firms. Wexley stayed with GridSight. Theo sent a bottle of wine with a card: “Good game. Partners again in the autumn?” Pip said yes, and started drafting the next pact.

— the AnnotatorA frenemy isn't a traitor. They're a competitor who's honest enough to work with you. Be honest back, in writing.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow Northgate became a frenemy

  1. 2010s · rivals

    MetroVolt and Northgate compete for the same customers, the same staff and the same regulators. They don't talk.

  2. 2024 · the regulator

    The regulator starts funding regional projects that need several utilities to share data. Rivals must now cooperate to compete.

  3. 2027 · Ruth moves

    Ruth Calloway leaves Northgate for MetroVolt. She knows exactly how Northgate thinks, and likes them anyway.

  4. 2028 · the pact

    A joint bid makes sense. So does competing elsewhere. Both are true at once. That's a frenemy.

The turning point. Markets where you must partner with your competitors to win anything big.

What might have saved them. Nothing needed saving. Frenemy pacts are normal and useful. What matters is writing the rules.

— the AnnotatorMost industries now run on frenemies. The ones that last are the ones with a one-page pact.

IIIKnow thy enemy

A frenemy is a partner who is also a competitor: you bid together for one thing and against each other for another. This is normal in most industries, and it can work well. It goes wrong when nobody writes down what's shared, and information from the partnership leaks into the competition.

What it wants

To win the joint prize, and to learn anything useful along the way. Both are reasonable. Only one is in the pact.

Why it probes

Because it's a competitor, and competitors are curious. It isn't personal. Don't take it personally, and don't answer.

What keeps it honest

  • A written, one-page pact.
  • Separate people for joint and competing work, where possible.
  • Saying the rivalry out loud.
  • Competition law (yes, it applies).

Three kinds of frenemy

The Honest Rival

Competes openly, partners fairly. Keep them.

The Prober

Always asking one question too many. Smile, and point to the pact.

The Raider

Uses what you share to beat you. End the partnership, and take advice.

— the AnnotatorNever share pricing with a competitor, even in a partnership. In many countries that's not just unwise, it's against competition law.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Your partner asks questions that aren't about the joint project.
  • You're bidding together for one client and against each other for another.
  • Nobody has written down what's shared and what isn't.
  • Your contact is very friendly, and very curious.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Written Pact

    Before working together, write down what you're doing jointly, what's shared, and what isn't.

    incantation“Before we start, let's agree a one-page note: what's joint, what stays ours, and who owns what we build.”

  2. 2The Clean Wall

    Keep the people and data on the joint work separate from the work where you compete.

  3. 3The Open Rivalry

    Say plainly where you compete. Frenemies who admit it stay friends longer.

    incantation“Just so it's said: we're both bidding for Wexley's next phase. Let's keep that off this project entirely.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Trusting Overshare

    Sharing your pricing, plans or client names with a partner who competes with you.

  • The Secret Raid

    Using what a partner shared in confidence to beat them elsewhere. Illegal in some cases, fatal to trust in all.

  • The Paranoid Freeze

    Sharing so little that the joint project fails. Then you're just rivals who wasted a quarter.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Honest Rival.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • The rivalry is openly acknowledged.
  • There's a written pact.
  • Both sides stick to it.

Do

  1. Keep the pact current.
  2. Review what's shared each quarter.
  3. Congratulate them when they win.

Don’t: The paranoid freeze.

If it gets worse: If they keep probing, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Prober.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Casual questions about your other work.
  • Friendly, persistent curiosity.
  • Requests for “just a bit more” data.

Do

  1. Point to the pact, kindly.
  2. Tell your boss about each probe.
  3. Separate the people on joint and competing work.
  4. Check with legal what you may share.

Don’t: The trusting overshare.

If it gets worse: If they use what you shared against you, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Raider.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • They've used your shared data to compete.
  • They deny it.
  • Your clients have heard your numbers.

Do

  1. Document what happened.
  2. Take legal advice.
  3. End or suspend the partnership.
  4. Tell affected clients what you can, honestly.

Don’t: The secret raid back.

If it gets worse: Never partner with them again without a much tighter pact.

VIIIAre you the Frenemy?

With other firms or other teams in your own company. Tick honestly.

  • You've asked a partner a question you knew was outside the agreement.
  • You've used something learned in a partnership to compete.
  • You've never said the rivalry out loud.
  • You've been friendly mainly to get information.
  • You think “worth a try” is a business ethic.

The cure, if you ticked two: Before your next joint project, write one page: what's shared, what isn't. Then keep to it, even when it would be easy not to.

IXThe rehearsal

The Written Pact and the Open Rivalry.

Scene: Tuesday 10 October 2028, Pip and Theo, before signing.

PipBefore we start, can we agree one page? What's joint, what stays ours, who owns the model.

TheoVery thorough. Sure.

PipAnd just so it's said: you'll probably bid for Wexley's next phase. We'll probably bid for your water clients.

TheoHa. Probably. Is that a problem?

PipNot if we keep it off this project entirely. Different people, no pricing, no client names.

TheoAgreed. Honestly, it's a relief to say it.

— the AnnotatorTheo's probe in January was, he admitted later, “a bit of a test”. Pip passed it. So the partnership continued.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your partner asks about your pricing for a client you compete for. What do you say?

Kindly, that it's outside the pact. Then check the written agreement covers it.

Should you never partner with a competitor?

No. Partner when it helps, with a written pact and a clean wall.

You lose a client to your partner. What now?

Congratulate them, learn why, and keep the joint work clean. You'll need each other again.
