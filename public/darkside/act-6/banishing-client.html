Banishing a Client

Some clients cost more than they pay: in money, in time, and in the people who have to deal with them.

Threat
Habitat
unprofitable accounts, abusive calls, bad debts, “we can't afford to lose them”
Weakness
the true cost written down, a clean exit, and a team that knows you'll protect them

IA sighting

August 2028 · the signing

Castellan Homes, a property developer, hired GridSight for grid-connection studies on new estates. Rex Dunmore, their managing director, was charming to Felix, generous with lunches and very keen on discounts. The contract was £90,000 a year.

Autumn · the cost

Castellan paid every invoice at 75 days on 30-day terms. Rex wanted calls at short notice and changes for free. With Pip he was charming. With Asha he was not: he interrupted her, called her “the girl with the spreadsheet”, and on Thursday 11 January 2029 shouted at her on a call because a substation's capacity was lower than he'd hoped.

January · the True Cost Ritual

Asha told Pip, shaking. Pip told Ruth the same day. Then he counted: 410 hours of work for £90,000 in fees, minus write-offs for free changes, meant GridSight was losing about £11,000 a year on Castellan. Plus Asha's evenings. Plus two projects they'd turned down for lack of time. Felix argued for keeping them: “Revenue is revenue.” Ruth didn't agree.

24 January to 31 March · the banishing

Ruth and Pip gave Rex the Last Warning, in person and in writing: how the team is spoken to must change, and invoices paid in 30 days, or GridSight would end the contract on 31 March. Rex laughed. On 1 February he shouted at Asha again. Ruth sent formal notice that afternoon. GridSight delivered every outstanding study, sent a full handover pack and a list of three other firms, and left on 31 March. Asha breathed out. The two turned-down projects were won in April.

— the AnnotatorYou can't choose your coworkers. You can choose your clients. And your team is watching which ones you choose.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow Rex became a client worth banishing

  1. 1990s · the builder

    Rex builds his firm from nothing, by squeezing every supplier. It works. Margins are thin; he takes them from others.

  2. 2010s · the reputation

    Castellan becomes known for paying late and shouting. Good suppliers stop bidding. Rex hires the ones who'll put up with it.

  3. 2028 · the new vendor

    GridSight is new and wants revenue. Felix, on commission, signs Castellan without asking anyone who'd worked with them.

  4. 2029 · the pattern

    Rex treats GridSight as he's treated every supplier for thirty years. Charm upwards, contempt downwards, payment late.

The turning point. Rex learning, decades ago, that suppliers would tolerate anything for revenue. Most did.

What might have saved them. One reference call before signing. Three of Castellan's former suppliers would have told GridSight everything.

— the AnnotatorClients who abuse suppliers have usually done it for years. Ask around before you sign.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Some clients cost more than they bring: in money, once you count all the hours; in stress; in the work you turn down to serve them; and in how your team feels about you for putting up with it. Ending a client relationship is hard, but sometimes it's the most important thing a leader can do.

What it wants

More for less, always, and someone to blame. It's rarely personal; it's how it treats everyone.

Why it's tolerated

Because revenue is visible and cost is hidden. Nobody counts the hours, the stress or the projects turned away.

What banishes it

  • The true cost, written down.
  • A clear, fair warning.
  • A clean, professional exit.
  • A team that sees you choose them.

Three kinds of client to consider banishing

The Unprofitable

Pleasant, but you lose money. Reprice, or part ways kindly.

The Draining

Profitable, but exhausting. Reset expectations, then decide.

The Abusive

Treats your people badly. One warning, then go.

— the AnnotatorEnding a client is not unprofessional. Ending it badly is. Give notice, finish what you owe, hand over cleanly.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • The account loses money when you count the hours.
  • Your team dreads their calls.
  • They're rude to junior staff and charming to you.
  • Every renewal comes with a discount demand and a threat.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The True Cost Ritual

    Count everything: hours, write-offs, late payments, staff time, and the projects you couldn't take.

  2. 2The Last Warning

    Tell the client plainly what has to change, with a date. Give them a real chance.

    incantation“Rex, we value the work, but how our team is spoken to has to change, and invoices need paying in 30 days. If not, we'll have to part ways in March.”

  3. 3The Clean Exit

    If it doesn't change, give proper notice, hand over everything they need, and leave politely.

    incantation“As discussed, we'll end our work on 31 March. Here's a full handover pack and a list of firms who could take it on.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Endless Endurance

    Keeping a bad client because revenue is revenue. Your best people leave first.

  • The Rage Quit

    Ending it in an angry email. Clients talk. So do their lawyers.

  • Sacrificing the Team

    Letting a client abuse your staff to keep the account. They will never forgive you, and they'll be right.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Unprofitable client.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • They're pleasant.
  • The numbers don't work.
  • They might accept a price rise.

Do

  1. Do the True Cost Ritual.
  2. Propose a new price or scope.
  3. If they decline, part ways warmly.

Don’t: The endless endurance.

If it gets worse: If they're exhausting your team, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Draining client.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Constant demands and changes.
  • Late payment.
  • Your team avoids their calls.

Do

  1. Count the true cost.
  2. Reset the rules: rhythm, change log, payment.
  3. Give a deadline for change.
  4. Take their calls yourself meanwhile.

Don’t: The rage quit.

If it gets worse: If they mistreat your people, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Abusive client.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • They shout at or belittle your staff.
  • They're charming to you.
  • Someone senior wants to keep them.

Do

  1. Believe your team, and say so.
  2. Give one clear warning, in writing.
  3. If it happens again, give notice.
  4. Exit cleanly: deliver, hand over, leave.

Don’t: Sacrificing the team.

If it gets worse: Afterwards, tell your team what you did and why.

VIIIAre you a client worth banishing?

To your suppliers, agencies or internal service teams. Tick honestly.

  • You've been sharper with a supplier's junior staff than with their boss.
  • You pay suppliers late because you can.
  • You expect changes for free.
  • Suppliers stopped bidding for your work and you didn't ask why.
  • You think suppliers should be grateful for the work.

The cure, if you ticked two: Ask a supplier you work with: “What's it like working with us?” Then listen. The good ones are choosing their clients too.

IXThe rehearsal

The Last Warning, then the Clean Exit.

Scene: Wednesday 24 January 2029, Ruth and Pip meet Rex Dunmore.

RuthRex, we value the work. But two things have to change. How our team is spoken to, and invoices paid in 30 days.

RexIs this about the girl with the spreadsheet? She needs a thicker skin.

PipHer name's Asha. She's the reason your studies are right. It's about how she's spoken to.

RexI've treated suppliers this way for thirty years.

RuthThen I'll be clear. If it happens again, we'll end our work on 31 March, with a full handover. I'll put that in writing today.

RexYou won't. Nobody does.

— the AnnotatorIt happened again on 1 February. Ruth sent notice that afternoon. Asha heard about it from Pip, in person, before anyone else.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

A client is profitable but abusive to your team. Should you keep them?

Not without change. Do the Last Warning, and mean it.

How do you know if a client is worth keeping?

The True Cost Ritual. Count the hours, the stress and the work you turned away.

How do you end it?

The Clean Exit: notice, handover, politeness. Leave the door shut, not slammed.

The Annotator's last wordYou can't choose your coworkers. You can choose your clients. Choose.
