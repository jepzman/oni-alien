The Scope-Creep Swarm

No single one is worth an argument. Together they eat the project alive.

Threat
Habitat
the end of calls, “quick” emails, demos, the words “just” and “also”
Weakness
a change log, a trade-off menu, and the word “instead”

IA sighting

August 2028 · the buzz

Even with the Rhythm Rite, the additions kept coming, now politely, in writing, one at a time. “While you're at it, could you add the school sites?” “Could you just colour the wards by deprivation?” “One small thing: could it work on an iPad?”

Friday 15 September · the count

Asha, who had been quietly keeping a list, sent it to Pip: twenty-three additions since the contract was signed. None more than two days' work. Together, thirty-one days. The project had been priced at forty.

Monday 18 September · the reset

Pip shared the Swarm Count with Gavin. Not as a complaint: as a table, with the original scope beside it. Gavin was astonished. “I had no idea it was that much. Why didn't you say?” It was a fair question. Pip hadn't, because each one had felt too small to mention.

From October · the menu

Every new request now came back with three options: in and later, instead of something, or in phase two. Gavin chose phase two eleven times out of fourteen. He'd never been offered the choice before.

— the AnnotatorScope creep isn't the client asking for too much. It's you never saying what anything costs.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the swarm was born

  1. July 2028 · the eager vendor

    GridSight is new and wants a happy first client. Pip says yes to small things to build goodwill.

  2. August · the habit

    Gavin learns that small requests are free. He's not greedy; he just has a long list and a vendor who never says “that costs”.

  3. September · the plague

    Twenty-three small yeses, thirty-one days of work, a project nine days from overrun, and nobody had decided any of it.

  4. The pattern everywhere

    Every project in history has died this way at least once. The swarm is not a client. It's the absence of a price.

The turning point. The first “sure, no problem” for something that was a problem, said to be nice.

What might have saved them. A change log from day one, and a trade-off menu offered with the very first “while you're at it”.

— the AnnotatorGoodwill isn't built by free work. It's built by delivering what you promised, on time.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Scope creep is the slow growth of a project through small, unpriced additions. Each one is reasonable. Together they break the budget, the timeline and the team. The client rarely knows it's happening, because nobody told them.

What it wants

More, because more is genuinely useful, and nobody has said it costs anything.

Why it swarms

Because saying “that'll cost something” feels awkward, and each request seems too small for the awkwardness.

What kills it

  • A visible log.
  • Choices, not refusals.
  • The original scope, cheerfully quoted.
  • Phase two.

Three kinds of swarm

The Innocent Swarm

The client doesn't realise. Show them the count and they'll stop.

The Tactical Swarm

The client knows exactly what they're doing. The Trade-Off Menu, every time.

The Internal Swarm

Your own team keeps adding “nice to haves”. Same cure.

— the Annotator“Phase two” is the most powerful phrase in delivery. It's yes, with a date.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • The plan hasn't changed but the work has doubled.
  • Requests start with “just”, “quick” or “while you're at it”.
  • Nobody can remember what was originally agreed.
  • You're delivering more and the client is less happy.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Swarm Count

    Keep a running log of every addition, however small, with an estimate. Share it monthly.

  2. 2The Trade-Off Menu

    Never just say no. Offer choices: add it and move the date, add it instead of something else, or add it later.

    incantation“Happy to add the bus depots. Option one, it's in and we deliver two weeks later. Option two, it replaces the school sites. Option three, phase two. Which works?”

  3. 3The Original Scroll

    Keep the signed scope one click away and refer back to it, cheerfully, without accusing anyone.

    incantation“Let me check the original scope… yes, that wasn't in it, so let's look at the options.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Hero Yes

    Saying yes to everything to be liked. They like you less when the deadline slips.

  • The Sudden Wall

    Saying nothing for months, then refusing everything at once. The client feels ambushed.

  • The Sneaky Shrink

    Quietly doing a worse job of the agreed work to make room. They notice, eventually.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Innocent Swarm, caught early.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • A few small requests.
  • The client is reasonable.
  • The project is still on track.

Do

  1. Start a log now.
  2. Offer the menu from the next request.
  3. Mention phase two early.

Don’t: The hero yes.

If it gets worse: If you've already absorbed lots, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The swarm has already eaten a third of the budget.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Dozens of small changes.
  • Nobody logged them.
  • The deadline is at risk.

Do

  1. Reconstruct the Swarm Count.
  2. Share it as a table, not a complaint.
  3. Reset: what's in, what's phase two.
  4. Tell your boss before the client.

Don’t: The sudden wall.

If it gets worse: If the client refuses to reset, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Tactical Swarm, refusing to pay.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • They know it's out of scope.
  • They insist it's “implied”.
  • They threaten to escalate.

Do

  1. Go back to the signed scope, line by line.
  2. Involve commercial and your boss.
  3. Offer a priced change request.
  4. Read the Escalation Summoning.

Don’t: The sneaky shrink.

If it gets worse: If it never stops, see Banishing a Client.

VIIIAre you the Swarm?

Whether you're a client or a colleague. Tick honestly.

  • You've started a request with “could you just”.
  • You've added something to a project without asking what it would delay.
  • You can't remember what was in the original brief.
  • You think small favours are free.
  • You've been surprised that a project took longer than planned, after you added to it.

The cure, if you ticked two: Next time you ask for something extra, add: “What would that move?” It's the most respectful sentence a client can say.

IXThe rehearsal

The Swarm Count and the Trade-Off Menu.

Scene: Monday 18 September 2028, the reset call.

PipGavin, I owe you an apology. I've said yes to twenty-three small things and never told you what they added up to.

GavinTwenty-three? Really?

PipThirty-one days, on a forty-day project. It's in the table I sent. That's my fault for not saying.

GavinWell, I'd rather know. What do we do?

PipFrom now on, every addition comes with three options: in and later, instead of something, or phase two. You choose.

GavinPhase two for the iPad thing, then. And the school sites. And… let me look at the rest.

— the AnnotatorPhase two was signed in November for £60,000. It contained nineteen of the twenty-three “small things”.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

The client asks you to “just” add one more dataset. What do you do?

Log it, estimate it, and offer the Trade-Off Menu.

You've already absorbed twenty small changes. Is it too late?

No. Share the Swarm Count honestly and reset with them. Better now than at the deadline.

Is scope creep always the client's fault?

No. Often it's yours, for never saying what anything costs.

The Annotator's last wordEach “just” costs something. Say what, every time, and let them choose.
