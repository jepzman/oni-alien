The Bake-Off Arena

Three vendors, one prize, and the eternal temptation to promise the impossible.

Threat
Habitat
shortlist presentations, tender responses, “best and final offers”
Weakness
understanding the buyer's fear, an honest plan, and proof

IA sighting

October 2028 · the tender

Northshire Combined Authority went to tender for a regional demand forecast: eight councils, one model, a budget of £240,000. Fourteen vendors applied. Three were shortlisted: GridSight, Corvid Analytics, and Vexmoor Consulting, who had never built a forecast but had very good slides.

Thursday 9 November · room 3

Corvid went first. Zara Okafor, their founder, was excellent: sharp, funny, deeply technical. She promised the full model in six weeks. The panel visibly relaxed. Vexmoor went second and said “transformational” nine times.

Thursday 9 November · 15:00 · GridSight

Felix had begged Pip to promise eight weeks. Pip had refused, and they'd argued about it in the car park. Pip's slide said sixteen weeks, and below it: “Week 4: first council live. Week 8: four councils. Week 16: all eight.” Then Asha showed Wexley's map, live, on her laptop. Then Pip asked the panel what would make this project a disaster for them.

Thursday 30 November · the result

The chair, it turned out, had been burned by a six-week promise in 2025. GridSight won. Bernard Quill, from procurement, said privately: “Your sixteen weeks was the only number in the room anyone believed.”

— the AnnotatorPanels aren't buying speed. They're buying the absence of embarrassment. Show them you won't embarrass them.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow bake-offs came to reward lies

  1. The 1990s · the price war

    Public tenders are judged mostly on price. Vendors learn to bid low and charge for changes later.

  2. The 2010s · the speed war

    Buyers want quick results. Vendors learn to promise fast and explain the delays later.

  3. 2025 · Northshire's scar

    The authority's last forecasting vendor promised six weeks and took eleven months. The chair still talks about it at dinner.

  4. 2028 · the arena

    Three vendors, one prize. The one with the most impressive number usually wins. Unless the panel has been burned.

The turning point. Buyers rewarding confidence over credibility, until they've been burned once.

What might have saved them. Every vendor asking, before the pitch: “What went wrong last time?”

— the AnnotatorEvery panel has a scar. Find it, and pitch to the scar, not the scoring sheet.

IIIKnow thy enemy

A vendor bake-off is a competition where several suppliers pitch for the same work. The temptation is to win by promising more, faster, cheaper. It works often enough to be dangerous. The vendors who last are the ones who win on credibility, and lose gracefully when credibility isn't enough.

What the panel wants

To choose someone they won't regret. Their fear of a bad choice is bigger than their wish for a perfect one.

Why rivals overpromise

Because it often wins, and the consequences arrive after the contract, to someone else (usually delivery).

What wins honestly

  • Understanding the buyer's fear.
  • A plan with early proof points.
  • A live demo.
  • A reference who'll take a call.

Three kinds of arena

The Fair Fight

Clear criteria, honest panel. Credibility wins.

The Price War

Cheapest wins. Decide whether you want to be cheapest.

The Beauty Contest

Slides win. Bring proof anyway; some panel member will notice.

— the AnnotatorIf you lose to an impossible promise, stay in touch. The buyer will need someone in about five months.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • The client sends the same questions to three vendors.
  • A rival promises something you know can't be done.
  • Your sales team wants to match it.
  • The panel has more people than the project.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Fear Reading

    Find out what the buyer is most afraid of (failure, cost, embarrassment) and answer that first.

    incantation“What would make this project a disaster for you? Let's start there.”

  2. 2The Honest Plan

    Show a credible plan with its risks, and say what you'd need from them. Credibility beats speed.

    incantation“We could promise six weeks. We'd miss it. Here's a sixteen-week plan we'll actually hit, and how we'd show progress in week four.”

  3. 3The Proof Rite

    Bring evidence: a working demo, a reference client, a pilot. Show; don't just tell.

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Impossible Promise

    Matching the rival's fantasy timeline. You'll win the contract and lose the client.

  • Rubbishing the Rival

    Criticising the other vendors. It makes the panel wonder what you'd say about them.

  • The Generic Deck

    Pitching the same slides you used for the last client. Panels can tell.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Fair Fight.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Clear scoring criteria.
  • The panel asks good questions.
  • Rivals are credible.

Do

  1. Find the buyer's fear.
  2. Show an honest plan.
  3. Bring proof.

Don’t: The generic deck.

If it gets worse: If a rival promises the impossible, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The rival promises the impossible.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Their timeline is half yours.
  • The panel liked it.
  • Your sales team is panicking.

Do

  1. Don't match it.
  2. Show early proof points instead.
  3. Ask the panel what went wrong last time.
  4. Name the risk of speed, without naming the rival.

Don’t: The impossible promise.

If it gets worse: If your own side forces the promise, you're at Hell.

Hell

Your own sales team wants to promise it.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your sales director has already hinted at the number.
  • Your boss wants the win.
  • You'll be the one delivering.

Do

  1. Put the real plan in writing.
  2. Tell your boss what the risk is.
  3. Offer a faster first phase instead.
  4. Refuse to sign what you can't deliver.

Don’t: Rubbishing the rival.

If it gets worse: If you're overruled, read the Overpromise Golem now.

VIIIAre you the overpromiser in the arena?

Whether you pitch to clients or to your own leadership. Tick honestly.

  • You've given a date in a pitch you weren't sure you could hit.
  • You've criticised a competitor to a buyer.
  • You've used the same deck for three different clients.
  • You've never asked a buyer what went wrong last time.
  • You think losing a pitch is always a failure.

The cure, if you ticked two: In your next pitch, include one honest risk and how you'll handle it. It's the most believable slide you'll ever show.

IXThe rehearsal

The Fear Reading and the Honest Plan.

Scene: Thursday 9 November 2028, room 3, GridSight's pitch.

Panel chairCorvid have offered six weeks. You're saying sixteen. Why should we wait ten weeks longer?

PipMay I ask a question first? What would make this project a disaster for you?

Panel chair…Promises we can't report to eight councils. We had that in 2025.

PipThen here's what you'd report: one council live in week four, four by week eight, all eight by sixteen. Each one real.

AshaAnd this is Wexley's version. It's live. You can click it.

Panel chairCan I speak to Wexley?

PipGavin's expecting your call.

— the AnnotatorGavin, once the Client Demon, gave GridSight a glowing reference. He mentioned the late-night calls, and how Pip had stopped them.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

A rival promises half your timeline. What do you do?

The Honest Plan: show why yours is credible and how they'll see progress early.

Your sales director wants to match the rival's promise. What do you say?

That you'll help win it honestly, and that you won't sign a plan you can't deliver.

You lost the bake-off. What now?

Ask for feedback, thank them, and stay in touch. Losing well wins the next one.

The Annotator's last wordWin with the plan you can deliver. The other kind of win is just a delayed loss.
