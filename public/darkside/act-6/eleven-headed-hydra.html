The Eleven-Headed Hydra

Eleven stakeholders, eleven opinions, and nobody who can say yes. Cut one head and two grow back.

Threat
Habitat
steering groups, “wider stakeholders”, emails with fourteen people copied
Weakness
a single named owner, a decision log, and a map of who decides what

IA sighting

Tuesday 26 September 2028 · the steering group

Phase two's first steering group had eleven people in it. Planning wanted ward boundaries. Highways wanted road segments. Housing wanted estates. Finance said nothing and took notes. Two councillors wanted their own wards on the first page. Five others had opinions on the colour scheme.

The next week

The housing layer, agreed in the meeting, was reopened by email by highways, who hadn't been there. Then by a councillor, who had. Gavin, in the middle, said “I'll need to take it to…” fourteen times in five days. Pip counted.

Friday 29 September · the Body Finder

Pip asked Gavin, kindly, who decided when the heads disagreed. Gavin didn't know. Neither did anyone else. Pip asked whether they could find out, explaining that without one person, the project couldn't finish, and Gavin would be blamed.

Tuesday 3 October · the body

Gavin took it to Nadia Sesay, Director of Place. She'd assumed Gavin was deciding. She named herself the owner, with Gavin running day to day. Decision 14, the housing layer, was approved that afternoon and stayed approved.

— the AnnotatorA hydra has many heads but one body. Most clients don't know who their body is until you ask.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow Wexley grew eleven heads

  1. 2018 · the consultation duty

    After a planning row, Wexley adopts a rule: every project involving land consults every affected department. Sensible.

  2. 2021 · the steering habit

    Consultation becomes steering groups, and steering groups become places where anyone can raise anything. Nobody is given the final say.

  3. 2024 · the safety of many

    If eleven people decide, nobody is blamed. The hydra is comfortable for everyone inside it.

  4. 2028 · the vendor's problem

    GridSight arrives, needing decisions. The hydra gives it eleven opinions and no answer.

The turning point. A good rule (consult everyone) that quietly became a bad one (everyone decides).

What might have saved them. One sentence in the original project charter: “Final decisions: Director of Place.”

— the AnnotatorConsultation is asking everyone. Decision is one person choosing. Hydras grow when the two get mixed up.

IIIKnow thy enemy

The hydra is a client with many stakeholders and no clear decision maker. It isn't malicious. It's an organisation where consulting has become deciding, so every head can veto and none can approve. Vendors get stuck in the middle, building versions for each head.

What it wants

To be heard. Each head wants its concern taken seriously. Most will accept a decision they disagree with, if someone with authority made it.

Why it regrows heads

Because decisions aren't written down, so anyone who missed a meeting can reopen anything.

What tames it

  • One named decision maker.
  • A decision log, shared after every meeting.
  • A stakeholder map.
  • A clear process for reopening.

Three kinds of hydra

The Committee

A formal group, no chair with authority. Ask for one.

The Headless Body

The decider exists but doesn't know it. Help your contact find them.

The Warring Heads

Two departments using your project to fight each other. Escalate to their shared boss.

— the AnnotatorThe quietest head is often finance. Talk to them early, before they surprise you at the end.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Every meeting adds a new stakeholder.
  • Decisions are made, then unmade by someone who wasn't there.
  • Your contact says “I'll need to take it to…” about everything.
  • Feedback contradicts itself, and nobody seems to mind.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Head Count

    Map every stakeholder: what they care about, what they can block, and who they listen to.

  2. 2The Body Finder

    Ask, early and plainly, who makes the final decision, and get it in writing.

    incantation“When the heads disagree, who decides? Can we name one person, so you aren't stuck in the middle?”

  3. 3The Decision Log

    Write down every decision, who made it and when. Share it after every meeting.

    incantation“Decision 14: Nadia approved the housing layer, 3 October. If anyone wants to revisit it, let's take it to her.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • Pleasing Every Head

    Trying to satisfy all eleven. You'll build eleven half-products and please nobody.

  • The Back Channel

    Letting each head brief you privately and promising each something different.

  • Ignoring the Quiet Head

    Forgetting the person who never comes to meetings. It's usually finance.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Committee with a likely chair.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Lots of stakeholders.
  • Someone senior seems to lead.
  • Decisions do get made, slowly.

Do

  1. Do the Head Count.
  2. Confirm the decider in writing.
  3. Start the Decision Log.

Don’t: Pleasing every head.

If it gets worse: If nobody can decide, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Headless Body.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Nobody can say yes.
  • Decisions are reopened weekly.
  • Your contact is stuck in the middle.

Do

  1. Do the Body Finder with your contact.
  2. Explain what it's costing them.
  3. Offer to help them write the case to their boss.
  4. Log everything.

Don’t: The back channel.

If it gets worse: If two heads are at war, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Warring Heads.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Two departments disagree on principle.
  • Each briefs you against the other.
  • Your project is their battlefield.

Do

  1. Refuse private promises.
  2. Put both options to the shared boss, in one document.
  3. Recommend one, with reasons.
  4. Pause work on the disputed part until it's decided.

Don’t: Choosing a side in secret.

If it gets worse: If nobody will decide at all, pause the project and say why.

VIIIAre you a head of the Hydra?

Think about the projects you're a stakeholder in. Tick honestly.

  • You've reopened a decision made in a meeting you didn't attend.
  • You don't know who has the final say on a project you comment on.
  • You've briefed a vendor privately against a colleague.
  • You've said “I'll need to check with…” without knowing who.
  • You attend a steering group and have never asked what it decides.

The cure, if you ticked two: Before your next comment on a shared project, ask: “Who decides this?” If nobody knows, help find out.

IXThe rehearsal

The Body Finder, kindly.

Scene: Friday 29 September 2028, Pip and Gavin, after the steering group.

PipGavin, can I ask something that might sound odd? When highways and housing disagree, who decides?

Gavin…The steering group?

PipThe steering group can't seem to. And I think you're getting the blame for that.

GavinI am. Every week.

PipCould we ask Nadia to name one person? Even her. I'll write a half-page on what it's costing, if that helps.

GavinPlease do. I've wanted to say this for a year.

— the AnnotatorNadia read the half-page on Monday, named herself the owner on Tuesday, and said: “Why did nobody ask me this in 2024?”

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Two stakeholders want opposite things. What do you do?

Take it to the named decision maker, with both options and your recommendation.

There is no named decision maker. What now?

Ask your contact to help you find one, and explain that without one the project can't finish.

A decision made last month gets reopened. How do you respond?

Point to the Decision Log, calmly, and ask the decider whether they want to change it.

The Annotator's last wordA hydra has many heads but one body. Find the body.
