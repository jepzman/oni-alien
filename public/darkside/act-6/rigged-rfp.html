The Rigged RFP

An open competition, written so that only one vendor can possibly win it.

Threat
Habitat
public tenders, requirement lists, very short deadlines, oddly specific appendices
Weakness
clarifying questions, an honest bid-or-no-bid decision, and knowing when to walk away

IA sighting

Friday 15 December 2028 · the tender

Ardmore County Council published a tender for a grid capacity study, value £180,000, responses due 3 January. Felix forwarded it at 17:42 with three exclamation marks. Requirement 4.7, page 31: “The supplier must demonstrate at least five years' experience of the Corvid platform.”

Monday 18 December · the Bid / No-Bid Rite

Corvid's platform was five years old. Only Corvid had five years' experience of it. Pip and Asha scored the tender: could we win (2/10), could we deliver (9/10), do we want it (7/10). Writing the bid would take three people most of the holidays.

Tuesday 19 December · the clarifying question

Pip asked, formally, whether equivalent experience with a comparable platform would be accepted. The answer came on the 22nd: “No.” Felix wanted to bid anyway (“they might not be strict”). Pip and Ruth said no.

Wednesday 20 December, then the spring · the graceful decline

Pip wrote a two-line note to Ardmore's procurement team declining, with the reason, and asking to talk about future work. GridSight's team had a Christmas. In April, Ardmore's head of planning rang: they'd like GridSight to quote for a different study. “You were the only firm that explained why you didn't bid.”

— the AnnotatorA no-bid isn't a loss. It's three weeks of your team's life, spent on something you could actually win.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow a tender came to be written for Corvid

  1. 2023 · the pilot

    Ardmore runs a small pilot with Corvid. It goes well. Ardmore's analysts learn Corvid's platform and like it.

  2. 2028 · the rules

    Public procurement rules require an open tender for anything over the threshold. Ardmore must advertise.

  3. December 2028 · the copying

    The analyst writing the requirements copies them from Corvid's pilot documentation. Not out of corruption; out of familiarity, and a deadline.

  4. The result

    An open tender only one vendor can win. Nobody at Ardmore thinks of it as rigged. They think of it as specific.

The turning point. A buyer who already knew what they wanted being forced to pretend they didn't.

What might have saved them. A pre-market conversation, so other vendors could ask about requirement 4.7 before it was published.

— the AnnotatorMost rigged tenders aren't corrupt. They're written by people who already have a favourite and a deadline.

IIIKnow thy enemy

A rigged RFP (request for proposal) is a tender whose requirements favour one vendor so strongly that nobody else can realistically win. Sometimes it's deliberate. More often the buyer already has a preferred supplier and has to run an open process anyway. Either way, bidding costs you weeks.

What it wants

Usually, to look open while choosing someone already chosen. Sometimes, a cheaper quote to push the favourite's price down.

Why it exists

Because procurement rules require competition, and people's preferences don't disappear because a rule says they should.

How to spot it

  • Requirements naming a product.
  • Absurdly short deadlines.
  • No pre-market engagement.
  • Oddly precise experience criteria.

Three kinds of rigged scroll

The Familiar Scroll

Written around what they know. A clarifying question may open it up.

The Price-Check Scroll

They want your quote to negotiate with their favourite. Don't bid hard.

The Truly Rigged Scroll

Deliberately unfair. Decline, or challenge formally if it really matters.

— the AnnotatorScore every tender before you write a word. The best bid teams say no more often than yes.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • The requirements read like one vendor's brochure.
  • The deadline is two weeks, over a holiday.
  • Nobody from the buyer would talk to you before it was published.
  • There's a requirement only one company in the world could meet.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Bid / No-Bid Rite

    Before writing a word, score the tender: can we win, can we deliver, do we want it? Below the line, walk away.

  2. 2The Clarifying Question

    Ask, politely and formally, about the oddly specific requirements. Sometimes they relax them.

    incantation“Requirement 4.7 names a specific platform. Would equivalent experience with a comparable platform be accepted?”

  3. 3The Graceful Decline

    If you don't bid, say so kindly, with a reason. Buyers remember who explained.

    incantation“We've decided not to bid this time, as the requirements fit a specific platform. We'd love to talk about future work.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • Bidding Everything

    Answering every tender to be seen. You'll spend weeks writing bids you were never going to win.

  • The Furious Complaint

    Publicly accusing the buyer of rigging it, without evidence. Nobody will hire you after.

  • The Fake Fit

    Claiming experience you don't have to meet the requirement. It's found out, and sometimes illegal.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Familiar Scroll.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • One requirement looks odd.
  • The rest is open.
  • The buyer answers questions.

Do

  1. Ask the clarifying question.
  2. Score the tender.
  3. Bid only if the answer opens it up.

Don’t: Bidding everything.

If it gets worse: If the answer is “no”, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The scroll that won't open.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • The requirement stays.
  • Your sales team wants to bid anyway.
  • It would take weeks.

Do

  1. Do the Bid / No-Bid Rite with your boss.
  2. Decline gracefully, with a reason.
  3. Ask about future work.
  4. Spend the time on a tender you can win.

Don’t: The fake fit.

If it gets worse: If it's genuinely unfair to all bidders, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Truly Rigged Scroll.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • It's public money.
  • The unfairness is clear and serious.
  • Other vendors have noticed too.

Do

  1. Take advice before acting.
  2. Raise it through the formal procurement challenge process.
  3. Stick to facts.
  4. Accept that it may cost you goodwill.

Don’t: The furious complaint.

If it gets worse: Choose your battles. Most aren't worth it.

VIIIHave you written a rigged scroll?

If you've ever written a job advert or a supplier brief. Tick honestly.

  • You've written requirements around someone you'd already chosen.
  • You've run a process you knew the outcome of.
  • You've asked for quotes just to push down a favourite's price.
  • You've set a deadline nobody new could meet.
  • You've copied requirements from a supplier's own documents.

The cure, if you ticked two: If you've already chosen, say so and use the right process for that. Don't make strangers write bids for a prize that's gone.

IXThe rehearsal

The Bid / No-Bid Rite.

Scene: Monday 18 December 2028, Pip, Asha and Felix.

Felix£180,000! We have to bid.

PipRequirement 4.7. Five years on Corvid's platform. Only Corvid has that.

FelixThey might not be strict.

AshaCan we win: two out of ten. Deliver: nine. Want it: seven. And it's three of us over Christmas.

PipLet's ask the clarifying question. If they relax it, we bid. If not, we decline, nicely.

Felix…Fine. But I'm writing the nice decline.

PipDeal. Keep it to two lines.

— the AnnotatorFelix's decline was three lines. It was still the one Ardmore remembered.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

A tender fits a rival perfectly. Should you bid?

Ask a clarifying question first. If the answer doesn't change, probably not.

Is a tender written for someone else always rigged?

Not always. Sometimes buyers just copy what they know. The clarifying question tells you which.

You think a public tender is genuinely unfair. What can you do?

Raise it formally through the procurement process. Calmly, with facts, and only if it really matters.

The Annotator's last wordThe best bid you'll ever write is the one you decided not to.
