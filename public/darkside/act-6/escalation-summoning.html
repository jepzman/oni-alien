The Escalation Summoning

The client, unhappy, draws a circle of CCs and summons your CEO.

Threat
Habitat
angry emails, “I've copied your CEO”, LinkedIn messages to executives
Weakness
speed, facts, no defensiveness, and a boss who's already been told

IA sighting

Monday 16 October 2028 · 07:04

Gavin's email arrived in Dame Helena's inbox, copying Ruth, Pip, Felix, Nadia and two councillors. Subject: “Unacceptable.” The housing layer, approved on 3 October, had slipped nine days, because highways had reopened it before Nadia was named owner. Gavin was presenting it to councillors that Thursday.

07:12 · Ruth and Pip

Ruth read it on the train. She wasn't surprised. Pip had told her, the previous Wednesday, that Gavin was unhappy about the slip and might escalate. She'd already told Dame Helena's office there was a delay, why, and what they were doing. The No-Surprise Rule had been followed. The summoning arrived; the CEO had been pre-warned.

07:30 to 10:45 · the Calm Reply

Pip drafted. Ruth cut it from 600 words to 140. No defence, no blame of highways. Acknowledge, facts, plan, owner: “You're right that we missed the date. Here's what happened. You'll have the housing layer on Wednesday, before your Thursday meeting. Pip will call you at noon.” Sent at 10:45.

11:02 · the reply from the summoned

Dame Helena replied all: “Thank you, Gavin, for raising this. Ruth has it in hand.” Seven words, to everyone. Gavin rang Pip at noon, calmer. On Wednesday he got the layer. In November they agreed an escalation path: Pip, then Ruth, then, if all else failed, Dame Helena. He never needed it again.

— the AnnotatorAn escalation is a client shouting that they weren't heard. Answer the shout, then fix the hearing.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow Gavin learned to summon CEOs

  1. 2019 · the consultants again

    Wexley's failed climate-plan vendor ignored Gavin for months. He emailed their CEO. The next day, everything moved.

  2. The lesson

    Gavin learns that CEOs make things happen. He doesn't learn that it also makes enemies.

  3. 2028 · the slip

    The housing layer slips, through nobody's fault in particular. Gavin has a councillors' meeting on Thursday and a director watching.

  4. 16 October · the summoning

    He reaches for the tool that worked in 2019. Copying the CEO is the fastest way he knows to feel heard.

The turning point. An escalation that worked once, against a vendor that truly deserved it, becoming a habit.

What might have saved them. An agreed escalation path at the start of the contract, so Gavin knew who to go to before the CEO.

— the AnnotatorClients escalate when they don't trust the normal route. Give them a route they trust.

IIIKnow thy enemy

An escalation over your head is when a client (or colleague) goes to your boss, or your boss's boss, instead of you. It's alarming, but it's usually a sign that they felt unheard, or that they'd learned from someone else that it works. How you respond in the first few hours matters far more than the original problem.

What it wants

Action, fast. And proof that someone powerful has noticed.

Why it summons

Because the normal route felt slow or deaf, or because it worked last time with another vendor.

What dispels it

  • A boss who already knew.
  • A same-day, short, calm reply.
  • A named owner and a date.
  • An escalation path for next time.

Three kinds of summoning

The Frightened Summoning

A worried client under pressure. Calm, fast, fixed.

The Habitual Summoning

They do it to everyone. Agree a path, and hold it.

The Political Summoning

Someone using the escalation to make a point internally. Facts, and your boss beside you.

— the AnnotatorThe best response to an escalation is short. The CEO wants to know it's handled, not why it happened.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • The client's tone has been getting colder for weeks.
  • They've asked “who's your boss?”.
  • A problem has been unresolved for longer than they'd tolerate.
  • Your boss doesn't know there's a problem.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The No-Surprise Rule

    Tell your boss about any client unhappiness early. Escalations hurt most when your boss hears first from the CEO.

    incantation“Ruth, heads up: Gavin's unhappy about the delay. Here's why and what we're doing. He may escalate.”

  2. 2The Calm Reply

    Respond the same day: acknowledge, facts, plan, owner. No defending, no blaming.

    incantation“Gavin, thank you for raising this. You're right that we missed the date. Here's what happened, what we're doing, and when you'll have it.”

  3. 3The Escalation Path

    Agree with clients upfront how to escalate, so they don't need to summon the CEO.

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Defensive Essay

    Replying with a long justification. The CEO reads it as an excuse.

  • The Counter-Summoning

    Escalating back to the client's boss in revenge. Now it's a war.

  • The Hidden Problem

    Never telling your boss there was a problem. The summoning is far worse when it's a surprise.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Frightened Summoning, pre-warned.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your boss knew already.
  • The problem is real but fixable.
  • The client is anxious, not hostile.

Do

  1. Reply the same day.
  2. Acknowledge, facts, plan, owner.
  3. Call them, don't just email.

Don’t: The defensive essay.

If it gets worse: If your boss heard it first from the CEO, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The surprise summoning.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your boss didn't know.
  • The CEO asked your boss what's going on.
  • You're now explaining twice.

Do

  1. Tell your boss everything, immediately.
  2. Draft the Calm Reply together.
  3. Apologise to your boss for the surprise.
  4. Start the No-Surprise Rule today.

Don’t: The hidden problem.

If it gets worse: If the client is doing it to make a point, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Political Summoning.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • The escalation is part of their internal fight.
  • The facts don't really matter to them.
  • Your CEO may know their CEO.

Do

  1. Stick strictly to facts.
  2. Keep your boss in front.
  3. Never counter-escalate.
  4. Agree a path in writing afterwards.

Don’t: The counter-summoning.

If it gets worse: If it repeats, read Banishing a Client.

VIIIAre you a Summoner?

Have you gone over someone's head? Tick honestly.

  • You've copied someone's boss to make them move faster.
  • You've escalated before talking to the person directly.
  • You've written “unacceptable” in a subject line.
  • You don't know the normal route for escalating with a supplier.
  • You felt good after the escalation, and didn't check how they felt.

The cure, if you ticked two: Before your next escalation, talk to the person first: “I'm worried about X; if it doesn't change by Friday, I'll need to involve your manager.” Then they get a chance.

IXThe rehearsal

The Calm Reply, cut to the bone.

Scene: Monday 16 October 2028, 07:20, Ruth and Pip on the phone.

PipI've drafted a reply. It explains highways, the reopened decision, the steering group…

RuthHow long is it?

PipSix hundred words.

RuthMake it a hundred and forty. He's right we missed the date. Say that first. Then when he'll have it, and when you'll call.

PipAnd highways?

RuthNot today. Today he needs to feel heard. We fix the hydra next week.

— the AnnotatorDame Helena's seven-word reply was, Ruth said, the best feedback GridSight got all year.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

A client emails your CEO. What do you do first?

Tell your boss within minutes, then draft the Calm Reply together.

Was the client wrong to escalate?

Sometimes. But usually it means they felt unheard. Find out why.

How do you stop it happening again?

The No-Surprise Rule and an agreed Escalation Path.
