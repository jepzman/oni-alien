The Weather Tyrant

Sunshine on Monday. Thunder by Tuesday. No forecast.

Threat
Habitat
Monday mornings, budget weeks, the minutes after a call with his boss
Weakness
patterns, written down, and a calm that doesn't move with the weather

IA sighting

Monday 13 January · ☀️

Seconded to Network Assets for the meter fix, Pip met good-day Gareth. Gareth bought pastries, told funny stories about the 2009 blackout, and approved the meter-fix plan in four minutes.

Tuesday 14 January · ⛈️

Bad-day Gareth slammed his laptop shut, called the same plan “unacceptable”, and made a junior engineer cry over a font. Nothing had changed except that Gareth had had a call with the executive committee at nine.

Every morning · the forecast

The team had a private chat called ☀️ / ⛈️. Someone posted the forecast each morning after seeing Gareth in the lift. Nobody brought bad news on a ⛈️ day. Bad news therefore arrived in large, late piles on ☀️ days, which made them ⛈️ days.

Monday 20 January · the line

In budget week, Gareth shouted at Sam from Field Ops in front of the whole team. Sam left the room. Pip wrote the date down. That was no longer weather.

— the AnnotatorYou can work for a moody manager for a while. You can't work for a cruel one. Learn the difference early: moods change; cruelty repeats.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow a decent engineer became the Weather Tyrant

  1. 2009 · the blackout

    Gareth is a young engineer on the night of the blackout. He sees his director scream at people for hours. Everyone remembers that night as the night the director “took control”.

  2. 2015 to 2021 · climbing

    Gareth rises through Network Assets. He's never trained to manage people. He manages the way he was managed: calm when things are fine, loud when they aren't.

  3. 2022 · the Tuesday calls

    Gareth joins the weekly executive committee call. His own boss criticises him on it, often in front of peers. He leaves every Tuesday call humiliated and furious.

  4. 2026 · the forecast

    The pressure goes straight into his team. Tuesdays are storms. Budget weeks are hurricanes. Gareth doesn't see the pattern. His team can predict it to the hour.

The turning point. A blackout night when shouting looked like leadership, and the Tuesday calls that taught him to pass pressure downwards.

What might have saved them. Management training, any time in fifteen years. And a boss who didn't humiliate him on Tuesdays.

— the AnnotatorMoody managers usually pass on weather from above. That explains the storms. It doesn't excuse shouting at Sam.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Moody managers are usually stressed and poorly regulated: they bring pressure from above straight into the room. Their moods have patterns. You're not responsible for their weather, but you can forecast it, time your asks and keep your own behaviour steady. And there is a line: shouting, humiliation or fear is not weather. It's conduct.

What it wants

Relief from pressure it can't control. Bad moods are how it discharges that pressure.

Why it thrives here

Because MetroVolt promotes engineers into management without training them, and because the culture at the top is harsh. Pressure flows downhill, and nobody stops it at Gareth's door.

What it fears

  • Its own boss.
  • Bad news on a bad day.
  • Being shown its pattern.
  • Formal complaints.

The three breeds

The Pressure Cooker

Stressed from above. Better with early news and calm.

The Mood-Driven

Swings with no clear trigger. Needs written approvals.

The Bully

Shouts, humiliates, frightens. Not weather: conduct. Use the formal channel.

— the AnnotatorYou can't control the weather. You can control whether you bring an umbrella, and a written record.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • The team checks “what mood is he in” before asking anything.
  • Approved work gets rejected on a bad day.
  • Bad news is held back and arrives late.
  • You feel relief when they're out of the office.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Barometer

    Notice the pattern (days, times, after which meetings). Time important asks for good windows.

  2. 2The Steady Hand

    Stay calm and consistent whatever the weather. Don't absorb the mood or reflect it back.

    incantation“I can see today's tough. Shall I come back with this at four, or do you want the two-minute version now?”

  3. 3The Written Approval

    Get approvals in writing on good days, so bad days can't quietly undo them.

    incantation“Great, so we're agreed on the layout. I'll note it in the project log.”

  4. 4The Line in the Sand

    If moods turn into shouting, humiliation or fear, name it calmly, privately, and take it to HR if it repeats.

    incantation“I want to do good work for you, but I'm not OK being shouted at. Can we talk about what went wrong?”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • Storm Chasing

    Bringing everything on good days only. Urgent bad news on bad days is still urgent; delay makes it worse.

  • Becoming the Weather

    Matching his mood: snapping back, sulking. Now there are two storm systems.

  • The Forecast Channel

    The ☀️ / ⛈️ chat is funny. Until it's screenshotted. Then it's a disciplinary.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Pressure Cooker. Occasional bad days under pressure.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Storms follow obvious triggers.
  • They apologise afterwards.
  • Good days are genuinely good.

Do

  1. Notice the pattern; time big asks for good windows.
  2. Stay steady whatever the weather.
  3. Get approvals in writing on good days.

Don’t: Storm chasing: holding urgent bad news for a good day.

If it gets worse: If swings affect decisions often, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Mood-Driven. Frequent swings that change decisions.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Approved work gets rejected on bad days.
  • The team runs a mood forecast.
  • Bad news arrives late, in piles.

Do

  1. Written approvals, always.
  2. Deliver bad news early and short, with a plan.
  3. On a good day, tell them calmly about the impact of the swings.
  4. Keep a private Weather Log.

Don’t: Becoming the weather: snapping back or sulking.

If it gets worse: If moods turn into shouting or humiliation, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Bully. Shouting, humiliation, tears.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • People have been shouted at in public.
  • Colleagues are scared.
  • It repeats.

Do

  1. Support the person targeted, afterwards.
  2. Keep a dated, factual record.
  3. Raise it with HR or the skip-level, or help the target do so.
  4. If it's you: “I'm not OK being shouted at. Can we talk about what went wrong?”

Don’t: The forecast channel. Funny until it's screenshotted, then it's a disciplinary, for you.

If it gets worse: If HR won't act, protect yourself and plan your exit. You don't have to wait for it to happen to you.

VIIIAre you the weather?

Pressure travels. Check whether it travels through you. Tick honestly.

  • Your team checks your mood before asking you things.
  • You've rejected something you'd approved, after a bad meeting.
  • You've raised your voice at someone at work this year.
  • People bring you bad news late.
  • You feel better after letting off steam at someone junior.

The cure, if you ticked two: After a bad meeting, wait 20 minutes before talking to your team. And when you snap, apologise the same day, specifically. The forecast improves fast.

IXThe rehearsal

The Steady Hand and the Written Approval: calm, short, and with a record.

Scene: Tuesday 14 January, 10:05. A ⛈️ day. Pip has urgent news.

GarethWhat now?

PipI can see today's tough. Two-minute version: the meter vendor's delivery slips a week. Plan B is attached, and it keeps us on track.

GarethAnd the plan you showed me yesterday? It's unacceptable.

PipYesterday we agreed that plan; I noted it in the log. Has something changed I should know about?

Gareth…ExCo wants it faster.

PipUnderstood. Shall I bring you two options for faster, at four, when you've had a breather?

Gareth…Four. Fine.

— the AnnotatorPip didn't absorb the storm or reflect it. He stayed level, pointed calmly to the record, found the real trigger (ExCo) and picked a better time.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your manager approved a plan yesterday and rejects it today, angrily. What do you do?

Stay calm, refer to yesterday's written approval, and ask what's changed: “Yesterday we agreed X. Has something changed I should know about?”

You have urgent bad news and it's a ⛈️ day. Wait?

No. Deliver it short and calm, with a plan. Delayed bad news is worse news.

Your manager shouts at a colleague in front of the team. What now?

Support the colleague afterwards, note it with date and facts, and encourage them (or yourself) to raise it. That's conduct, not weather.

The Annotator's last wordYou can't control the weather. You can control whether you bring an umbrella, and a written record.
