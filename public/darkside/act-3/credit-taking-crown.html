The Credit-Taking Crown

When the Credit Vampire is your boss.

Threat
Habitat
leadership updates, the board pack, Victor's LinkedIn
Weakness
relationships above and around them that know your name

IA sighting

Tuesday 7 January · the cold snap

A cold snap hit the city. Pip's Q1 load model had predicted the peak to within 1.5%. Network Ops bought power at the right time and saved an estimated £600,000.

Thursday 16 January · the board

Victor's update to the board said: “My team delivered a step-change in forecast accuracy, using an approach I introduced.” The approach was Pip's. It was in a document with Pip's name on it, dated November, before Victor joined.

Monday 27 January · LinkedIn

Victor posted about “the power of data-driven leadership”, with a photo of the forecast chart. It had 412 likes. Pip's mum liked it, not knowing.

February · the review

Pip's mid-year review, written by Victor, said: “Solid contributor to team successes.”

— the AnnotatorHelp your boss get credit for having you. It's the only credit war you can win.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow a consultant learned to wear the crown

  1. 2014 · the partner presents

    At Victor's consultancy, analysts build, managers polish, and the partner presents. The client sees one face. That face gets the credit and the bonus.

  2. 2017 · the training

    Promoted to manager, Victor is trained to “own the narrative”: present the team's work as one coherent story, in his voice. Naming every analyst is considered “messy”.

  3. 2021 · the decimal

    After the decimal disaster (see the All-Seeing Eye), Victor also learns that whoever presents is whoever is blamed. If he's taking the risk, he reasons, he takes the credit.

  4. 2026 · MetroVolt

    Victor arrives needing quick wins to prove himself. Pip's model is the best thing in his new team. “My team delivered” feels, to Victor, like generosity. Pip's name never crosses his mind.

The turning point. A career in a world where the presenter owns the work, and naming the builders was called “messy”.

What might have saved them. A culture where leaders were praised for growing visible people. Victor would have named Pip in every slide if it had made him look good.

— the AnnotatorMany credit-taking bosses don't know they're doing it; they were trained in places where it's normal. That's why framing visibility as something that makes them look good works.

IIIKnow thy enemy

When the Credit Vampire is your manager, it's harder: they write your review, and some credit-taking is normal (“my team delivered” is fair). It becomes a problem when your name disappears and your manager becomes the only route to anyone senior. You win by making your name known above and around them, in ways that also make them look good.

What it wants

To look good to its own boss, especially when it's new. Your work is the best material it has.

Why it thrives here

Because at MetroVolt the manager writes the board paper and the review. If they don't mention you, nobody senior ever hears your name.

What it fears

  • Your direct relationships with senior people.
  • Your name on artefacts that travel without them.
  • Being seen as a manager who doesn't develop people.
  • A calm, direct conversation about visibility.

The three breeds

The Unaware

Says “my team” out of habit. Fixed by the Shared Spotlight.

The Insecure

Needs the wins. Fixed by helping them look good for developing you.

The Climber

Takes credit as strategy. Needs your Wider Web, and maybe your exit.

— the AnnotatorYour boss can take the credit. Make sure they can't take your name.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • “My team delivered”, with no names, ever.
  • You're never invited to present your own work upwards.
  • Your manager is the only person senior people hear from about you.
  • Your ideas come back as “an approach I introduced”.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Shared Spotlight

    Suggest you present the detail, framed as making them look good.

    incantation“For the board, would it help if I took the five minutes on the method? You'd have someone to field the technical questions.”

  2. 2The Wider Web

    Build direct relationships with the people who use your work (and your skip-level). Then your name doesn't depend on one route.

    incantation“I'd love to show Ops how the new model handles cold snaps. Could I book 20 minutes with their planners?”

  3. 3The Direct Word

    If it's a pattern, raise it privately and calmly, as a career request, not an accusation.

    incantation“I'd like more visibility for my work this year. When the model's discussed at the board, could my name be mentioned, or could I attend?”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Board Ambush

    Telling the board it was your idea, in the meeting. You'll be right, and you'll be done.

  • The LinkedIn Correction

    Commenting “Actually, I built this 😊” under his post. 412 people will remember you. None fondly.

  • The Quiet Strike

    Doing worse work so he has less to steal. You'll just have worse work with your name on it.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Unaware. “My team” out of habit.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • They say “we” and “my team”, never “I did”.
  • They're happy when you ask to present.
  • Your name is in the detail, just not the headline.

Do

  1. Offer the Shared Spotlight: you present the method.
  2. Put your name on the artefacts.
  3. Thank them publicly for the chance.

Don’t: The LinkedIn correction. 412 people will remember you, none fondly.

If it gets worse: If your name never appears and you can't reach anyone senior, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Insecure. A pattern of erasure.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • “An approach I introduced” about your approach.
  • You're never invited upwards.
  • Your review doesn't mention your biggest work.

Do

  1. Build the Wider Web: the people who use your work, your skip-level.
  2. Keep the Weekly Deeds Scroll (Act II).
  3. Have the Direct Word, framed as development.
  4. Make it easy for them to look good by naming you.

Don’t: The quiet strike: doing worse work so there's less to steal.

If it gets worse: If they actively block your visibility, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Climber. Actively blocking your visibility.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • They stop you meeting senior people.
  • They rewrite your documents without your name.
  • Your review contradicts your results.

Do

  1. Document your work and its impact, with dates.
  2. Build your skip-level relationship openly.
  3. Ask for the review to include specific evidence.
  4. Plan an internal move or exit.

Don’t: The board ambush: telling the board it was your idea, in the meeting.

If it gets worse: If your review is unfair, challenge it formally with your record.

VIIIDo you wear the crown?

If you lead anyone, check your slides. Tick honestly.

  • Your board papers name no one in your team.
  • You've said “an approach I introduced” about someone else's idea.
  • Your team never presents to your boss.
  • You post about your team's wins without naming them.
  • You'd struggle to say who built your last success.

The cure, if you ticked two: Name the builder on every slide. Bring them to present. Your boss will see a leader who grows people, which is exactly what gets leaders promoted.

IXThe rehearsal

The Direct Word, framed as development, and as a way for Victor to look good.

Scene: Monday 3 February, Pip's 1:1 with Victor.

PipVictor, I'd like more visibility for my work this year. When the forecast's discussed at the board, could I present the method with you?

VictorThe board likes one voice.

PipUnderstood. It might also show them you're building a strong team. Benedict mentioned wanting to see more of the analysts.

Victor…He did say that.

PipAnd I'd love to meet the Ops planning leads monthly. They use the model most. It'd keep the team's work in front of them.

VictorFine. Five minutes at the March board. Make it sharp.

March board: Pip presents for five minutes. Benedict asks him two questions. Victor says “Pip leads our forecasting”. It's the first time anyone has said so.

— the AnnotatorPip never accused Victor of anything. He made naming Pip the thing that would make Victor look good.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your manager presents your work as their idea. First move?

Offer the Shared Spotlight for next time, and start building your Wider Web. Don't confront it in public.

Is “my team delivered” credit-taking?

Not on its own; it's often fair. It's a problem when your name never appears and you can't reach anyone senior.

Your manager blocks you from presenting anywhere. What now?

Have the Direct Word, then build relationships with your skip-level and users. If nothing changes, plan a move.

The Annotator's last wordYour boss can take the credit. Make sure they can't take your name.
