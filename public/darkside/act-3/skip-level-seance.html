The Skip-Level Séance

Summoning your boss's boss. Powerful. Dangerous. Read the instructions.

Threat
Habitat
skip-level 1:1s, open-door policies, the lift with the director
Weakness
transparency: your manager knows you're summoning

IA sighting

Tuesday 11 February · the preparation

After ten weeks of Victor, Pip booked a meeting with Benedict Crane, who, miraculously, was in the building. He prepared a list of eleven problems with Victor. He rehearsed it in the bathroom mirror.

Wednesday 12 February · 11:00 · problem six

Halfway through the list, Benedict held up a hand. “Have you raised these with Victor?” Pip had not. “Then do that first,” said Benedict, not unkindly, “and come back if it doesn't work.” The meeting ended at 11:14.

Wednesday · 17:30

Victor knew. He didn't say how. He didn't need to.

Thursday 13 February · the 1:1

The 1:1 the next day lasted four minutes. It was very polite. It was the coldest four minutes of Pip's year.

— the AnnotatorA skip-level is a relationship, not an emergency exit. Visit when nothing's on fire, and they'll listen when something is.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow Benedict's open door closed

  1. January 2024 · the policy

    Benedict announces an “open door policy”: anyone in Strategy can book time with him, about anything.

  2. February 2024 · the first visitor

    The first person who walks through the door brings a complaint about their manager. Benedict, unsure what to do, mentions it to the manager to “sort it out”.

  3. February 2024 · the lesson

    The manager confronts the complainer. The story spreads. Everyone in Strategy learns that the open door leads straight back to your boss.

  4. 2024 to 2026 · the empty doorway

    Nobody walks through again. The door stays open. Benedict wonders why nobody uses it. And newcomers, like Pip, walk through with eleven grievances, not knowing the history.

The turning point. One complaint, handled clumsily, that taught a whole department the door was a trap.

What might have saved them. Benedict saying, in 2024: “I'll keep this confidential unless someone's at risk. Have you raised it with your manager? Would you like help doing that?”

— the AnnotatorSkip-levels work best as a relationship built in calm weather. Most open doors were closed by the first storm that came through them.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Your boss's boss has power over your manager and over your career: they're in calibration and promotion discussions. A good relationship with them is valuable. A complaint about your manager delivered to them is radioactive: they must back their manager first, and the manager will almost always find out.

What the skip-level wants

Insight into the business, early warning of real problems, and not to be dragged into every disagreement between a manager and their team.

Why it's dangerous here

Because MetroVolt's skip-levels pass complaints back down, and managers find out. A relationship built on ideas is safe. One built on grievances isn't.

What makes it work

  • Your manager knowing about it.
  • Ideas and questions, not complaints.
  • Regular, light contact.
  • Escalating only after trying with your manager.

Three kinds of séance

The Coffee

Regular, open, about the business. Safe and valuable.

The Escalation

A problem raised after trying with your manager, with them told. Legitimate.

The Secret Complaint

Behind your manager's back. Radioactive.

— the AnnotatorSummon the higher power often, openly and for good reasons. Never in secret to curse your lord.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • You've never spoken to your boss's boss.
  • You're tempted to “just mention” your manager's flaws to them.
  • An “open door policy” that nobody seems to walk through.
  • Calibration happens and you don't know who spoke for you.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Open Summoning

    Ask your manager if you can have occasional skip-levels, and share what you'll discuss.

    incantation“Would you mind if I had a quarterly coffee with Benedict? I'd like to understand Strategy's priorities. I'll share what I learn.”

  2. 2The Idea Offering

    Bring the skip-level ideas and questions about the business, not complaints.

    incantation“What's the one thing you wish Grid Analytics did that we don't?”

  3. 3The Proper Escalation

    If you must escalate about your manager: raise it with them first, then tell them you're taking it further, then go with facts.

    incantation“Victor, I raised the workload twice and nothing changed. I'm going to talk to Benedict about it. I wanted you to hear it from me.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Secret Séance

    Complaining about your manager to their boss behind their back. Your manager will find out. Every manager finds out.

  • The Eleven Grievances

    Bringing a long list. One problem, with facts and a request, is heard. Eleven is a rant.

  • The Name-Drop

    “As I was saying to Benedict…” to your manager. You've just told them you're going over their head, as a threat.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

A healthy skip-level culture.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Skip-level coffees are normal.
  • Your manager encourages them.
  • Conversations are about the business.

Do

  1. Ask your manager for a quarterly skip-level coffee.
  2. Bring ideas and questions.
  3. Share a short summary with your manager afterwards.

Don’t: The name-drop: “as I was saying to Benedict” to your manager.

If it gets worse: If your manager is defensive about skip-levels, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

A defensive manager and a closed door.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your manager discourages direct contact upwards.
  • Past complaints were passed back down.
  • Nobody uses the open door.

Do

  1. Keep skip-level contact about the business only.
  2. Share notes with your manager every time.
  3. Build it slowly, over quarters.
  4. If you must escalate, raise it with your manager first and tell them you're going further.

Don’t: The eleven grievances: one problem with facts is heard; eleven is a rant.

If it gets worse: If your manager is part of something serious, you're at Hell.

Hell

Serious issues, and your manager is part of them.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Harassment, safety or fraud is involved.
  • Your manager is involved or protecting it.
  • Raising it with them could put you at risk.

Do

  1. Go to the skip-level, HR or the formal channel directly.
  2. Bring facts and dates.
  3. Ask how confidentiality will be handled.
  4. Keep your own record.

Don’t: The secret séance for ordinary problems. Save direct escalation for when it's truly needed.

If it gets worse: If the formal channel fails, there are external ones (Act VIII, the Whistle of Last Resort).

VIIIHow do you summon?

Check how you use the people above your manager. Tick honestly.

  • You've complained about your manager to their boss before raising it with them.
  • You've never met your skip-level outside a big meeting.
  • You've used your skip-level relationship as a threat.
  • You've brought a list of grievances to a senior person.
  • You'd be uncomfortable if your manager saw your skip-level notes.

The cure, if you ticked two: Book one skip-level coffee this quarter about the business, with your manager's blessing. Share what you learn. Build the relationship before you ever need it.

IXThe rehearsal

The Proper Escalation: with your manager first, then openly.

Scene: Monday 17 February. Pip tries again, the right way.

PipVictor, I want to raise something properly. The late-night emails and weekend requests are wearing me down. I've mentioned it twice. Can we agree core hours?

Victor…I didn't realise it was that bad.

PipAnd separately, would you mind if I had a quarterly coffee with Benedict about Strategy's priorities? I'll share what I learn with you.

VictorAfter last week?

PipLast week I went about it wrong, and I'm sorry. This would be about the business, and open.

Victor…Fine. Quarterly.

Core hours are agreed that week. In April, Pip has coffee with Benedict and asks what he looks for in people he promotes. He sends Victor a three-line summary. Victor replies: “Useful. Thanks.”

— the AnnotatorAn apology, a direct conversation and an open request repaired what one secret meeting had broken.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

You have serious frustrations with your manager. Do you go to their boss?

Not first. Raise them with your manager, clearly. If nothing changes, tell your manager you're escalating, then go with facts.

Is a regular skip-level coffee disloyal?

Not if it's open, agreed and about the business. It's how directors get to know the people they'll judge in calibration.

When can you go straight to the skip-level?

When it's serious (harm, harassment, safety, fraud), or when your manager is the problem in that sense.
