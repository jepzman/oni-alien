The Conflict Phantom

Kind, warm, and gone the moment anything gets difficult.

Threat
Habitat
1:1s that end early, performance reviews that say “fine”
Weakness
small problems raised early, with a suggested solution

IA sighting

Marcus Bell was the kindest manager Pip ever had. He remembered birthdays. He took the blame in public. And for four years he let Neville (the Coasting Zombie, Act II) do almost nothing, because talking to Neville would have been awkward.

The rest of the team carried Neville's work. Two good analysts left. When Pip asked Marcus for honest feedback, Marcus said “you're doing great” and changed the subject to football. Pip found out at calibration that he was “not quite ready for level 4”. Nobody had told him why.

IIKnow thy enemy

Conflict-avoidant managers want to be liked and want people to be happy. Hard conversations feel like cruelty to them. So problems are postponed, feedback is softened into nothing, and decisions that would upset someone simply don't get made.

The cost lands on you: unclear expectations, unfair workloads, surprises in your review. You can't make them brave. But you can make the hard thing easier to say: ask specific questions, raise problems small and early, and bring solutions they can agree to without a fight.

— the Annotator“You're doing great” is not feedback. It's a hug. Hugs are nice. They don't get you promoted.

IIISigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Feedback is always “great”, and reviews still go badly.
  • Underperformers stay for years.
  • Disagreements in the team are never addressed.
  • 1:1s drift to small talk whenever you raise a problem.

IVCounter-spells

  1. 1The Specific Question

    Don't ask “how am I doing?” Ask a question that's easy to answer honestly.

    incantation“What's one thing I'd need to do differently to be ready for level 4?”

  2. 2The Pre-Solved Problem

    Raise problems with a solution attached, so saying yes is easier than avoiding it.

    incantation“The data clean-up is uneven: I've done 80%. Could we split the rest by name, on the board, from Monday?”

  3. 3The Calibration Prep

    Before review season, ask what the people in calibration will say about you, and what evidence you should give them.

    incantation“When my name comes up at calibration, what will the concerns be? I'd like to address them now.”

VForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Ambush Confrontation

    A big, emotional confrontation about everything at once. They'll freeze, apologise, and nothing will change.

  • Going Around Them Too Early

    Taking it straight to their boss without trying with them. It reads as betrayal to a manager who thought you were friends.

  • Accepting the Hug

    Settling for “you're doing great”. Next year's review will say otherwise.

VIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

A kind manager who softens feedback. Specific questions get you real answers.

Nightmare

Problems are festering: unfair workloads, an underperformer. Bring pre-solved problems, one at a time, in writing.

Hell

Avoidance is hurting people: bullying ignored, people burning out. Raise it formally with your skip-level; the kind manager can't protect you.

VIIILoot drop

⚿Five Questions a Nice Manager Can Answer

Easier to answer honestly than “how am I doing?”.

1. What's one thing I should start doing?
2. What's one thing I should stop doing?
3. If I left tomorrow, what would you miss least?
4. What would make you put me forward for level 4?
5. What will people in calibration say about me?

IXThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your kind manager always says “great”. How do you get real feedback?

Ask specific, low-threat questions: “What's one thing I should do differently?”

A coaster has been tolerated for years and you're carrying their work. What do you bring?

A pre-solved problem: a named split of the work, on a shared board, from a specific date.

When do you go over a conflict-avoidant manager's head?

After you've raised it clearly with them, or straight away if someone is being harmed.

The Annotator's last wordKindness that avoids the truth isn't kindness. Ask the question that makes the truth easy to say.
