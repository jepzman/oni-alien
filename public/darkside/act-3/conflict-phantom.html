The Conflict Phantom

Kind, warm, and gone the moment anything gets difficult.

Threat
Habitat
1:1s that end early, performance reviews that say “fine”
Weakness
small problems raised early, with a suggested solution

IA sighting

2022 to 2026 · the kindest manager

Marcus Bell was the kindest manager Pip ever had. He remembered birthdays. He took the blame in public. He once drove an analyst to hospital at midnight.

Four years · Neville

For four years he let Neville (the Coasting Zombie, Act II) do almost nothing, because talking to Neville would have been awkward. The rest of the team carried Neville's work. Two good analysts left. Their exit interviews mentioned “workload fairness”. Marcus read them, felt terrible and did nothing.

Every 1:1 · the hug

When Pip asked Marcus for honest feedback, Marcus said “you're doing great” and changed the subject to football. Pip asked again in a different way. Marcus said “honestly, nothing major” and asked about Pip's weekend.

October · calibration

Pip found out at calibration that he was “not quite ready for level 4”. The reasons were in a document Marcus had helped write. Nobody had told Pip any of them.

— the Annotator“You're doing great” is not feedback. It's a hug. Hugs are nice. They don't get you promoted.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow a kind boy became the Conflict Phantom

  1. 1980s · a loud house

    Marcus grows up with a father who shouts. Every disagreement at home is a frightening one. He learns to keep the peace, to make people laugh, to smooth things over.

  2. 1990s · a promise

    As a teenager, Marcus promises himself he will never make anyone feel the way his father made him feel.

  3. 2012 · a manager

    Marcus becomes a manager and keeps the promise. He's warm, generous and safe. His team adores him.

  4. 2016 onwards · the cost

    But to Marcus, every hard conversation feels like shouting. So he doesn't have them. Problems are postponed, feedback is softened into nothing, and his kindness quietly becomes avoidance. The people who pay are the ones he most wants to protect.

The turning point. A childhood promise to never be cruel that, over the years, became a rule never to be honest.

What might have saved them. Someone teaching him that clear feedback, kindly given, is the opposite of shouting. The kindest managers are clear ones.

— the AnnotatorConflict Phantoms usually avoid conflict because, somewhere, they learned it was dangerous. Specific, gentle questions make honesty feel safe for them, too.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Conflict-avoidant managers want to be liked and want people to be happy. Hard conversations feel like cruelty to them. So problems are postponed, feedback is softened into nothing, and decisions that would upset someone don't get made. The cost lands on you. You can make the hard thing easier to say.

What it wants

Harmony. Everyone happy, nobody upset, no raised voices.

Why it thrives here

Because MetroVolt rewards managers with high engagement scores and few complaints, and conflict-avoidant managers have both, right up until the good people leave.

What it fears

  • Upsetting anyone.
  • Big, emotional confrontations.
  • Being seen as unfair.
  • Having to deliver bad news.

The three breeds

The Peacekeeper

Avoids conflict to protect people. Responds to specific, gentle questions.

The People-Pleaser

Wants to be liked. Responds to pre-solved problems that please everyone.

The Absentee

Avoids by disappearing. Needs things in writing and the skip-level.

— the AnnotatorKindness that avoids the truth isn't kindness. Ask the question that makes the truth easy to say.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Feedback is always “great”, and reviews still go badly.
  • Underperformers stay for years.
  • Disagreements in the team are never addressed.
  • 1:1s drift to small talk whenever you raise a problem.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Specific Question

    Don't ask “how am I doing?” Ask a question that's easy to answer honestly.

    incantation“What's one thing I'd need to do differently to be ready for level 4?”

  2. 2The Pre-Solved Problem

    Raise problems with a solution attached, so saying yes is easier than avoiding it.

    incantation“The data clean-up is uneven: I've done 80%. Could we split the rest by name, on the board, from Monday?”

  3. 3The Calibration Prep

    Before review season, ask what the people in calibration will say about you, and what evidence you should give them.

    incantation“When my name comes up at calibration, what will the concerns be? I'd like to address them now.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Ambush Confrontation

    A big, emotional confrontation about everything at once. They'll freeze, apologise, and nothing will change.

  • Going Around Them Too Early

    Taking it straight to their boss without trying with them. It reads as betrayal to a manager who thought you were friends.

  • Accepting the Hug

    Settling for “you're doing great”. Next year's review will say otherwise.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Peacekeeper. A kind manager who softens feedback.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Feedback is always “great”.
  • They answer specific questions honestly.
  • Problems do get fixed, slowly.

Do

  1. Ask specific, low-threat questions.
  2. Ask for one thing to start, one to stop.
  3. Thank them for honest answers, so honesty feels safe.

Don’t: Accepting the hug. Next year's review will say otherwise.

If it gets worse: If problems are festering (workload, an underperformer), you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The People-Pleaser. Problems festering for years.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Unfair workloads are tolerated.
  • Good people leave.
  • 1:1s drift to small talk whenever you raise a problem.

Do

  1. Bring pre-solved problems, one at a time, in writing.
  2. Prepare for calibration: ask what will be said about you.
  3. Make the hard thing a small, concrete step.
  4. Thank them when they act.

Don’t: The ambush confrontation about everything at once. They'll freeze and nothing will change.

If it gets worse: If avoidance is hurting people (bullying ignored, burnout), you're at Hell.

Hell

The Absentee. Avoidance that harms people.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Bullying or harassment is being ignored.
  • People are burning out.
  • Your manager won't act even when asked directly.

Do

  1. Raise it clearly with them once, in writing.
  2. Then take it to your skip-level or HR.
  3. Support colleagues who are affected.
  4. Don't wait for the kind manager to protect you; they can't.

Don’t: Going around them before trying with them. It reads as betrayal to a manager who thought you were friends.

If it gets worse: If nobody acts, use the formal channel. It exists for exactly this.

VIIIAre you a Phantom?

Kind people are often Phantoms. Tick honestly.

  • You've told someone “you're doing great” when you had concerns.
  • You've put off a hard conversation for more than a month.
  • You've let someone underperform because talking about it felt cruel.
  • Your team found out about a problem in their review, not from you.
  • You change the subject when things get tense.

The cure, if you ticked two: This week, give one person one piece of clear, kind feedback: what you saw, why it matters, what you'd like instead. It's the kindest thing you'll do all month.

IXThe rehearsal

The Specific Question and the Calibration Prep: easy to answer honestly.

Scene: Thursday 2 October, Pip's 1:1 with Marcus, before calibration.

PipMarcus, what's one thing I'd need to do differently to be ready for level 4?

MarcusOh, honestly, you're doing great—

PipThank you. Pretend you had to name one. What would it be?

Marcus…You don't present your own work. People don't know what you do.

PipThat's really useful. And when my name comes up at calibration, what will the concerns be?

MarcusThat. And that you said yes to everything in the budget review.

PipThank you. Genuinely. Can we check in on both monthly?

Marcus looks relieved. He's wanted to say this for months. He just needed someone to ask in a way that didn't feel like shouting.

— the AnnotatorTwo specific questions got Pip more honest feedback than two years of “how am I doing?”

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your kind manager always says “great”. How do you get real feedback?

Ask specific, low-threat questions: “What's one thing I should do differently?”

A coaster has been tolerated for years and you're carrying their work. What do you bring?

A pre-solved problem: a named split of the work, on a shared board, from a specific date.

When do you go over a conflict-avoidant manager's head?

After you've raised it clearly with them, or straight away if someone is being harmed.
