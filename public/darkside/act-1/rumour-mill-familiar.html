The Rumour Mill Familiar

Small, fast, everywhere. Feeds on half a sentence.

Threat
Habitat
the kitchen, the smoking area, private chats with names like “the real team”
Weakness
facts, shared early, by the people who know them

IA sighting

Wednesday 22 October · 09:10 · the kitchen

Pip mentioned to one person that Marcus had “looked stressed after the budget meeting”. Just that. Seven words.

11:30 · Customer Ops

Customer Ops believed Grid Planning was being shut down. The version that reached them included “Marcus is devastated” and “they're moving everyone to Strategy”.

13:00 · lunch

Two people asked Pip whether he was being made redundant. One offered him a hug. He accepted it, confused.

15:00 · Marcus's office

Marcus had heard that Pip was spreading rumours about him. He was very kind about it, which was worse. Marcus had, in fact, been stressed because the coffee machine on floor 9 was broken.

— the AnnotatorA rumour needs three things: an anxious audience, a silent leadership, and you, saying “I probably shouldn't say this, but…”. Remove yourself from the recipe.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the grapevine became MetroVolt's real news service

  1. 2017 · the layoffs

    MetroVolt cuts 300 jobs. Staff learn about it from a newspaper website, two hours before the internal email. Trust in official channels collapses in an afternoon.

  2. 2018 · the replacement

    If the news won't come from the top, people find it themselves: the kitchen, the smoking area, private chats with names like the real team. Tamsin on floor 27 becomes the unofficial news desk (though she says almost nothing).

  3. 2019 to 2024 · faster than email

    The grapevine is often right, and always faster. Leadership responds by saying even less, which makes the grapevine stronger.

  4. 2025 · the familiar

    Every budget season the Familiar wakes up, hungry. It feeds on silence and half-sentences. Seven words about a stressed manager become a department closure by lunchtime.

The turning point. The day staff read about their own layoffs in the newspaper, and decided never again to rely on official news.

What might have saved them. Leaders who shared what they knew early, and said what they didn't. Silence is the Familiar's food.

— the AnnotatorThe grapevine isn't the problem. It's the symptom of leadership silence. You can't fix the silence, but you can stop feeding the symptom.

IIIKnow thy enemy

The rumour mill grows in the dark: uncertainty plus silence from leadership. People fill gaps with guesses, and guesses travel faster than facts because they're more interesting. The grapevine is also genuinely useful: it often knows things weeks before the announcement. The skill is to listen without feeding it.

What it wants

Certainty. People repeat rumours to make sense of an anxious situation, and to feel included.

Why it thrives here

Because MetroVolt's leaders say little until everything is final, and staff stopped trusting official news in 2017. Every silence is filled with the most interesting available guess.

What it fears

  • Facts, shared early.
  • Leaders who say “here's what I don't know yet”.
  • People who answer “I'll wait to hear it from my manager”.
  • Direct questions to the person concerned.

Three kinds of rumour

The Gossip

About people. Never repeat it.

The Signal

An early warning about real change. Listen, check, prepare quietly.

The Weaponised

Planted to hurt someone. Never touch it; protect the target.

— the AnnotatorHear everything. Repeat nothing you couldn't sign.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • “I probably shouldn't say this, but…”
  • Leadership has been quiet for weeks during a budget season.
  • Private chats are busier than team channels.
  • You hear your own words back, changed.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Listening Ear

    Listen to rumours, thank people, and treat them as weak signals, not facts. Check with someone who knows.

    incantation“Interesting, thanks. I'll wait to hear it from Marcus before I believe it.”

  2. 2The Sealed Lips

    Never repeat anything about a person you wouldn't say with them in the room.

  3. 3The Fact Lantern

    If you lead people, share what you know early and say what you don't. Silence is the rumour mill's food.

    incantation“Here's what I know: budgets are being reviewed. Here's what I don't: whether it affects us. I'll update you on Friday either way.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • Feeding the Familiar

    Passing on a juicy rumour to be interesting. You become the source, and sources get remembered.

  • The Strategic Leak

    Planting a rumour on purpose to shape a decision. It's traceable, it's unethical, and it's how careers end quietly.

  • The Total Denial

    Ignoring the grapevine entirely. You'll be the last to know everything, including the things that matter.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

An anxious week. Rumours, but no harm.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • There's real uncertainty (budget, reorg).
  • Rumours are about change, not people.
  • Leaders are quiet but not hostile.

Do

  1. Listen; treat rumours as weak signals.
  2. Don't repeat them.
  3. Ask your manager directly what they know.

Don’t: Feeding the Familiar to seem interesting. You become the source.

If it gets worse: If the grapevine is faster than every official channel, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The grapevine runs the building.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • People believe the private chats over official emails.
  • Rumours get personal.
  • Leaders are silent for weeks.

Do

  1. Build a few trusted sources and check before acting.
  2. Use the Rumour Filter before repeating anything.
  3. If you lead people, run weekly fact updates, even “nothing new”.
  4. Correct personal rumours kindly when you hear them.

Don’t: The total denial: ignoring the grapevine. You'll be the last to know what matters.

If it gets worse: If rumours are being planted about people, you're at Hell.

Hell

Rumours are weaponised: stories planted about rivals.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Rumours target specific people.
  • They appear before key decisions.
  • The stories are traceable to someone who benefits.

Do

  1. Keep your own conduct spotless.
  2. Talk directly to the people a rumour is about.
  3. Keep a record if you're targeted.
  4. Raise planted rumours with HR if they're harming someone.

Don’t: The strategic leak. It's traceable, unethical, and it's how careers end quietly.

If it gets worse: If you're the target, document it and talk to your manager or HR early.

VIIIDo you feed the Familiar?

Everyone has, at some point. Tick honestly.

  • You've said “I probably shouldn't say this, but…” this month.
  • You've passed on something you hadn't checked.
  • You've repeated something about a person you wouldn't say to their face.
  • You enjoy being the one who knows first.
  • You've heard your own words come back, changed.

The cure, if you ticked two: Before you repeat anything, ask: do I know it, would I say it with them here, and does repeating it help anyone? If not, let it end with you.

IXThe rehearsal

Owning a rumour you started: first, directly, briefly.

Scene: Wednesday 22 October, 15:05. Marcus's office. Pip owns it.

PipMarcus, I said something careless this morning and it grew. I said you looked stressed after the budget meeting. That's all I said. I'm sorry.

MarcusAh. I heard I was devastated and the department was closing.

PipThat's how it came back to me too. Do you want me to correct it with anyone?

MarcusNo, I'll send the team a short note. Honestly, I should have said something after the budget meeting. People are anxious.

PipWhat can you tell us?

MarcusThat budgets are being reviewed, we don't know the outcome, and I'll update everyone Friday, either way.

Friday: Marcus sends a four-line update. The kitchen goes quiet for the first time in a week.

— the AnnotatorTwo things killed this rumour: Pip owning his part fast, and Marcus filling the silence with facts. You can do the first one every time.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

A colleague says your team will be cut next month. What do you do?

Thank them, don't repeat it, and ask your manager directly: “I've heard this. Is there anything you can tell me?”

You let slip that your manager looked stressed and it's grown into a story. Now what?

Go to your manager first and own it: “I said something careless and it grew. I'm sorry. Here's what I actually said.”

You manage a team during a budget review and know very little. What do you tell them?

The Fact Lantern: what you know, what you don't, and when you'll update them. Even “nothing new” beats silence.

The Annotator's last wordHear everything. Repeat nothing you couldn't sign.
