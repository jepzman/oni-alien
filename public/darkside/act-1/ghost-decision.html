The Ghost Decision

Made in a room you weren't in. By people who won't say.

Threat
Habitat
leadership off-sites, “quick calls”, decks marked FINAL_v7
Weakness
asking to be in the room next time, with a reason they can accept

IA sighting

On a Monday in October, the Grid Planning team learned from a calendar invite titled “Transition: Grid Analytics to Strategy” that half their work was moving to Dorian's team. Nobody had asked them. Nobody could say who had decided.

“It came out of the off-site,” said Marcus, Pip's manager, who had not been at the off-site. The decision was already in a deck called FINAL_v7. Six earlier versions existed. None had been shared.

IIKnow thy enemy

Ghost decisions happen when decision making is informal and invisible: leaders decide in small groups, often for good reasons (speed, confidentiality), and forget to explain. Sometimes they avoid consulting on purpose, because consultation would mean argument.

You usually can't reverse a ghost decision. But you can learn how it was made, influence how it's carried out, and get into the room for the next one. Those three are worth more than one reversed decision.

— the AnnotatorBeing angry about not being consulted is fair. Staying angry is expensive. Spend the anger on getting invited next time.

IIISigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Big changes arrive as calendar invites.
  • Nobody can name who decided.
  • A deck is called FINAL and has a version number above five.
  • Your manager is as surprised as you are.

IVCounter-spells

  1. 1The Calm Inquiry

    Ask, without accusation, what problem the decision solves and how it was made. Understand before you object.

    incantation“Help me understand the problem this solves. Then I can make the transition work as well as possible.”

  2. 2The Implementation Seat

    Volunteer to help carry it out. The people who implement a decision shape most of what it becomes.

    incantation“I'd like to be on the transition team. I know where the data and the gotchas are.”

  3. 3The Next-Room Request

    Ask to be involved next time, with a reason that helps them.

    incantation“Next time Grid Analytics is discussed, could Marcus or I join for twenty minutes? We'd catch the data issues early.”

VForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Rebellion

    Organising your team to refuse or slow-walk the decision. Leadership hears “resistance”, and the next decision will be about you.

  • The Leak Hunt

    Digging for who said what at the off-site. You'll get gossip, not facts, and a reputation for digging.

  • The Martyr Email

    A long email to all about how disrespectful this was. True, maybe. Career-limiting, certainly.

VIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

Leaders moved fast and forgot to explain. A calm question gets you the reasons and a seat in the transition.

Nightmare

A pattern of decisions without consultation. Get into the implementation, and build a link to the people who do get invited.

Hell

Decisions are made against your team on purpose. Protect your people's work in writing, find a sponsor in the room, and decide whether this is still your place.

VIIILoot drop

⚿The Ghost Decision Debrief

Five questions to ask (calmly) after any decision you weren't part of.

1. What problem does this decision solve?
2. What options were considered?
3. What's fixed, and what's still open?
4. Who will carry it out, and can I help?
5. How can our team be involved next time?

IXThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Half your team's work moves to another team without consultation. First reaction?

The Calm Inquiry: understand the problem it solves. Then ask for the Implementation Seat.

Your team wants to protest. Do you join?

Channel it: help your manager write a calm, factual note on risks and what the team needs. Protest becomes input.

How do you avoid the next ghost decision?

Make your team useful to the people in the room, and ask openly for a seat when your area is discussed.

The Annotator's last wordIf you can't be in the room, be the person the room calls.
