The Ghost Decision

Made in a room you weren't in. By people who won't say.

Threat
Habitat
leadership off-sites, “quick calls”, decks marked FINAL_v7
Weakness
asking to be in the room next time, with a reason they can accept

IA sighting

Monday 20 October · 08:31 · a calendar invite

The Grid Planning team learned from a calendar invite titled “Transition: Grid Analytics to Strategy” that half their work was moving to Dorian's team. Nobody had asked them. Nobody could say who had decided.

09:15 · the team meeting

“It came out of the off-site,” said Marcus, who had not been at the off-site. He looked as surprised as everyone else. The decision was already in a deck called FINAL_v7. Six earlier versions existed. None had been shared.

11:00 · the private chat

The team's private chat lit up: “who decided this??”, “is this about the budget?”, “are we next?” Someone suggested refusing to hand over the data. Someone else suggested a strongly worded email to all.

17:30

Pip, who had read the Map of Hidden Thrones entry by now, did neither. He went home and wrote down three questions to ask in the morning. They turned out to be the right three.

— the AnnotatorBeing angry about not being consulted is fair. Staying angry is expensive. Spend the anger on getting invited next time.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow MetroVolt learned to decide in small rooms

  1. 2020 · the leak

    A planned reorganisation leaks to the local press before staff are told. It's humiliating for leadership and frightening for staff. The CEO orders an investigation. It finds nothing.

  2. 2020 · the new rule

    Leadership decides that sensitive decisions will be made in small rooms, with as few people as possible, and announced only when final.

  3. 2021 to 2024 · the creep

    The rule spreads. Not just sensitive decisions, but ordinary ones are made at off-sites and announced as done. Small rooms are faster. Nobody argues with a calendar invite.

  4. 2025 · the ghost

    Decisions appear from nowhere, fully formed. Even the managers affected are surprised. Secrecy, introduced for one good reason, has become a habit for every reason.

The turning point. A sensible rule for sensitive decisions that slowly became the default for all decisions.

What might have saved them. A simple distinction: secret for genuinely sensitive things, consulted for everything else. And someone checking the difference each time.

— the AnnotatorMost ghost decisions aren't malicious. They're habits of speed and secrecy. That's why asking calmly for a seat next time often works.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Ghost decisions happen when decision making is informal and invisible: leaders decide in small groups, often for good reasons (speed, confidentiality), and forget to explain. You usually can't reverse one. But you can learn how it was made, influence how it's carried out, and get into the room for the next one.

What leaders want

Speed, no leaks, and no long arguments. Consultation feels slow and risky.

Why it thrives here

Because off-sites are where leaders feel most free to decide, and nobody below them is there. And because once a decision is in a deck called FINAL, challenging it looks like resistance.

What it doesn't survive

  • Calm questions about the problem it solves.
  • People who volunteer to make it work.
  • A reasonable request to be involved next time.
  • Implementation that reveals the gaps.

Three kinds of ghost

The Secret

Genuinely confidential. Accept it; help with the transition.

The Shortcut

Made fast to save time. Ask to be in the room next time.

The Dodge

Made in private to avoid argument. Build allies in the room.

— the AnnotatorIf you can't be in the room, be the person the room calls.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Big changes arrive as calendar invites.
  • Nobody can name who decided.
  • A deck is called FINAL and has a version number above five.
  • Your manager is as surprised as you are.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Calm Inquiry

    Ask, without accusation, what problem the decision solves and how it was made. Understand before you object.

    incantation“Help me understand the problem this solves. Then I can make the transition work as well as possible.”

  2. 2The Implementation Seat

    Volunteer to help carry it out. The people who implement a decision shape most of what it becomes.

    incantation“I'd like to be on the transition team. I know where the data and the gotchas are.”

  3. 3The Next-Room Request

    Ask to be involved next time, with a reason that helps them.

    incantation“Next time Grid Analytics is discussed, could Marcus or I join for twenty minutes? We'd catch the data issues early.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Rebellion

    Organising your team to refuse or slow-walk the decision. Leadership hears “resistance”, and the next decision will be about you.

  • The Leak Hunt

    Digging for who said what at the off-site. You'll get gossip, not facts, and a reputation for digging.

  • The Martyr Email

    A long email to all about how disrespectful this was. True, maybe. Career-limiting, certainly.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Shortcut. Leaders moved fast and forgot to explain.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • It's a one-off.
  • Leaders explain when asked.
  • There's room to shape the details.

Do

  1. Ask calmly what problem the decision solves.
  2. Volunteer for the transition team.
  3. Ask to be involved next time, with a reason.

Don’t: The martyr email to all. True, maybe. Career-limiting, certainly.

If it gets worse: If decisions keep arriving this way, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

A pattern of decisions without consultation.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Every big change arrives as a calendar invite.
  • Your manager is never consulted either.
  • Teams are angry and talking about resistance.

Do

  1. Use the Ghost Decision Debrief (five calm questions).
  2. Get into the implementation, where details are decided.
  3. Help your manager write a calm note on risks and needs.
  4. Build links to people who do get invited.

Don’t: The rebellion: organising the team to slow-walk it. Leadership will hear “resistance”.

If it gets worse: If decisions are made against your team on purpose, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Dodge, aimed at you. Decisions are made against your team deliberately.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your team is shrinking, decision by decision.
  • Questions are treated as disloyalty.
  • Nobody in the room speaks for you.

Do

  1. Protect your people's work in writing.
  2. Find a sponsor who is in the room.
  3. Make your team's value visible in numbers.
  4. Decide whether this is still your place.

Don’t: The leak hunt: digging for who said what at the off-site. You'll get gossip and a reputation.

If it gets worse: If your team is being dismantled, look after your people first, then yourself.

VIIIDo you make ghosts?

Anyone with a bit of authority can make a ghost decision. Tick honestly.

  • You've announced a decision to people it affected without asking them first.
  • You've used “it was decided” without saying by whom.
  • You've made a team decision in a 1:1 with one person.
  • You've had a deck called FINAL that nobody else saw.
  • You think consulting people slows things down.

The cure, if you ticked two: Before your next decision that affects others, ask two of them one question: “What would I be missing?” It takes ten minutes and saves weeks.

IXThe rehearsal

The Calm Inquiry and the Implementation Seat: understand, then help shape it.

Scene: Tuesday 21 October, 09:30. Marcus's office. Pip has three questions.

PipMarcus, I know you weren't at the off-site either. Do we know what problem the move solves?

MarcusBenedict thinks Strategy needs its own analysts. They keep waiting for our numbers.

PipThat's fair, actually. What's fixed, and what's still open?

MarcusThe move's fixed. Who moves, and how, isn't.

PipThen I'd like to be on the transition team. I know where the data and the gotchas are. And could we ask to join for twenty minutes next time Grid Analytics comes up?

Marcus…That's a much better email than the one the team wanted to send.

Pip joins the transition team. In Act III he moves to Strategy with the work, on terms he helped write.

— the AnnotatorThe decision didn't change. Pip's place in it did. That's usually the win available.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Half your team's work moves to another team without consultation. First reaction?

The Calm Inquiry: understand the problem it solves. Then ask for the Implementation Seat.

Your team wants to protest. Do you join?

Channel it: help your manager write a calm, factual note on risks and what the team needs. Protest becomes input.

How do you avoid the next ghost decision?

Make your team useful to the people in the room, and ask openly for a seat when your area is discussed.

The Annotator's last wordIf you can't be in the room, be the person the room calls.
