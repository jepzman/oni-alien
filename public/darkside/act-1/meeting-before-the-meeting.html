The Meeting Before the Meeting

By the time the meeting starts, the decision has already been made.

Threat
Habitat
corridors, coffee queues, 15-minute 1:1s the day before
Weakness
nothing. This one is a tool. Learn it.

IA sighting

Tuesday 14 October · 10:00 · the Network Investment Board

Pip presented his capacity plan. Seven directors, forty minutes, eighteen slides. He had rehearsed. He was ready.

10:06 · slide 3

Gareth Pound asked why nobody had consulted Network Assets. Pip said he'd been planning to. Gareth said “I see” in the voice people use when they've decided something.

10:11 · slide 5

Priya Nair asked how it affected call-centre volumes. Pip didn't know. Lord Varnish said “interesting” in the voice people use for a dead bird. The board asked Pip to “take it away and come back with more alignment”.

10:41 · the corridor

Priya caught him by the lifts. “Next time, show me first,” she said kindly. “I'd have said yes.” Then she added, “So would Gareth, probably, if you'd asked him before the meeting instead of during it.”

— the AnnotatorThe rule is: no one should hear an important idea for the first time in a meeting. Especially not the person who can kill it.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow MetroVolt's board meetings became theatre

  1. 2013 · the Kessler incident

    A director presents a major investment to the board. Another director, unwarned, produces data showing it's based on a wrong assumption. In front of everyone. The first director never recovers.

  2. 2013 · the lesson

    Every director learns the same thing that week: never let anything surprise you in the board, and never surprise anyone else. The safest board meeting is one where everything was agreed in advance.

  3. 2014 to 2024 · the habit

    Real decisions move to corridors, coffees and 15-minute calls the day before. The board becomes a place where decisions are confirmed, not made. Everyone who's been here long enough knows.

  4. 2025 · the newcomers

    Nobody tells new people. They arrive at the board with eighteen slides and an open question, and walk straight into the old ambush.

The turning point. One public humiliation in 2013, after which no director ever again walked into the board without knowing the answer.

What might have saved them. It doesn't need saving: pre-wiring is a good habit. What needs fixing is that nobody tells the newcomers.

— the AnnotatorPre-wiring is not sneaky if you do it openly. It's how sensible people make sure nobody is ambushed, including you.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Formal meetings are bad places to make decisions. People feel exposed and nobody wants to agree in public to something they haven't thought through. So sensible people decide before, in private, and the meeting confirms it. That's pre-wiring.

What decision makers want

No surprises, time to think, and the chance to shape an idea before they have to defend it in public.

Why it works

Because people support what they helped shape. A concern raised privately gets fixed; the same concern raised in a meeting becomes a position that must be defended.

What kills proposals

  • A powerful person hearing it first in the room.
  • An unanswered concern from someone with a veto.
  • Surprising someone's boss.
  • Eighteen slides.

Three kinds of meeting

The Formality

Everything agreed beforehand. Ten minutes and a nod. The goal.

The Ambush Arena

Nobody pre-wired. Every concern becomes a fight. Where Pip went.

The Rubber Stamp

Decided by one person beforehand, without anyone else. Pre-wire that person's advisors.

— the AnnotatorMeetings don't make decisions. People do. Usually on Tuesday, near the coffee machine.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Meetings where nothing new is ever decided.
  • Directors who arrive already knowing the answer.
  • Your proposals get “take it away and align”.
  • Someone says “I wish I'd seen this earlier”.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Walkabout

    List everyone who can say yes, no or “not yet”. See each one, briefly, before the meeting. Ask, don't sell.

    incantation“I'm bringing a capacity plan to the board on the 14th. Could I show you in ten minutes first? I want to know what would worry you.”

  2. 2The Concern Harvest

    Write down each concern and change the proposal where it's fair. Then credit them in the meeting.

    incantation“Priya raised call-centre volumes, so slide 5 now shows the impact by week.”

  3. 3The Quiet Count

    Before the meeting, know who supports, who's neutral and who objects. If an objector holds a veto, don't go yet.

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Ambush

    Surprising a powerful person with your idea in public, hoping momentum will carry it. It won't. You've made them look uninformed, and they'll remember.

  • The Secret Alliance

    Pre-wiring everyone except your opponent, to outvote them. They'll know, and they'll fight everything you bring from now on.

  • The Promise Sprinkle

    Telling each person what they want to hear. In the meeting they compare notes.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

A few stakeholders and a simple decision.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • You know everyone who can say no.
  • Nobody has a strong prior position.
  • The decision is reversible.

Do

  1. See each key person for ten minutes beforehand.
  2. Ask what would worry them.
  3. Adjust and thank them in the meeting.

Don’t: Selling. Ask questions; listen; change things.

If it gets worse: If stakeholders have conflicting needs, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

Many stakeholders with conflicting needs.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Different directors want different things.
  • Someone has a veto.
  • There's history between departments.

Do

  1. Do the Walkabout with everyone who can block it.
  2. Use the Concern Harvest: change what's fair, credit them.
  3. Bring options, not one answer.
  4. Do the Quiet Count: don't go until the veto-holder is at least neutral.

Don’t: The secret alliance: pre-wiring everyone except your opponent to outvote them.

If it gets worse: If one person's mood decides everything, you're at Hell.

Hell

A board where one person decides, and their mood varies.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Proposals live or die on one person's reaction.
  • Pre-wiring them directly isn't possible.
  • Their advisors matter more than the papers.

Do

  1. Pre-wire their trusted advisors (see the Map of Hidden Thrones).
  2. Time your proposal for a good day.
  3. Keep it to one page.
  4. Be ready to wait.

Don’t: The promise sprinkle: telling each person what they want to hear. They compare notes in the meeting.

If it gets worse: If nothing can get through, find a smaller first step that can.

VIIIAre you an ambusher?

Pre-wiring is a skill. Ambushing (even by accident) costs trust. Tick honestly.

  • You've presented an idea to a group without showing the key person first.
  • You've been surprised by an objection you could have predicted.
  • You've raised a concern about someone's proposal for the first time in a meeting.
  • You think pre-wiring is “politics”.
  • Your proposals often get “take it away and align”.

The cure, if you ticked two: Before your next important meeting, list everyone who can say no and see each one for ten minutes. And when you have concerns about someone else's proposal, tell them before the meeting, too.

IXThe rehearsal

The Walkabout and the Concern Harvest: ask, don't sell.

Scene: Monday 10 November, Gareth's office, the day before Pip's second attempt.

PipGareth, I'm bringing the capacity plan back on Tuesday. Before I do, I want to know what would worry you.

GarethNobody asked Network Assets last time. Half your capacity numbers come from our data.

PipThat's fair, and I'm sorry. Would you check the asset numbers on slide 4? And could we present that part together?

Gareth…Fine. Joint review Thursday.

Tuesday, 10:00. Pip presents seven slides. Gareth says “we've checked the asset data together”. Priya says “I've seen the call-centre impact on slide 5”. Lord Varnish says “good”. It takes eleven minutes.

— the AnnotatorSame plan, same board. The difference was four conversations and one apology, all before the meeting.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your proposal goes to the board in a week. Three directors haven't seen it. What now?

The Walkabout: ten minutes with each, this week, asking what would worry them.

One director hates the idea and has a veto. Should you present anyway?

Usually not. Hear the objection properly, change what's fair, or find a smaller first step they can live with.

Isn't pre-wiring just politics?

It's preparation. Done openly, it gives every decision maker time to think, which leads to better decisions.
