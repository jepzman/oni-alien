The Turf Wars of the Two Towers

Two departments. One problem. Neither will touch it, both will own it.

Threat
Habitat
the gap between two teams, RACI charts nobody agreed
Weakness
a shared customer problem with numbers on it

IA sighting

Monday 3 November · the spreadsheet

While checking forecast data, Pip found 11,400 smart meters that had stopped reporting. On average they had been silent for 63 days. Their customers were getting estimated bills.

Tuesday to Friday · four slides

Pip asked who owned the problem. Grid Planning said it was a Customer Ops problem (customers complained). Customer Ops said it was a Network Assets problem (the meters were broken). Network Assets said it was a Lumen problem (the project installed them). Lumen said it was a Grid Planning problem (it was data). Each department produced a slide proving it was someone else's.

Monday 10 November · the tickets

Pip looked at the ticket system. The problem had been opened as a ticket 37 times since August. Each ticket had been reassigned between the four teams an average of five times, then closed as “not our remit”.

That week

2,100 customers complained about estimated bills. One of them was Pip's aunt.

— the AnnotatorNothing ends a turf war like a number with a currency sign next to it, and a newspaper headline next to that.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow four departments built a no-man's-land

  1. 2021 · the restructure

    MetroVolt reorganises into clear departments, each with its own target: complaints for Customer Ops, uptime for Network Assets, install schedule for Lumen, forecast accuracy for Grid Planning.

  2. 2022 · the budgets

    Budgets become fixed per department. Every hour spent on someone else's target is an hour stolen from your own. Helping becomes expensive.

  3. 2023 · the gap

    Problems that touch several targets belong to nobody. Fixing them costs your budget and improves someone else's number. Owning them is pure cost and possible blame.

  4. 2025 · the war

    Each department is behaving perfectly rationally. Together they've built a no-man's-land, and 11,400 customers are living in it.

The turning point. The day every department got its own target and its own budget, and nobody got the space between them.

What might have saved them. One shared target (say, customer complaints) across all four departments. Or a leader above them who owned the gaps.

— the AnnotatorTurf wars are usually a design problem, not a people problem. That's why the fix is a shared number and a shared owner, not a fight.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Turf wars happen when ownership is unclear and incentives differ: each department is measured on its own targets, so a shared problem is pure cost. You can't settle a turf war from the middle, but you can make the customer problem impossible to ignore, and offer a way for both towers to win.

What each tower wants

To hit its own target, protect its own budget, and not be blamed for the problem in the gap.

Why it thrives here

Because MetroVolt measures departments separately and rewards them separately. The gap has no target, no budget and no owner. Rational departments leave it alone.

What ends it

  • A customer number with a cost on it.
  • A small, time-boxed joint fix.
  • A leader above both towers who has to decide.
  • Shared credit.

Three kinds of turf

The Orphan

Nobody's problem. Needs a number and an owner.

The Hot Potato

Passed around fast. Needs a joint fix nobody can pass.

The Land Dispute

Everybody wants it (budget, status). Needs a decision from above both.

— the AnnotatorIn a turf war, the only safe flag to wave is the customer's.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • “That's not our remit.”
  • Problems bounce between teams in the ticket system.
  • Every department has a slide about why it's someone else's.
  • Customers are suffering in the gap.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Shared Wound

    Frame the problem around the customer and the cost, not the org chart. Put a number on it.

    incantation“11,400 customers have had estimated bills for two months. That's about £1.2m in disputed billing. Who can we put on it together?”

  2. 2The Joint Pact

    Propose a small, time-boxed joint fix with a named person from each side. Small is easier to say yes to.

    incantation“Could Ops and Assets each give one person for four weeks? I'll run the data side.”

  3. 3The Escalation Above Both

    If it stays stuck, take it, together with both sides, to the first leader above both towers.

    incantation“Priya and Gareth both agree this needs an owner. Could you decide who, by Friday?”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • Picking a Tower

    Joining one side to win the war. The other tower now treats you as an enemy combatant, permanently.

  • The Land Grab

    Quietly taking over the disputed area to build your own empire. See entry 8. Don't be entry 8.

  • The Sigh and Shrug

    Deciding it's not your problem either. The 11,400 customers disagree.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Orphan. Two teams who've simply never talked.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Nobody has a strong position.
  • The teams are friendly.
  • It's a new problem.

Do

  1. Get both teams in one room with the customer data.
  2. Agree a named person each.
  3. Report the fix to both leads, crediting both.

Don’t: Picking a tower to blame.

If it gets worse: If it's a long-running rivalry, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Hot Potato. A long-running rivalry over a shared problem.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Each side has a slide blaming the other.
  • Tickets bounce between teams.
  • Customers are suffering in the gap.

Do

  1. Frame it as a Shared Wound: customers and cost, with numbers.
  2. Propose a small Joint Pact: one person each, four weeks.
  3. Keep it small enough to say yes to.
  4. Give both leaders equal credit.

Don’t: The land grab: quietly taking over the area to build your own empire.

If it gets worse: If the leaders above both towers are rivals too, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Land Dispute, all the way up.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • The directors above both towers are rivals.
  • Joint fixes are refused on principle.
  • The problem is reaching the press or the regulator.

Do

  1. Write the Orphan Problem Brief with numbers.
  2. Take it to the first leader above both (often the chief of staff).
  3. Ask for a decision on an owner by a date.
  4. Keep a record of the cost of delay.

Don’t: The sigh and shrug. The customers in the gap disagree.

If it gets worse: If nobody will own it, the regulator eventually will. Make sure your brief is on record.

VIIIAre you defending a tower?

Departments are made of people. Tick honestly.

  • You've said “that's not our remit” this month.
  • You've reassigned a ticket without talking to anyone.
  • You have a slide about why a problem is someone else's.
  • You know of a customer problem nobody owns, and you've done nothing.
  • You'd rather a problem stay unsolved than be solved by another team.

The cure, if you ticked two: Next time something lands in the gap, ask “what would it take to fix this together?” before “whose is it?”

IXThe rehearsal

The Shared Wound and the Joint Pact: customers first, small ask, both towers win.

Scene: Wednesday 12 November, 14:00. Room 9C. Priya and Gareth, together for the first time.

PipThanks for coming. One slide: 11,400 meters, 63 days silent, 2,100 complaints, about £1.2 million in disputed bills.

GarethThe hardware's fine. It's a data issue.

PriyaMy agents can't tell which customers to call back.

PipI don't think it matters whose it is yet. Could each of you lend one person for four weeks? I'll run the data side. We report back to you both.

Priya…Four weeks. One person. Fine.

GarethFine. But the report says Network Assets helped.

PipIt'll say both teams fixed it. Because you will have.

— the AnnotatorPip never decided whose problem it was. He made it small enough, and shared enough, that nobody needed to win the argument.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

A problem sits between two departments and both say it isn't theirs. What's your first move?

Make the customer impact and cost visible, with numbers, to both sides at once.

One department lead asks you to help prove it's the other team's fault. Do you?

No. Offer to help fix it instead. The moment you pick a tower, you lose the other one.

The joint fix works. Who gets credit?

Both towers, publicly and equally. That's what makes them say yes next time.
