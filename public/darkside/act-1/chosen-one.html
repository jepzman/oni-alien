The Chosen One

Every court has a favourite. It is rarely you.

Threat
Habitat
talent reviews, stretch assignments, the VP's “quick coffee”
Weakness
your own visible results, and a sponsor of your own

IA sighting

September · the board presentation

Jules got the Lumen board presentation. Pip had built half the slides.

October · the regulator

Jules went to the regulator meeting in London. Pip found out from the photo on the intranet.

November · the programme

Jules was selected for the “high potential” programme, which nobody had told Pip existed. Every time Lord Varnish needed someone, he said “ask Jules”.

December · the worst part

Jules was good. Kind, fast, clear. Pip couldn't even hate them properly. He tried once, for an afternoon, and Jules brought him a coffee and asked how his model was going. It was exhausting.

— the AnnotatorMost favourites aren't chosen for being brilliant. They're chosen for being reliable in front of the person choosing. You can learn that.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow Jules became the Chosen One

  1. March 2023 · Lord Varnish's first big project

    Simon Varnish launches his first flagship at MetroVolt: a dashboard for the CEO. The night before the launch, it breaks.

  2. March 2023 · 23:00

    Jules, then a junior analyst, happens to be working late, notices, and fixes it by 02:00. Nobody asked them to. The launch goes perfectly.

  3. 2023 onwards · “ask Jules”

    Simon never forgets. Jules becomes his safe pair of hands. Each success brings another chance; each chance brings another success. It compounds.

  4. 2025 · the circle

    Jules is genuinely good, but also simply first in line. Others, just as good, never got their 02:00 moment in front of the person who decides.

The turning point. One late night, in front of exactly the right person, at exactly the right time.

What might have saved them. Nothing here is a curse for Jules. For Pip, it's a lesson: favour often begins with being visibly reliable for the decider, once, when it counts.

— the AnnotatorFavouritism is usually not a plot. It's a leader's memory of who saved them. You can't manufacture that night, but you can make sure they know you're reliable too.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Favouritism is partly human nature: leaders trust people who are like them, who've succeeded before, or who make their lives easier. Once someone is chosen, they get more chances, so they succeed more, so they get chosen more. Sometimes it's unfair. Sometimes it's earned, and simply more visible than your work.

What the chooser wants

Safety. A leader under pressure picks the person who won't let them down. It's a bet on certainty, not a judgement on everyone else.

Why it thrives here

Because opportunities at MetroVolt are handed out informally, by one person's memory. There's no open process for the board presentation or the regulator trip. Whoever is top of mind gets it.

What breaks the spell

  • Asking, directly, for specific opportunities.
  • Your own visible, reliable results.
  • A sponsor of your own.
  • Open processes for opportunities.

Three kinds of favourite

The Earned

Chosen for real reliability. Learn from them; ask for your turn.

The Mirror

Chosen for being like the boss. Build visibility beyond the boss.

The Convenient

Chosen for being nearby. Make yourself nearby, too.

— the AnnotatorDon't wait to be chosen. Ask to be considered. It's shocking how often that works.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • The same name comes up for every opportunity.
  • Programmes and trips you hear about after they're full.
  • Your work is praised; theirs is rewarded.
  • You catch yourself counting their wins.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Honest Mirror

    Ask your manager what the favourite does that you could do more of. Listen without defending.

    incantation“Jules gets a lot of the visible work. What would I need to show for you to put me forward too?”

  2. 2The Direct Ask

    Ask for specific opportunities, by name. Leaders often simply don't think of the quiet ones.

    incantation“I'd like to present the meter data at the next board. Could I take that one?”

  3. 3The Own Sponsor

    Find someone senior who values your work and will put your name forward. Different courts have different favourites.

  4. 4The Fairness Check

    If favouritism crosses into unfair treatment (pay, promotion, protected traits), document it and raise it formally.

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Usurper's Plot

    Undermining the favourite to take their place. You'll be seen as bitter, and the leader will protect their choice.

  • The Sulk

    Withdrawing and doing the minimum because it's unfair. It confirms the leader's choice.

  • The Copycat

    Imitating the favourite's style. You'll be a weaker version of someone who's already there.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Earned. A leader who leans on one reliable person.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • The favourite is genuinely good.
  • Your manager supports you when asked.
  • Nobody has ever said no to you; you've never asked.

Do

  1. Ask what the favourite does that you could do more of.
  2. Ask for a specific opportunity, by name.
  3. Deliver it reliably and visibly.

Don’t: The copycat: imitating the favourite's style.

If it gets worse: If there's a clear inner circle you can't enter, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

An inner circle.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • The same few names come up for everything.
  • Opportunities are never advertised.
  • Your direct asks get vague answers.

Do

  1. Find your own sponsor, in another part of the business.
  2. Build visibility beyond your manager.
  3. Keep asking for specific things, in writing.
  4. Keep delivering.

Don’t: The usurper's plot: undermining the favourite. You'll look bitter and the leader will protect their choice.

If it gets worse: If favouritism is tied to personal relationships or protected traits, you're at Hell.

Hell

Favouritism that's unfair, or discriminatory.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • It affects pay or promotion.
  • It follows personal relationships, not work.
  • It seems linked to gender, age, background or similar.

Do

  1. Keep a factual record of opportunities and who got them.
  2. Raise it with HR or a formal channel.
  3. Get advice if it's discrimination.
  4. Look after yourself; this isn't a career tip, it's a fairness issue.

Don’t: The sulk: withdrawing to the minimum. It confirms the leader's choice.

If it gets worse: If the organisation won't address it, protect your career elsewhere.

VIIIDo you play favourites?

If you lead anyone, you probably do, a little. Tick honestly.

  • You give the same person most of the visible opportunities.
  • You think of the same name first, every time.
  • You've never advertised an opportunity within your team.
  • Some team members have never presented to your boss.
  • You're more patient with people who remind you of yourself.

The cure, if you ticked two: Next time an opportunity comes up, ask: who hasn't had a turn? Give it to them, and back them. You'll find new favourites.

IXThe rehearsal

The Honest Mirror and the Direct Ask: specific, positive, about the future.

Scene: Thursday 11 December, Pip's 1:1 with Marcus.

PipJules gets a lot of the visible work, and honestly, they're good. What would I need to show for you to put me forward too?

MarcusHm. Jules always tells Simon what's coming before he asks. You do great work, but he only hears about it from me.

PipThat's useful. Could I present the meter data at the January board?

MarcusI'll ask. Send Simon a short heads-up before it, like you did last week. He liked that.

PipAnd is the high-potential programme open to nominations?

Marcus…I didn't know you wanted it. I'll nominate you for the next intake.

— the AnnotatorMarcus had never thought of Pip for these things. Not because he didn't rate him: because Pip had never asked.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

The favourite gets an opportunity you wanted. What's your first move?

Ask your manager what you'd need to show to be considered next time, and ask for a specific opportunity.

You become the favourite. Is that a problem?

Enjoy it, and share it: bring others into the work and credit them. Courts change; allies stay.

When is favouritism a formal issue?

When it affects pay, promotion or treatment unfairly, especially linked to protected characteristics. Document and raise it.

The Annotator's last wordDon't wait to be chosen. Ask to be considered. It's shocking how often that works.
