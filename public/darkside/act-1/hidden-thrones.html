The Map of Hidden Thrones

The org chart shows the chairs. Not who sits on them.

Threat
Habitat
floor 27, the CEO's calendar, the space between two boxes on the chart
Weakness
curiosity: asking how decisions actually got made

IA sighting

In September Pip wrote a two-page proposal: move the Lumen smart-meter rollout to the districts with the worst outage history first. It was obviously right. He sent it, as the org chart said he should, up the line to Lord Varnish, whose box was the biggest on the page.

Nothing happened for five weeks. Then, on a Tuesday, the plan was approved, almost word for word, as “Ottoline's sequencing idea”. Pip had never heard of Ottoline. He looked her up. Chief of Staff. A small box, off to the side, with a dotted line. He had sent his idea to the biggest box and it had walked, by itself, to the smallest one, because that was where decisions lived.

IIKnow thy enemy

Every organisation has two maps. The formal map (the org chart) shows reporting lines. The real map shows influence: who the decision makers listen to, who controls the calendar, who has been right before, who can say no quietly. The two overlap, but less than you'd think.

Hidden thrones are usually not sinister. Chiefs of staff, long-serving engineers, the finance partner who builds the budget model, the assistant who decides which emails reach the CEO: they hold power because they hold information, access or trust. Learn who they are, treat them well, and the building starts to make sense.

— the AnnotatorPower = information + access + trust. Titles are just where the building keeps the furniture.

IIISigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Decisions arrive from somewhere you can't see on the chart.
  • A senior person always says “let me run it past someone” and you never learn who.
  • A junior-looking person is in every important meeting.
  • Your proposal comes back approved under someone else's name.
  • People are noticeably nicer to one assistant than to their own boss.

IVCounter-spells

  1. 1The Cartographer's Question

    After any decision, ask how it was made. People love explaining how things work, and you learn the map.

    incantation“That decision landed really quickly. Out of curiosity, who shaped it? I'm trying to learn how things work up there.”

  2. 2The Hand-Drawn Map

    Draw the real map for your area: decision makers, the people they listen to, and the gatekeepers. Update it monthly. Keep it to yourself.

  3. 3The Courteous Approach

    Introduce yourself to the hidden throne with something useful, not a request. Gatekeepers remember who brought them value.

    incantation“Hi Ottoline, I built the outage data behind the Lumen sequencing. If it's ever useful for the CEO's briefings, I'm happy to keep it updated.”

  4. 4The Double Route

    Send important ideas up the formal line and make sure the real decision makers hear about them, openly, with your manager's knowledge.

    incantation“Marcus, I'd like to walk Ottoline through the proposal too, since she's shaping Lumen. OK with you if I copy you in?”

VForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Secret Back Door

    Going round your manager to the throne without telling them. It works once. Then your manager learns of it from the throne, which is worse than any rejection.

  • The Throne-Hunter

    Befriending gatekeepers only for access. They can smell it. Their whole job is smelling it.

  • The Cynic's Map

    Deciding that because power is informal, merit doesn't matter. Merit is the entry ticket. The map is how you use it.

VIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

A healthy organisation with a few informal influencers. Learn who they are and keep them informed.

Nightmare

Real power is far from the chart and nobody admits it. Your hand-drawn map is essential; ask “who else should see this?” every time.

Hell

A court where proximity beats competence and favour can be withdrawn overnight. Protect your record, build relationships across several thrones, and keep your exit options warm.

IXThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your proposal has sat with a VP for five weeks. What do you do?

Ask your manager who else shapes this decision, and offer to brief them openly. Don't send it round the VP in secret.

You discover the CEO's chief of staff makes most rollout decisions. First move?

Bring her something useful (data, a clear summary), not a request. Introduce yourself with value.

Is drawing a power map manipulative?

No. Understanding how decisions are made is part of the job. It becomes manipulative only when you use it to deceive.

The Annotator's last wordDon't ask who's in charge. Ask who they call before they decide.
