Survivor's Haunting

You kept your job. Somehow it doesn't feel like winning.

IA sighting

Monday 2 August · after

Horizon was done. 584 people had left MetroVolt. On floor 9, every fourth desk was empty. Some still had things on them: a mug, a plant, a photograph someone hadn't come back for.

Marcus's desk

Marcus's desk in Grid Planning had his football scarf on the chair. Nobody moved it for three weeks. Nobody could explain why.

The team meeting

Pip's first team meeting after Horizon began, by habit, with the week's priorities. Asha was looking at Tomasz's empty chair. Pip stopped, put down his list, and said that this week was hard. Asha cried for a minute, then laughed at herself for crying, then they talked about Tomasz and Marcus for twenty minutes. Then they talked about the work.

September · the quiet

People who had kept their jobs were quieter, more careful, more tired. Two of them told Pip they felt guilty for being relieved. One said she was waiting for round two.

— the AnnotatorThe ones who stay have work to do: for the people who left, and for themselves.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the Chronicler learned about the haunting

  1. 2017 · the first survival

    The Chronicler keeps their job in the 2017 cuts. Their closest colleague doesn't. They feel relief, then shame at the relief.

  2. 2017 · the silence

    Nobody talks about it. Leadership says “let's focus on the future”. Survivors keep their heads down. Productivity drops for a year; nobody connects it to the cuts.

  3. 2018 to 2026 · seven reorgs

    The Chronicler survives every round. Each time, the same guilt, the same silence, the same quiet year afterwards.

  4. 2027 · writing it down

    After Horizon, the Chronicler writes this entry, because nobody told them in 2017 that the haunting was normal, and that it lifts when you name it.

The turning point. An organisation that told survivors to move on, so nobody was allowed to grieve the people who left.

What might have saved them. One leader in 2017 saying, “This is hard. People we valued have gone. It's OK to feel that.”

— the AnnotatorI've survived seven rounds. The guilt never goes away entirely. It gets lighter every time you help someone who didn't.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Layoff survivors often feel guilt, fear and exhaustion: guilt at keeping their jobs, fear of the next round, exhaustion from absorbing the work of people who left. It's so common it has a name: survivor syndrome. It lifts faster when it's named, when the people who left are honoured, and when the work is honestly re-planned.

What survivors need

Permission to feel strange, honesty about what's next, and a workload that fits the team that's left.

Why it lingers

Because organisations want to “move forward” quickly, and silence lets fear grow. The empty desks speak louder than any memo.

What lifts it

  • Naming it as a team.
  • Marking farewells properly.
  • Helping the people who left.
  • An honest re-plan of the work.

Three kinds of haunting

The Guilt

“Why me and not them?” Helping the people who left eases it.

The Fear

“Am I next?” Honesty from leaders, and your go-bag, ease it.

The Weight

Their work on your desk. Only a re-plan eases it.

— the AnnotatorMove the scarf when you're ready. Not before. But do, eventually, move the scarf.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Empty desks with someone's things still on them.
  • Guilt that you stayed and they didn't.
  • Fear that you're next.
  • Their work is landing on you, unspoken.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Naming

    Say it out loud as a team: this is hard, people we liked have gone, and it's OK to feel strange.

    incantation“Before we start: this week is hard. Marcus and Tomasz were good colleagues. It's fine to feel odd about it.”

  2. 2The Farewell Rite

    Mark people leaving properly: a message, a lunch, a reference. It matters to them and to everyone watching.

    incantation“We're doing lunch for Tomasz on Friday. Everyone's invited. And I've written him a LinkedIn recommendation.”

  3. 3The Honest Re-plan

    Re-plan the work for the team that's left (see More with Less). Don't let the missing people's work land silently.

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Brave Face

    Pretending everything's fine and pushing on. The haunting just goes underground and comes out as burnout.

  • The Blame Ghost

    Deciding the people who left must have been weaker. It's comforting. It's usually wrong, and it makes you smaller.

  • The Bunker

    Keeping your head down and trusting nobody in case you're next. You'll be miserable and less visible.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

A team that lost a colleague and feels it.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • People are quiet and sad.
  • Work continues.
  • Leaders acknowledged it.

Do

  1. Name it in the team.
  2. Mark farewells.
  3. Check in with each person 1:1.

Don’t: The brave face.

If it gets worse: If fear of the next round is spreading, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

Fear and overload after deep cuts.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • People expect a round two.
  • Work is piling on survivors.
  • Leaders say “move forward”.

Do

  1. Ask leaders for honesty about what's next, and pass it on.
  2. Re-plan the work honestly.
  3. Help the people who left: references, introductions.
  4. Encourage people to pack their go-bags; it reduces fear.

Don’t: The blame ghost: deciding the leavers were weaker.

If it gets worse: If people are burning out or deeply distressed, you're at Hell.

Hell

Burnout and distress after repeated rounds.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • People are ill, anxious or withdrawn.
  • Mistakes are rising.
  • Good people are quietly leaving too.

Do

  1. Treat it as a wellbeing issue: point people to support (EAP, doctor).
  2. Reduce workload formally.
  3. Escalate the risk to leadership in writing.
  4. Look after yourself too.

Don’t: The bunker: everyone keeping their head down in silence.

If it gets worse: If you can't recover here, the Escape Portal (Act III) is a legitimate door.

VIIIAre you haunted?

Survivors often don't notice. Tick honestly.

  • You feel guilty for keeping your job.
  • You're waiting for round two.
  • You're doing a missing colleague's work without saying so.
  • You haven't contacted anyone who left.
  • You've stopped speaking up in meetings since the cuts.

The cure, if you ticked two: Message one person who left this week: a reference, an introduction, or just “how are you?”. Then tell your manager one piece of work you can't carry. Both help.

IXThe rehearsal

The Naming and the Farewell Rite.

Scene: Monday 2 August, 09:30. Pip's first team meeting after Horizon.

PipBefore we start on priorities: this week is hard. Tomasz and Marcus were good colleagues and good people. It's fine to feel odd about it.

AshaI feel bad that I'm relieved.

PipMe too. I think everyone who stayed does. It doesn't make us bad people.

AshaIs there going to be a round two?

PipI asked Priya. She says no further cuts are planned this year, and she'll tell us straight if that changes. I'll pass on whatever I hear.

PipWe're doing lunch for Tomasz on Friday. And I've written him a LinkedIn recommendation. Want to add one?

Friday: eleven people come to Tomasz's lunch, including Gareth. On Monday, someone finally moves Marcus's scarf, folds it, and posts it to him.

— the AnnotatorNaming it took two minutes. The team was quicker, kinder and more honest for months afterwards.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

The first team meeting after layoffs. What do you say first?

Name it: this is hard, people we liked have gone. Then talk about the work.

You feel guilty that you kept your job. What helps?

Help the people who left: references, introductions. And talk to someone; survivor's guilt is common and real.

The people who left had work. Where does it go?

Into an honest re-plan, agreed with your manager. Not silently onto whoever is nearest.

The Annotator's last wordThe ones who stay have work to do: for the people who left, and for themselves.
