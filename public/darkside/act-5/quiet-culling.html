The Quiet Culling

Nobody fires you. Your work just… drifts away.

Threat
Habitat
reassignments, missing invites, projects given to “fresh eyes”
Weakness
noticing early, asking directly, and keeping your options and records

IA sighting

April · the report

The weekly outage report, which Morag had written by hand every week since 2009, was “automated to free up her capacity”. The automated version had her name nowhere on it.

May · the invites

Morag stopped being invited to the operations review. Then to the incident planning group. Her manager said the meetings had been “streamlined”.

June · the plan

After fifteen years of strong reviews, Morag was given a “performance improvement plan” citing “resistance to new ways of working”. Her Slack status, for the first time since February 2025, did not say drowning. It said nothing at all.

July · the coffee

Pip, who still had coffee with Morag sometimes, noticed that nobody had told her anything directly. Everything had been “capacity”, “streamlining” and “new ways of working”. Nobody had said the word they meant.

— the AnnotatorQuiet things deserve loud questions. Ask early, while there's still a choice.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow MetroVolt learned to cull quietly

  1. 2017 · the bad press

    The 2017 layoffs make the newspapers. Leadership decides never again: future reductions will be “managed” quietly, wherever possible.

  2. 2018 to 2024 · the method

    People who don't fit new plans are sidelined slowly: work moved, invites dropped, performance plans introduced. Many leave on their own. No redundancy costs, no headlines.

  3. 2025 · the target

    Morag's hero status from 2009 makes her hard to cut openly. Her refusal of help (see Act II, the Martyr Banshee) and her single-person knowledge make her look, to Horizon, like a risk.

  4. 2027 · the culling

    Nobody decides to push Morag out in a meeting. It happens through a dozen small decisions, none of which anyone has to defend.

The turning point. A choice to avoid headlines by making departures look voluntary, which made them harder to see and harder to challenge.

What might have saved them. A direct conversation, early. If Morag's role was changing, someone owed her the words, and the redundancy terms that come with them.

— the AnnotatorQuiet culling saves the company money and saves managers from hard conversations. The cost lands on the person who's never told.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Sometimes organisations don't make people redundant; they make them want to leave. Work is moved, invites stop, a performance process appears. Nobody says the real reason. It's cheaper and quieter than redundancy, and it can, in some cases, be unlawful.

What the organisation wants

A departure without redundancy costs, conflict or headlines.

Why it's hard to see

Because each step has a reasonable explanation. Only the pattern tells you, and it's easy to deny a pattern.

What protects you

  • A direct question, asked early.
  • A dated record of changes.
  • Proper advice if it looks like constructive dismissal.
  • Options you control.

Three kinds of quiet

The Genuine Change

Your role really is changing. Ask, and you'll be told.

The Drift

Nobody's decided anything; you're just being forgotten. A direct question restarts things.

The Culling

A planned push out. Records, advice, and your own exit plan.

— the AnnotatorIf everyone keeps saying “capacity” and “streamlining”, ask what word they actually mean.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Your projects are reassigned “to give you capacity”.
  • Invites to key meetings stop arriving.
  • Your manager is suddenly very formal with you.
  • A performance plan appears after years of good reviews.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Direct Question

    Ask your manager plainly whether your role is changing and what the plan is for you.

    incantation“I've noticed my projects moving to others. Can you tell me honestly what the plan is for my role?”

  2. 2The Record

    Keep a dated record of reassignments, missed invites, and any sudden performance concerns.

  3. 3The Advice

    If it looks like constructive dismissal or an unfair process, get proper advice: HR, a union or a lawyer.

  4. 4The Pivot

    Sometimes the best answer is to move first: an internal role, or an external one, on your terms.

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Denial

    Telling yourself it's nothing for six months. By then it's done.

  • The Overwork Rescue

    Working twice as hard to win the work back. It rarely changes a decision already made, and it burns you out.

  • The Hallway Campaign

    Complaining to everyone. It confirms whatever story is being told about you.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Genuine Change, poorly communicated.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your manager answers when asked.
  • There's a new role for you.
  • Nothing is hostile.

Do

  1. Ask the Direct Question.
  2. Agree your new role in writing.
  3. Ask what support you'll have.

Don’t: The denial: waiting six months to ask.

If it gets worse: If questions get vague answers and work keeps vanishing, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Drift, or the early culling.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Invites stop; work moves.
  • Answers are vague.
  • Your manager is suddenly formal.

Do

  1. Ask directly, in writing, what the plan is for your role.
  2. Keep the Record.
  3. Talk to your skip-level or HR.
  4. Prepare your options.

Don’t: The overwork rescue: working twice as hard to win it back.

If it gets worse: If a performance plan appears after years of good reviews, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Culling: a planned push out.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • A sudden performance plan.
  • Your role is being emptied.
  • You're isolated from colleagues.

Do

  1. Get proper advice (union, lawyer): this may be constructive dismissal.
  2. Respond to any plan in writing, factually.
  3. Ask whether redundancy is on the table; it may be fairer to you.
  4. Plan your exit on your terms.

Don’t: The hallway campaign: complaining to everyone. It confirms the story.

If it gets worse: If you leave, negotiate. A quiet culling often has room for a better exit.

VIIIAre you being culled, or culling?

This one has two sides. Tick honestly.

  • Your projects have been moved “for capacity” this year.
  • You're no longer invited to meetings you used to attend.
  • Your manager has become formal with you.
  • Or: you've sidelined someone rather than telling them the truth.
  • Or: you've said “streamlining” when you meant something else.

The cure, if you ticked two: If you're being culled: ask the direct question this week. If you're culling: have the honest conversation. Either way, someone has to say the real word.

IXThe rehearsal

The Direct Question, asked calmly.

Scene: Thursday 15 July. Morag's 1:1 with her manager, after a coffee with Pip.

MoragI've noticed my report, my meetings and the incident group have all moved away from me. Can you tell me honestly what the plan is for my role?

ManagerIt's about capacity, and new ways of—

MoragI understand. But in plain words: is my role being phased out?

Manager…Network Ops is being restructured. Your role isn't in the September structure.

MoragThank you for saying it. Then I'd like that confirmed in writing, and I'd like to discuss redundancy terms, not a performance plan.

Morag gets advice. The performance plan is withdrawn. She leaves in October with redundancy pay, a reference that mentions 2009, and a job offer from a rival utility's control room.

— the AnnotatorOne plain question turned a quiet culling into an honest redundancy, with the money and dignity that come with it.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

Your best projects are being moved to others “for your capacity”. What do you ask?

Directly, calmly: what's the plan for my role?

A performance plan appears after years of good reviews. What do you do?

Take it seriously, keep records, ask for specific evidence, and get advice. It may be genuine; it may be a process.

When should you move rather than fight?

When the direct question confirms it, and your energy is better spent on your next role.
