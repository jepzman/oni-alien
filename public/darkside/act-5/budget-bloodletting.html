The Budget Bloodletting

Every team is asked to bleed a little. Those who bleed first bleed most.

Threat
Habitat
Horizon review sessions, zero-based budget templates, Finance's 8 a.m. calls
Weakness
value in numbers, options in tiers, and never volunteering in the room

IA sighting

Monday 10 May · the template

A spreadsheet arrived from Vexmoor: Horizon Zero-Based Budget Template v3. Every team had to justify every pound of its budget “from zero”, with a column headed Savings Opportunity (%). The column was mandatory.

Thursday 20 May · the Customer Ops review, 14:00

Priya, Pip, Rupert Thorne, Oliver Thane from Vexmoor and Lena from Finance. Oliver opened with a benchmark: “Peer utilities run customer analytics with two people.” Pip's team was three. Four, if you counted the frozen chair.

14:12

“What can you give up?” asked Oliver. Pip, who had read Act I, did not answer straight away. He let the silence sit for four seconds, then put a single page on the table.

14:20

The page showed what the team's £310,000 bought: £1.1 million a year in avoided complaint costs and regulator penalties. Then three tiers of cuts, and what each one broke. Lena from Finance nodded, very slightly. Rupert looked at his watch; it was nearly time for the 14:05, which he had already missed.

— the AnnotatorThe first team to volunteer a cut gets the deepest one. The team with a value ledger gets a conversation.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the bloodletting became a ritual

  1. 2019 · the voluntary rounds

    The CFO introduces voluntary savings rounds (see Act I, Plausible Silence). Volunteers get cut deepest; the silent keep their budgets.

  2. 2021 · the targets

    Finance switches to targets per department: everyone cuts 5%, regardless of size or value. Efficient teams suffer most.

  3. 2025 · zero-based

    Vexmoor sells the parent group “zero-based budgeting”: justify everything from zero, every year. It sounds rigorous. In practice it rewards the best storytellers.

  4. 2027 · Horizon

    Every team at MetroVolt fills in the template. Those with numbers survive better. Those with feelings, and those who volunteer, bleed.

The turning point. Budget processes that rewarded silence, then size, then storytelling, but never simply value.

What might have saved them. A process that asked “what is this worth?” before “what can you give up?”. Failing that, teams who answer the first question even when it isn't asked.

— the AnnotatorThe template asks what you can give up. Answer what you're worth first. Then answer the template.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Cost-cutting reviews are designed to find money fast. Reviewers look for teams that can't explain their value in pounds, volunteers who give things up easily, and duplication. Your defence is value in numbers, options in tiers, and allies in Finance.

What the reviewers want

Savings they can defend to the parent group, quickly, with numbers. They're not hunting you; they're hunting a total.

Why some teams bleed more

Because they describe effort, not value; volunteer cuts in the room; or have no one in Finance who understands what they do.

What protects a budget

  • A one-line value case in pounds.
  • Tiered options with consequences.
  • A Finance partner who's seen it before the review.
  • Not volunteering in the room.

Three kinds of review

The Trim

Small, fair cuts across the board. Offer a tier; protect the core.

The Hunt

Looking for big savings in a few places. Value ledgers decide who's found.

The Decree

The number is decided above. Shape how it lands, not whether.

— the AnnotatorBring numbers to a bloodletting. Feelings are what the knife is looking for.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • A template arrives asking you to justify every pound “from zero”.
  • Review sessions where each team presents to Finance and a consultant.
  • “Savings targets” are given per department, regardless of size.
  • Teams start quietly comparing who's cutting what.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Value Ledger

    Show what your budget buys, in money or risk avoided. Finance speaks pounds, not effort.

    incantation“Our £310k budget avoided about £1.1m in complaint costs and regulator fines last year. That's the case, in one line.”

  2. 2The Three Tiers

    Offer tiered options: cut 5%, 10%, 15%, and what each one breaks. Let them choose, with consequences visible.

    incantation“At 5% we lose the weekly report. At 10%, the regulator dashboard. At 15%, one analyst and the complaint model.”

  3. 3The Finance Ally

    Talk to your Finance partner before the review. They often know where savings will come from anyway.

    incantation“Lena, before the Horizon review: what does a credible submission from us look like?”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Eager Sacrifice

    Volunteering a big cut in the room to look like a team player. You'll get the cut, and no credit (see Act I, Plausible Silence).

  • The Padded Budget

    Inflating your budget so the cut leaves you whole. It works once. Then Finance trusts nothing you send.

  • The Neighbour's Throat

    Arguing that another team should be cut instead of yours. They'll remember, and the next round is theirs to argue.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Trim: modest, fair cuts.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Everyone's asked for a small percentage.
  • The process is open.
  • Finance is reasonable.

Do

  1. Offer the smallest tier with honest consequences.
  2. Protect the work that creates the most value.
  3. Thank Finance for the process.

Don’t: The padded budget. Credibility is your best asset next year.

If it gets worse: If reviewers are hunting big savings in a few places, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Hunt: big savings, in a few places.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Consultants run the reviews.
  • Benchmarks are used against teams.
  • Some teams will be cut deeply.

Do

  1. Write the Value Ledger: one line, in pounds.
  2. Offer the Three Tiers.
  3. See your Finance partner before the review.
  4. Question benchmarks politely: what's in the peer group?

Don’t: The eager sacrifice in the room.

If it gets worse: If the number is decided above and your team is the target, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Decree: your cut is decided; only the shape is open.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • You're told the number, not asked.
  • Roles are included.
  • There's little appeal.

Do

  1. Shape how it lands: which work stops, how people are treated.
  2. Push for redeployment and fair process for anyone affected.
  3. Record the risks of the cut in writing.
  4. Look after your team (see the Executioner's Burden).

Don’t: The neighbour's throat: arguing another team should be cut instead.

If it gets worse: If roles in your team are cut, read the Executioner's Burden before any conversation.

VIIIDo you bleed easily?

Check how you defend your team's resources. Tick honestly.

  • You describe your team's work as effort, not value.
  • You've volunteered savings in a meeting.
  • You've never met your Finance partner.
  • You couldn't say in one line what your budget buys.
  • You've padded a budget to survive a cut.

The cure, if you ticked two: Write one sentence this month: “Our budget of £X buys £Y of value, because Z.” If you can't write it, you've found your homework.

IXThe rehearsal

The Held Pause, the Value Ledger and the Three Tiers.

Scene: Thursday 20 May, 14:12. The Customer Ops review.

OliverPeer utilities run customer analytics with two people. What can you give up?

Four seconds of silence. Pip counts them.

PipBefore that, one page. Our £310k buys about £1.1 million a year in avoided complaint costs and regulator penalties. The calculation's on the back.

OliverBenchmarks still suggest two people.

PipCould you share what the peer teams cover? Ours also runs regulator reporting, which most don't. Here's what 5, 10 and 15% would break.

LenaThe regulator reporting isn't in the benchmark, Oliver. I checked.

Customer Ops analytics is cut by 5%: the weekly deep-dive and the board appendix go. The team stays. Pip buys Lena a coffee.

— the AnnotatorPip didn't argue, plead or volunteer. He brought value, options and an ally. That's the whole spell.

XThe trial
