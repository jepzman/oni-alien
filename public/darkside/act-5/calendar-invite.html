The Day of the Calendar Invite

Fifteen minutes. A room on the ground floor. Someone from HR you've never met.

Threat
Habitat
ground-floor meeting rooms, invites with no agenda, the last week of a consultation
Weakness
knowing your rights before the day, and never signing anything on it

IA sighting

Monday 2 June · 17:12 · the invite

Marcus Bell, Head of Grid Planning, 23 years at MetroVolt, received an invite for Tuesday at 10:00: Quick catch-up, 15 minutes, room 4B, ground floor, with Rupert Thorne and someone called Karen from HR. Marcus had never been to room 4B. Nobody he knew had ever been to room 4B.

Tuesday 3 June · 09:40

Marcus's system access stopped working. He told himself it was IT.

10:00 · room 4B

A glass of water. A box of tissues. An envelope. Rupert said “this is a difficult conversation” and then read from a page. Marcus's role was at risk of redundancy as part of Horizon. Karen explained the consultation process. Marcus nodded throughout, because nodding was what he had always done.

10:14

Karen slid a settlement agreement across the table and said it would be “simplest for everyone” if he signed today. Marcus picked up the pen. Then he remembered something Pip had once forwarded him from this book, and put it down.

— the AnnotatorThe meeting is fifteen minutes. The decisions you make in the next fifteen days matter far more. Don't make them in the room.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the fifteen-minute meeting was designed

  1. 2017 · the first big layoff

    MetroVolt's 2017 cuts go badly: a press leak, angry scenes, a tribunal claim. Lawyers are brought in to design “a safe process”.

  2. 2017 · the script

    The process they design is legally careful and humanly cold: short invites, neutral rooms, a script, HR present, access removed the same morning, a settlement offered at once.

  3. 2018 to 2026 · the template

    The process is never revisited. Managers aren't trained in it; they're handed it. Rupert reads from the page because he was given the page.

  4. June 2027 · room 4B

    Marcus, who has run Grid Planning kindly for twelve years, walks into a process designed to minimise risk to the company, not harm to him. He doesn't know that he can slow it down.

The turning point. A process designed by lawyers to protect the company, which nobody ever redesigned to also protect people.

What might have saved them. A process with a human at its centre: a manager who knows the person, time to digest, advice offered, no pressure to sign. And Marcus knowing his rights before the day.

— the AnnotatorThe script isn't evil. It's just designed for the company's safety, not yours. Your safety is your job: slow it down, get advice, sign nothing in the room.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Redundancy meetings are usually short, scripted and designed to be legally safe for the company. You're not expected to respond on the day, and in most places you don't have to sign anything on the day. What you're owed depends on your country, contract and length of service.

What the company wants

A clean, quick, legally safe process, and ideally a signed settlement, which usually means you give up the right to make claims.

Why people sign too fast

Shock, shame, and the wish for it to be over. And because the agreement is presented as “simplest for everyone”.

What protects you

  • Taking everything away to read.
  • Independent advice (in some countries the company must pay for it).
  • Asking about notice, pay, pension, benefits, criteria, redeployment and references.
  • Your own records and contacts, never company data.

Three kinds of day

The Consultation

“At risk”, with a process and time. Use the time.

The Offer

A settlement on the table. Never sign it in the room.

The Escort

Same-day exit, access cut. Stay calm; everything else can follow by email.

— the AnnotatorNod if you must. Sign nothing. Ask for it in writing. Leave. Then call someone who knows employment law.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • A short invite with no agenda from your manager plus someone from HR.
  • The meeting is in a room you never use, often on the ground floor.
  • Your manager can't look at you the day before.
  • Your system access is “being reviewed”.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Calm Witness

    In the meeting, listen, take notes, and ask for everything in writing. You don't have to respond or decide anything today.

    incantation“Thank you for telling me. Could I have all of this in writing, please? I'd like time to read it properly.”

  2. 2The Unsigned Scroll

    Don't sign anything on the day. Take the documents away. Check your rights with a lawyer, union or advice service.

    incantation“I'm not going to sign anything today. When do you need my response by?”

  3. 3The Question List

    Ask the questions that matter: notice, pay, benefits, pension, selection criteria, redeployment, references, timing.

    incantation“What are the selection criteria? Is there redeployment? What reference will the company give? When is my last day?”

  4. 4The Personal Archive

    Before access goes, make sure you have your personal things: your contacts, your own documents, your achievements list. Not company data.

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Scene

    Shouting, crying in anger, telling HR what you think of the CEO. Human. Understandable. Unhelpful for your reference.

  • The Data Heist

    Copying company files on the way out. It can cost you your severance and land you in legal trouble.

  • The Instant Signature

    Signing the settlement in the room because you want it over. You may be signing away more than you know.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Consultation: a fair process with time.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • You're “at risk”, not dismissed.
  • There's a consultation period.
  • Redeployment is offered.

Do

  1. Ask for everything in writing.
  2. Use the consultation: ask about criteria and alternatives.
  3. Apply for redeployment roles seriously.
  4. Get advice before agreeing anything.

Don’t: The scene. Your reference matters more than your exit line.

If it gets worse: If you're pushed to sign a settlement on the day, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Offer: pressure to sign quickly.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • A settlement is offered in the meeting.
  • You're told today's terms expire soon.
  • HR says it's “simplest for everyone”.

Do

  1. Say you won't sign today, and ask the deadline.
  2. Get independent legal advice.
  3. Use the Question List.
  4. Negotiate: notice, pay, reference wording, timing.

Don’t: The instant signature.

If it gets worse: If the process feels unfair or discriminatory, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Escort, or an unfair process.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • You're escorted out the same day.
  • The selection looks unfair or targeted.
  • You weren't consulted at all.

Do

  1. Stay calm and leave with dignity.
  2. Write down everything that happened, the same day.
  3. Get advice quickly; claims often have short time limits.
  4. Contact a union or advice service.

Don’t: The data heist. It can cost you your severance and your case.

If it gets worse: Look after yourself. Talk to people. This is a loss, and it's OK to treat it as one.

VIIIWould you be ready?

Nobody wants to read this entry. Tick honestly anyway.

  • You don't know your notice period or redundancy terms.
  • You don't know whether your company would pay for legal advice.
  • You'd sign whatever you were given to make it stop.
  • Your contacts live only in your work phone.
  • You've never written down your achievements.

The cure, if you ticked two: Spend one hour this month: find your contract, note your notice and terms, save your personal contacts, and write your achievements list. You'll probably never need it. If you do, you'll be glad.

IXThe rehearsal

The Calm Witness and the Unsigned Scroll.

Scene: Tuesday 3 June, 10:14, room 4B. Marcus puts the pen down.

KarenIt would be simplest for everyone if you signed today.

MarcusThank you for telling me. I'm not going to sign anything today. When do you need my response by?

Karen…The offer is open for two weeks.

MarcusCould I have all of this in writing, please? And the selection criteria, and details of any redeployment roles?

RupertOf course.

MarcusOne more question: what reference will MetroVolt give?

Marcus takes the envelope home. A lawyer, paid for by MetroVolt, gets him three more months' pay and an agreed reference. Pip writes him a LinkedIn recommendation the same week.

— the AnnotatorMarcus said almost nothing in the room. That was exactly right. Everything that mattered happened in the following two weeks.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

You're told your role is at risk. What do you say in the meeting?

Very little: thank them, ask for everything in writing, ask when they need a response, and leave.

You're handed a settlement agreement. Do you sign it?

Not today. Read it, get independent advice (often the company must pay for it in some countries), then decide.

What do you take with you?

Your personal contacts, your own documents and your achievements list. Never company data.

The Annotator's last wordThe meeting is fifteen minutes. The decisions you make in the next fifteen days matter far more. Don't make them in the room.
