The Reorg Ritual

The boxes move. The names change. The work stays exactly where it was.

Threat
Habitat
org-design workshops, new team names, the “mapping” spreadsheet
Weakness
knowing exactly what your work is, who needs it, and saying so early

IA sighting

Monday 12 July · the new structure

Horizon's new structure arrived in a 60-slide deck. MetroVolt would now be organised into four Value Streams, each containing Squads, supported by Chapters and coordinated by a Guild. Nobody at MetroVolt had ever used any of these words about electricity.

Wednesday 14 July · the mapping

A spreadsheet called Mapping_v4 listed every person and their new box. It listed people, not work. Pip's team was mapped to Customer Value Stream · Insight Squad. The regulator dashboard, which Pip's team produced, was mapped to nobody.

July · the Chronicler's notebook

The Chronicler added a page: Reorg #8. Previous names for the same work: Customer Analytics (2008), Insight Unit (2012), Customer Intelligence (2016), Ops Analytics (2021), Customer Analytics again (2024).

Wednesday 1 September · day one

Squads went live. The regulator dashboard was due on the 3rd. Nobody knew whose it was. Pip did, because he had sent a one-page Work Map in July. It was the only reason the dashboard went out.

— the AnnotatorReorgs move boxes. Make sure someone remembers what was inside them.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the ritual became eight reorgs

  1. 2008 · reorg #1

    A new CEO reorganises MetroVolt into functions. It makes sense. People stay in their seats; their boxes get new names.

  2. 2012 to 2021 · reorgs #2 to #6

    Each new leader brings a new structure. Each one moves the boxes. The work underneath barely changes; only the names do.

  3. 2024 · reorg #7

    Customer Analytics goes back to being called Customer Analytics. The Chronicler laughs for the first time in a year.

  4. 2027 · reorg #8

    Horizon brings Value Streams and Squads from a consultant's playbook. The people designing it have never seen the work they're mapping.

The turning point. Treating structure as the answer to every problem, so every new leader reached for a new chart.

What might have saved them. Starting each reorg from the work (what flows where, who depends on what) instead of from the boxes.

— the AnnotatorSeven reorgs in nineteen years, and the same work done by the same people each time. Structure matters less than it looks. Work maps matter more.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Reorgs change who reports to whom. They rarely change what the work is. The danger is in the gaps: work that doesn't fit a new box, dependencies nobody mapped, people who lose the manager who understood them. You can shape the gaps if you speak early, with a map of the work.

What reorg designers want

A clean structure, delivered on time, that meets the cost target. They don't know your work, and they're short of time.

Why work falls through

Because mapping spreadsheets list people, not outputs. Anything shared between teams, or done quietly by one person, is invisible to the designers.

What protects the work

  • A one-page Work Map sent early.
  • Someone from your team in the design sessions.
  • A clear landing in writing.
  • Naming orphaned work before day one.

Three kinds of reorg

The Rename

New names, same work. Learn the words; keep doing the job.

The Reshuffle

Boxes move, work splits. Map it; catch what falls.

The Cut in Disguise

A reorg that removes roles. Read the Famine entries for what comes next.

— the AnnotatorLearn the new words. Keep the old map. You'll need both.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • A new structure with new names: tribes, squads, streams, pods.
  • A “mapping” spreadsheet that lists people, not work.
  • Nobody can tell you who your manager will be.
  • Your work doesn't obviously fit any new box.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Work Map

    Write down what your team actually does, who depends on it, and what breaks if it stops. Send it to the people designing the new structure.

    incantation“Before the mapping is final: here's a one-page view of what our team does and who depends on it.”

  2. 2The Early Voice

    Ask to be part of the design for your area, early. The people in the room shape the boxes.

    incantation“Could someone from our team join the design session for Customer Value? We'd catch the gaps.”

  3. 3The Clear Landing

    Once it's decided, find out exactly who your manager is, what your role is, and what's expected, in writing.

    incantation“Just to be clear on the new structure: who do I report to, and what are my top three priorities from 1 September?”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Box Hunt

    Lobbying for a bigger box for yourself instead of a good home for the work. People notice.

  • The Old Map

    Carrying on as if the reorg didn't happen. In six months, nobody knows what you do.

  • The Name Mockery

    Laughing at “Customer Value Stream”. It's silly. Also, it's your new team.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

The Rename.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Your manager and work don't change.
  • Only names and slides change.
  • Nobody's role is at risk.

Do

  1. Learn the new language.
  2. Update your work map anyway.
  3. Help colleagues who are confused.

Don’t: The name mockery. It's your new team.

If it gets worse: If work is being split between new boxes, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

The Reshuffle: boxes move and work splits.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Mapping lists people, not work.
  • Some outputs have no owner in the new structure.
  • Your manager may change.

Do

  1. Send the Work Map to the designers early.
  2. Ask for an Early Voice in your area's design.
  3. Name orphaned work before day one.
  4. Get your Clear Landing in writing.

Don’t: The box hunt: lobbying for a bigger box instead of a good home for the work.

If it gets worse: If the reorg removes roles, you're at Hell.

Hell

The Cut in Disguise.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Fewer boxes than people.
  • “Selection” is mentioned.
  • Consultants run the mapping.

Do

  1. Read the Day of the Calendar Invite and know your rights.
  2. Make your value visible in numbers.
  3. Pack your go-bag.
  4. Support colleagues at risk.

Don’t: The old map: carrying on as if nothing happened.

If it gets worse: Follow the Famine entries in order: omens, go-bag, calendar invite.

VIIIDo you know what's inside your box?

Could you explain your team's work to a stranger designing your future? Tick honestly.

  • You couldn't list your team's outputs on one page.
  • You don't know who depends on your work.
  • You've never been involved in a reorg design.
  • You've lost work in a previous reorg without noticing.
  • You care more about the name than the work.

The cure, if you ticked two: Write your Work Map now: outputs, who uses them, what breaks if they stop. It's useful in every reorg, and in every interview.

IXThe rehearsal

The Work Map and the Early Voice.

Scene: Thursday 15 July. Pip emails the Horizon design team.

PipBefore Mapping_v4 is final: here's a one-page view of what Customer Analytics does and who depends on it. Item 3, the regulator dashboard, doesn't have an owner in the new structure.

OliverThanks. Which squad should own it?

PipInsight Squad, with a named backup in Network Assets. Could someone from our team join the Customer Value design session?

OliverThursday, 10:00. Twenty minutes.

Thursday: Pip shows the map. Two more orphaned outputs are found. Mapping_v5 includes all three.

— the AnnotatorThe designers weren't careless. They simply couldn't see the work. One page made it visible.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

A reorg is announced and nobody knows where your team will land. First move?

The Work Map: what you do, who depends on it, what breaks if it stops. Send it to the designers early.

The new structure is decided and it's worse for your work. What now?

Make it work, and record the risks calmly. Raise specific problems with numbers once it's live.

Is it worth fighting for your old team's name?

Rarely. Fight for the work and the people. The name was never the point.
