The Suits from Beyond

They arrive with lanyards and leave with your org chart.

Threat
Habitat
floor 27's meeting rooms, “day in the life” interviews, benchmark slides
Weakness
calm, factual, useful answers, and knowing what they're actually there to find

IA sighting

Monday 15 February · arrival

Six people from Vexmoor Consulting arrived on floor 27. They were polite, worked late and had remarkable shoes. Their leader, Oliver Thane, spoke a language made almost entirely of nouns: spans, layers, levers, baselines, opportunities.

Tuesday 30 March · the interview

Pip was booked for a “day in the life” interview: 45 minutes with a junior consultant called Bea, who typed everything he said into a spreadsheet with a column headed Value-add (Y/N).

March · the corridor

Colleagues compared notes afterwards. Some had used the interview to complain about other teams. Some had exaggerated their workload. One had refused to say anything at all. Morag had talked for 45 minutes about the 2009 blackout.

May · the benchmarks

Vexmoor's final deck compared every team to “peer utilities”. Most comparisons showed MetroVolt teams as too big. Pip's had a footnote: scope differs (regulator reporting). He had told Bea that. She had written it down.

— the AnnotatorConsultants leave. Their spreadsheets don't. Make sure yours says something true.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow Vexmoor came to MetroVolt

  1. 2012 · the founding

    Three partners leave a big consultancy and start Vexmoor, specialising in “operating model efficiency” for utilities. Their method: benchmark headcount against peers, find gaps, recommend cuts.

  2. 2019 · Rotterdam

    Vexmoor supports Rupert Thorne's Rotterdam turnaround. It's a success. They become the parent group's preferred cost-cutters.

  3. 2021 · the spreadsheet

    Vexmoor's “spans and layers” spreadsheet becomes famous inside the group. It counts managers, layers and people per team. It does not measure what the work is worth.

  4. 2027 · MetroVolt

    Oliver Thane, partner, leads Horizon's analysis. His team has eight weeks, a target and a spreadsheet. Whatever people tell them is all they'll ever know about MetroVolt.

The turning point. A method that counts people quickly but can't see value, applied by smart strangers on a deadline.

What might have saved them. Nothing about Vexmoor needs saving. What helps is people inside giving them true, useful, specific information, since that's all they have.

— the AnnotatorMost consultants aren't villains. They're clever outsiders with eight weeks. Whatever you tell them becomes the truth about your team.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Cost-cutting consultants are hired to find savings quickly. They have little time and no context, so they rely on benchmarks and interviews. What you say in their interviews becomes, for eight weeks, the official truth about your work.

What they want

Savings they can defend, fast. Also, quietly, to be right: consultants remember the people who helped them avoid mistakes.

Why they're dangerous

Because benchmarks compare headcount, not value. And because interviews capture complaints and exaggerations as data.

What helps you with them

  • A one-page description of your work's value.
  • Calm, factual answers.
  • Genuinely useful insights about real waste.
  • Polite questions about benchmarks.

Three kinds of consultant

The Analyst

Junior, overworked, typing. Make their spreadsheet accurate.

The Partner

Senior, persuasive, protective of the recommendation. Give them evidence, not opinions.

The Ally

Rare. Genuinely curious. Help them find the real waste.

— the AnnotatorGive the consultant something true and useful, and they'll quote you. Give them gossip, and they'll quote that too.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • Consultants with long-term badges on the executive floor.
  • Interviews titled “a day in the life” or “activity analysis”.
  • Benchmarks comparing your team's size to “peers”.
  • Phrases like “spans and layers” and “duplication”.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The Factual Witness

    In interviews, be calm, factual and specific. Describe your work, its value and its dependencies. Don't speculate about others.

    incantation“Our team's main output is the complaint model. It feeds the regulator report and cut repeat calls by 9%.”

  2. 2The Useful Insight

    Tell them something genuinely useful: a real inefficiency, a real risk. Consultants remember people who help them be right.

    incantation“The biggest waste I see isn't our team. It's three teams counting transformers differently.”

  3. 3The Benchmark Question

    If benchmarks are used against you, ask politely what the peer group is and what it includes.

    incantation“Could you share what the peer teams do? Our team also covers regulator reporting, which most don't.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Spill

    Using the interview to complain about colleagues. It goes in a spreadsheet. Spreadsheets travel.

  • The Inflation

    Exaggerating your workload to look indispensable. They'll compare your answer to your outputs.

  • The Stonewall

    Refusing to engage or giving nothing. They'll fill the gap with assumptions, and the assumptions will be worse.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

A genuine review with open questions.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Interviews are about processes.
  • Findings are shared with teams.
  • Nothing is pre-decided.

Do

  1. Prepare one page on your work.
  2. Be the Factual Witness.
  3. Offer a Useful Insight.

Don’t: The stonewall: refusing to engage.

If it gets worse: If benchmarks are used to justify cuts, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

A cost-cutting review with targets.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Benchmarks drive conclusions.
  • Interviews feel like audits.
  • Teams are compared by size.

Do

  1. Ask the Benchmark Question politely.
  2. Show your scope, so like is compared with like.
  3. Put your value in pounds.
  4. Brief your manager on what you told them.

Don’t: The inflation: exaggerating your workload.

If it gets worse: If the conclusions are decided before the interviews, you're at Hell.

Hell

The decision is made; the review is cover.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • The savings number existed before the review.
  • Findings are never shared.
  • Interviews are a formality.

Do

  1. Stay factual; your words may be quoted later.
  2. Focus on what you can shape: how cuts land.
  3. Keep your go-bag packed.
  4. Look after colleagues.

Don’t: The spill: complaining about colleagues. It'll be in a spreadsheet forever.

If it gets worse: Read the Budget Bloodletting and the Calendar Invite entries.

VIIIWhat would the spreadsheet say about you?

Imagine a stranger writing down your work in one row. Tick honestly.

  • You'd describe your work as busy rather than valuable.
  • You'd be tempted to criticise another team.
  • You don't know your work's value in pounds.
  • You'd exaggerate a little, just in case.
  • You'd say nothing, hoping to stay invisible.

The cure, if you ticked two: Write your one row now: what you produce, who uses it, what it's worth. Practise saying it in two sentences. It's your answer to every consultant, and every interview.

IXThe rehearsal

The Factual Witness and the Useful Insight.

Scene: Tuesday 30 March, 14:00. Pip's “day in the life” with Bea.

BeaCould you walk me through a typical week?

PipOur main output is the complaint model. It feeds the regulator report and cut repeat calls by 9% last year, about £410k. I've put it on one page for you.

BeaThank you. That's… unusually helpful.

PipOne more thing that might be useful: the biggest waste I've seen isn't in our team. Three teams count transformers differently. Fixing that would save real money.

BeaCan I quote you on that?

PipPlease. And one note for your benchmark: we do regulator reporting, which most peer teams don't.

May: Vexmoor's final deck recommends unifying asset data. It credits “an interviewee in Customer Ops”. Pip's team has a footnote.

— the AnnotatorPip gave the consultant two true, useful things. Both ended up in the final deck, one of them protecting his team.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

A consultant books you for a “day in the life” interview. How do you prepare?

One page: what you do, what it's worth, who depends on it. Calm and factual.

They ask what your colleagues do. What do you say?

Describe how your work connects with theirs. Don't judge their output; that's not your role here.

Their benchmark says your team is too big. What do you ask?

What's in the peer group, and what do those teams cover? Show what yours does that theirs don't.
