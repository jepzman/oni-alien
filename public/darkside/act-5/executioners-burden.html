The Executioner's Burden

When you're the one who has to tell them.

Threat
Habitat
consultation meetings, HR scripts, the 1:1 you've been dreading
Weakness
clarity, kindness, and saying the hard thing in the first minute

IA sighting

Friday 28 May · the decision

Horizon's final structure for Customer Ops had one fewer analyst role. Priya fought it and lost. Pip had to choose, using the published criteria, between Asha and Tomasz. The criteria pointed to Tomasz's role: it overlapped most with work moving to Field Ops.

Weekend · rehearsing

Pip wrote four versions of what to say. All of them started with twenty minutes about the weather. He threw them away.

Tuesday 1 June · 09:30 · the meeting

HR had given Pip a script. It said “as you know, the organisation is going through a period of change”. Pip noticed it took three paragraphs to reach the news.

09:31

Pip put the script down and said it in the first minute. Tomasz went very still. Then he asked about his children's school fees. Pip didn't know the answer, said so, and wrote the question down. The meeting took forty minutes. Most of it was silence. Pip let it be silence.

— the AnnotatorYou can't make it painless. You can make it clear, kind and fast. That's the job.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow managers learn to deliver bad news (they don't)

  1. 2017 · Gareth's first time

    Gareth, newly promoted, has to tell six engineers their roles are going. He's given a script and no training. He reads it, word for word, to each of them. Two cry. One never speaks to him again.

  2. 2017 · the lesson nobody shared

    Gareth learns, badly, what works: say it early, stop talking, help practically. He never tells anyone. Nobody asks.

  3. 2018 to 2026 · the scripts

    Every manager facing cuts gets the same script and no training. Each learns alone, usually by getting it wrong the first time.

  4. 2027 · Pip

    Pip, five weeks into his first management role, is handed the script. He has read this book. That's the only training he gets.

The turning point. Scripts handed out instead of skills taught, so every manager's first time is someone else's worst day.

What might have saved them. One afternoon of training. Or Gareth, in 2017, writing down what he learned and passing it on.

— the AnnotatorIf you've had to do this, write down what you learned and give it to the next manager. It's the most useful thing nobody will ask you for.

IIIKnow thy enemy

Delivering redundancy news is one of the hardest things a manager does. The person will remember the conversation for years: not the words, but whether they were treated with clarity and respect. The essentials are simple: say it early, stop talking, help practically, don't promise what you can't control.

What the person needs

To hear it clearly, to be given time to react, to know what happens next, and to be treated as a person, not a line in Horizon.

Why managers get it wrong

Because they're frightened, so they delay. Because they want to be liked, so they promise. Because they feel guilty, so they talk about themselves.

What makes it bearable

  • The news in the first minute.
  • Silence, allowed.
  • Facts in writing and next steps.
  • Real help: references, introductions, time.

Three kinds of executioner

The Reader

Reads the script and hides behind it. Feels safe; lands cold.

The Apologiser

Makes it about their own pain. Feels kind; lands wrong.

The Steady

Clear, quiet, practical. Feels hard; lands with dignity.

— the AnnotatorSay the sentence you're dreading first. Everything after it is easier, for both of you.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • HR sends you a script and a slot.
  • You know something your team doesn't, and they're asking.
  • You're lying awake rehearsing.
  • You've been told not to tell them “yet”.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The First Minute

    Say the news in the first minute, plainly. Don't make them sit through small talk while you work up to it.

    incantation“Tomasz, I've got difficult news, and I want to say it straight. Your role is at risk of redundancy.”

  2. 2The Steady Presence

    After saying it, stop. Let them react. Don't fill the silence with reassurance you can't back up.

  3. 3The Practical Help

    Give them the facts in writing, the next steps, and everything you can genuinely do: references, introductions, time off for interviews.

    incantation“I'll write you a strong reference, and I'll introduce you to Field Ops; they're hiring. Take whatever time you need for interviews.”

  4. 4The Honest Limits

    Be honest about what you can't change. Never promise what you don't control.

    incantation“I argued for your role and lost. I can't change the decision. I can help with what comes next.”

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Long Lead-in

    Twenty minutes of chat before the news. They'll know from your face; the delay is cruel.

  • The Self-Pity

    Talking about how hard this is for you. It is. It's harder for them. Not the moment.

  • The False Hope

    “I'm sure something will come up.” If it doesn't, you've made it worse.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

A fair process, and a person who may find another role.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • There's consultation and redeployment.
  • The criteria are clear.
  • You can help practically.

Do

  1. Say it in the first minute.
  2. Let them react.
  3. Give facts in writing and next steps.
  4. Offer references and introductions.

Don’t: The long lead-in.

If it gets worse: If you disagree with the decision, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

You disagree with the decision you must deliver.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • You argued and lost.
  • The criteria aren't how you'd choose.
  • You like the person.

Do

  1. Be honest that you argued and lost, without disowning the process.
  2. Don't suggest unfairness unless you'll say so formally.
  3. Focus on what you can do now.
  4. Get support for yourself afterwards.

Don’t: False hope: “I'm sure something will come up.”

If it gets worse: If the process itself seems unfair or unlawful, you're at Hell.

Hell

The process seems unfair, or you're told to do it badly.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Selection looks targeted.
  • You're told to deliver it with no notice or support.
  • HR wants signatures on the day.

Do

  1. Raise your concerns in writing before the meeting.
  2. Refuse to pressure anyone to sign on the day.
  3. Tell the person they can get advice.
  4. Take care of your own conscience; get advice yourself if needed.

Don’t: The self-pity: making it about how hard this is for you.

If it gets worse: If you're asked to do something wrong, read Act VIII, the Dark Pact.

VIIIHow would you deliver it?

If you lead anyone, you may have to one day. Tick honestly.

  • You'd start with small talk to soften it.
  • You'd read the script so you didn't have to find your own words.
  • You'd fill the silence with reassurance.
  • You'd say “this is really hard for me too”.
  • You've never thought about what you'd offer practically.

The cure, if you ticked two: Write the first sentence now, in your own words, and what you'd offer afterwards. Then hope you never need it.

IXThe rehearsal

The First Minute, the Steady Presence, the Practical Help, the Honest Limits.

Scene: Tuesday 1 June, 09:30. Pip, Tomasz, and HR.

PipTomasz, I've got difficult news, and I want to say it straight. Your role is at risk of redundancy as part of Horizon.

Silence. Pip waits. He does not fill it.

Tomasz…Why mine?

PipThe criteria looked at overlap with work moving to Field Ops. Your role overlapped most. I argued for both roles and lost. I can't change the decision.

TomaszWhat about my kids' school fees? We've just signed for September.

PipI don't know the answer, and I won't guess. I'll find out today. What I can do: Field Ops are hiring, I'll introduce you to Sam this week, and I'll write you the strongest reference I've ever written.

Tomasz is redeployed to Field Ops in July. He sends Pip a message in December: “Thank you for saying it straight.”

— the AnnotatorPip didn't make it painless. He made it clear, honest and practical. Tomasz remembered exactly that.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

You must tell a team member their role is at risk. How do you open?

With the news, in the first minute, plainly and kindly.

They ask “why me?” and you don't fully agree with the decision. What do you say?

Explain the criteria honestly. Don't disown the decision in a way that suggests it's unfair unless you'll stand behind that formally.

What can you do for them after the meeting?

References, introductions, time for interviews, and staying in touch. People remember who helped when it was hard.

The Annotator's last wordYou can't make it painless. You can make it clear, kind and fast. That's the job.
