The Exit Interview Trap

On your way out, a friendly voice asks you to tell them everything. It's a trap only if you do.

Threat
Habitat
leaving dates, HR forms, “one last chat”, the temptation to finally say it
Weakness
one constructive point, said calmly, and saving the rest for friends

IA sighting

October 2030 · the offer

Rafi was offered a role at Kestrel, a small energy-data start-up. Better pay, smaller team, no hot-desks. He accepted, gave a month's notice, and handed over beautifully. His last day was 28 November.

The list

Rafi had a list for the exit interview. Twenty-three items. Rupert's pact. The three roadmaps. The hot-desks. The badge reports. The banner. The time VoltMind got the heatwave wrong and someone senior blamed the analysts. The list was true. It was also, the Annotator noted, a crater, not a door.

Monday 24 November · the interview

Rafi said one useful thing: that the team had rebuilt its roadmap three times in a year, and that stable priorities would help those who stayed more than anything. He thanked Ruth and Pip specifically: for the heatwave write-up, the Data Office role, and the Salt Circle. Asked “anything else?”, he said: “No, thank you. I've learned a lot here.”

Friday 28 November · the friend's ear

That evening, at the pub, Rafi read all twenty-three items out loud to Pip and Asha. They laughed, agreed with most and argued about two. In March 2031, MetroVolt's planning guidance included a new principle: “priorities stable for at least two quarters”, citing exit-interview themes. Kestrel, it turned out, works with GridSight. Rafi is now Asha's client.

— the AnnotatorLeave a door, not a crater. Say one useful thing, thank them, and save the rest for friends.

IIOrigin

flashbackHow the exit interview became a trap

  1. The intention

    Exit interviews exist so companies can learn why people leave. It's a good idea.

  2. The reality

    The answers go into a file. Patterns are sometimes acted on. Individual complaints rarely are.

  3. The temptation

    Leavers feel free at last to say everything. It feels like justice. It mostly becomes a reputation.

  4. The small world

    Leavers come back, become clients, need references. Rafi became Asha's client within a year.

The turning point. A useful listening tool being mistaken for a place to settle scores.

What might have saved them. Knowing, before the interview, what one point you want to make.

— the AnnotatorExit interviews change things through patterns, not single complaints. Add one clear point to the pattern.

IIIKnow thy enemy

An exit interview is a conversation (or form) when you leave, asking why you're going and what could be better. Companies use them to spot patterns. They're rarely fully confidential, rarely acted on individually, and sometimes read by people who'll later give or receive references about you. Used well, they let you leave one useful thing behind.

What it's for

Spotting patterns in why people leave. Sometimes, ticking a box.

Why it's a trap

Because you feel free to say everything, and “everything” is rarely useful and often remembered.

What to say

  • One specific, constructive point.
  • Specific thanks.
  • Serious concerns through the proper route, before you go.
  • Nothing you'd mind being quoted.

Three kinds of leaver

The Graceful Leaver

One point, specific thanks. Remembered well.

The Crater Leaver

Everything, angrily. Remembered for that.

The Silent Leaver

Nothing at all. Safe, but leaves nothing for those who stay.

— the AnnotatorIf you're leaving because of harassment or discrimination, raise it formally before you leave, with advice, not just in the exit interview.

IVSigns and omens

Tick two or more and you are in its lair.

  • You're leaving, and you have a list.
  • You've been saving it up for months.
  • The interviewer says it's “completely confidential”.
  • You'll still need references.

VCounter-spells

  1. 1The One Useful Thing

    Pick one specific, constructive point that would help those who stay. Say it calmly.

    incantation“The thing that would help most is fewer priority changes. We rebuilt our roadmap three times in a year.”

  2. 2The Gracious Close

    Thank them for the good things, specifically. You'll want those references and those people again.

  3. 3The Friend's Ear

    Save the full, angry list for a friend over a drink. It belongs there.

VIForbidden spells

Tempting. Effective for about a day. Each one has a soul cost = one ember of your soul.

  • The Scorched Earth

    Telling them everything that's wrong with everyone. It's filed, remembered, and rarely acted on.

  • Naming Names Angrily

    Personal attacks in the exit interview. If there's a real complaint, raise it properly before you leave.

  • The Pointless Lie

    Saying everything was perfect. One honest, useful point helps the people you're leaving.

VIIDifficulty

The same creature at three strengths. Pick the one you're facing.

Normal

A happy-enough leaving.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • You're leaving for something better.
  • Few grievances.
  • Good relationships.

Do

  1. Make one useful point.
  2. Thank people specifically.
  3. Hand over well.

Don’t: The pointless lie.

If it gets worse: If you're leaving because of problems, you're at Nightmare.

Nightmare

Leaving with a list.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Many grievances.
  • Some are serious, most aren't.
  • You're tempted to say it all.

Do

  1. Choose the one most useful point.
  2. Write the full list for a friend.
  3. Stay calm and factual.
  4. Keep your references in mind.

Don’t: The scorched earth.

If it gets worse: If something serious happened, you're at Hell.

Hell

Leaving because of something serious.

You’ll know it’s this level when

  • Harassment, discrimination or wrongdoing.
  • You're leaving because of it.
  • Others may still be affected.

Do

  1. Raise it formally before you leave.
  2. Get independent advice.
  3. Keep records.
  4. Use the exit interview to confirm it was raised.

Don’t: Naming names angrily.

If it gets worse: See HR's True Allegiance and the Whistle of Last Resort.

VIIIWill you leave a door or a crater?

Tick honestly.

  • You have a mental list of everything you'll say when you leave.
  • You've imagined the exit interview as revenge.
  • You haven't raised serious problems because “I'll say it when I go”.
  • You don't know who'd give you a reference.
  • You've never thanked your manager specifically.

The cure, if you ticked two: Raise the serious things now, properly. Save the exit interview for one useful point and a real thank-you.

IXThe rehearsal

The One Useful Thing, and the Gracious Close.

Scene: Monday 24 November 2030, Rafi's exit interview.

HRWhy are you leaving, and what could we do better?

RafiA great opportunity. And one thing: we rebuilt our roadmap three times in a year. Stable priorities would help everyone who stays.

HRThat's useful. Thank you.

RafiAnd I'd like to thank Ruth and Pip specifically. The heatwave write-up and the Data Office role changed my career.

HRAnything else?

RafiNo, thank you. I've learned a lot here.

— the AnnotatorItem 24 on the list, added later at the pub: “The biscuits at Thursday procurement meetings are excellent.” This is not about MetroVolt.

XThe trial

What would you do? Tap a question for the answer.

You're leaving because of a bad manager. Do you say so?

If it's serious (harassment, discrimination), raise it formally. Otherwise, one calm, factual point at most.

Is the exit interview confidential?

Usually partly. Assume what you say may be read by people who'll be asked for your reference.

What's the most useful thing to say?

One specific, constructive point that would help those who stay.

The Annotator's last wordLeave a door, not a crater. Say one useful thing, thank them, and save the rest for friends.
