B2B vs B2C Products

TL;DR

Business customers (B2B) and consumers (B2C) can use the same product and still be different customers. In B2B the buyer, user, payer and sponsor are different people; there are contracts and service levels; payment is by invoice; and customers leave at renewal, not quietly. Map who's who, keep a shared core for what's truly the same, and give each a front door that fits how they buy.

1The situation Larder, 5 July 2027

Larder for Business now has Korenwolf and three other office clients, ordering through the portal and the Business API. Grace keeps treating them like households with bigger baskets, and it keeps going wrong: invoices need cost centres, the buyer isn't the user, and a missing item is a contract breach, not a refund.

  • B2B has several people per customer, each wanting something different.
  • Contracts turn quality into promises with penalties.
  • Business customers leave at renewal, after warnings you can miss.

2Real-life analogy the bakery and the hotel

A bakery sells to walk-in customers and to a hotel. The walk-in customer chooses, pays and eats. At the hotel, the chef chooses, purchasing signs a contract, accounts pay monthly, and guests eat. Same bread, same ovens, a different business, and a different conversation when something goes wrong.

3How it solves it who's who, shared core, two front doors

Grace compared the two. Households: one person decides, uses and pays; a missing item means a refund; they leave quietly. Offices: the office manager uses it, procurement buys it, finance pays monthly, a sponsor cares about complaints; a missing item breaks a service level; they leave at renewal. Shared: catalogue, stock, picking, substitutions, delivery. Different: accounts and roles, contracts, invoices, ordering ahead. Her rule: one ordering core, two front doors.

  1. Map the roles

    Buyer, user, payer, sponsor, approver.

  2. Write what each wants

    One line per role.

  3. Name the promises

    Contracts and service levels.

  4. Separate core from front door

    What's truly shared, what isn't.

  5. Measure each side

    Retention for households; renewal health for offices.

  6. Watch the renewal calendar

    Business churn has a date.

Shared core: catalogue · stock · picking · substitutions · delivery
Front doors: app + web shop · business portal + Business API (ADR 0019)

tools: a roles map per business client, the strategy page, separate measures for each customer type.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Fewer wrong assumptions about offices.
  • The right people served in each account.
  • A shared core, not two products.
  • Renewal risks seen early.
  • Clear measures per customer type.

What we lose

  • Two front doors to build and keep.
  • More roles and permissions.
  • Contracts limit flexibility.
  • Tension over what's shared.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Bigger baskets

    Offices as large households. Fix → map the roles.

  • Two products

    Separate stock and picking. Fix → one shared core.

  • One measure

    Perfect orders for offices too. Fix → SLA and renewal health.

  • Forgetting the buyer

    Users love it; procurement doesn't renew. Fix → serve every role.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Products sold to both businesses and consumers.
  • Adding a business line to a consumer product.
  • Before designing business features.
  • When business customers keep being surprised.

Skip it when…

  • Purely consumer or purely business products.
