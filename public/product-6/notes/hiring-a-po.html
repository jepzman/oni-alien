Hiring a Product Owner

TL;DR

Hiring a product owner by CV and conversation tests how well people talk about product work. Instead: write a scorecard of the few qualities the role needs, give a work sample that looks like the real job, let the people they'll work with interview, include a real customer conversation if you can, and decide against the scorecard. Treat candidates well: their time is real, too.

  • Origin: structured hiring and work-sample tests (Schmidt and Hunter, 1998)

1The situation Larder, 22 November 2027

Larder for Business needs its own product owner. Grace's first instinct is the usual process: a copied job ad, a chat about frameworks, a case study about a famous app. Last year, a hire made that way talked well and struggled with real customers.

  • Conversations test talking, not doing.
  • Generic cases miss the role's real difficulties.
  • Without a scorecard, the most likeable candidate wins.

2Real-life analogy the trial shift

A good restaurant doesn't hire a chef after a chat about cooking theory. It offers a trial shift: two dishes from the real menu, in the real kitchen, with the real team, then tasting together and talking about what they'd change. The kitchen learns more in two hours than in a week of interviews.

3How it solves it scorecard, work sample, colleagues, a customer

Grace wrote a scorecard: judgment with customers, clear writing, comfort with numbers, working with sales without becoming sales. The work sample: ten anonymised requests like Korenwolf's to sort in two hours, with a one-page note. Interviews with Mo, Ben and Noor. One observed conversation with a real office manager. The decision followed the scorecard. Hired: Jonas, from a business-software company, starting 3 January 2028.

  1. Write the scorecard

    Three to five qualities the role really needs.

  2. Design a work sample

    A slice of the real job, two hours, paid if longer.

  3. Involve future colleagues

    Each tests different qualities.

  4. Add a real customer moment

    Observed, if possible.

  5. Decide against the scorecard

    Together, with evidence.

  6. Treat candidates well

    Clear steps, quick answers, feedback.

# Hiring: product owner, Larder for Business · Grace · from 22 Nov 2027

Scorecard   1 judgment with business customers (needs vs requests)
            2 clear writing (one page, decisions with reasons)
            3 comfort with numbers (SLA %, cost per order, renewal health)
            4 works with sales without becoming sales
Steps       30-min call (Grace) → work sample (2 h, 10 anonymised requests + 1-page note)
            → interviews: Mo (4), Ben (1, 3), Noor (1, 2) → observed call with an office manager
Decision    scored per quality by each interviewer, discussed together · answer within 3 days
Hired       Jonas (from a business-software company) · starts 3 Jan 2028 · handover plan ready

tools: a scorecard, a realistic work sample, a structured interview plan, a debrief meeting.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Hiring for the job, not the talk.
  • Colleagues invested in the hire.
  • Fairer decisions.
  • Candidates see the real job.
  • A better start: the work sample shows gaps early.

What we lose

  • More preparation.
  • Candidates' time: keep it short.
  • Some good people decline work samples.
  • Longer process.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Framework quizzes

    “Explain RICE.” Fix → a real slice of the job.

  • No scorecard

    The likeable one wins. Fix → qualities written first.

  • Only the manager

    Colleagues meet them on day one. Fix → future colleagues interview.

  • Long unpaid tasks

    A weekend of work. Fix → two hours, or paid.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Hiring any product role.
  • When past hires talked well and struggled.
  • Splitting a product area.
  • Building a team's hiring habits.

Skip it when…

  • Never; only the size of the process changes.
