Pricing Experiments

TL;DR

Prices are among the most powerful and least tested product decisions. Start with research (what range do customers find acceptable?), then test carefully: on new customers, for a fixed period, with legal agreement and a fair treatment of anyone who paid more. Measure behaviour (sign-ups, revenue per visitor, retention, frequency), not stated intent, and choose the price on the whole picture.

1The situation Larder, 14 June 2027

Larder Plus, free delivery for a monthly fee, launches this month. Femke picked €7.99 because a competitor charges that. Marco worries it's too cheap to pay for itself; Priya worries it's too dear to sell. Nobody has asked customers, and a subscription price is hard to change once people have signed up.

  • Copied prices fit someone else's costs and customers.
  • Opinions about price are loud and untested.
  • Subscription prices stick.

2Real-life analogy the strawberry stall

A market trader setting the price of strawberries asks regulars what would feel too cheap or too dear. Then she tries two prices on two mornings and counts punnets sold and takings, not compliments. The answer is in the till.

3How it solves it ask, test fairly, measure behaviour

Grace ran a price sensitivity survey with 1,100 customers: acceptable range about €5–€9. Then, agreed with Lena and Marco, a four-week test on new sign-ups only: €6.99 vs €7.99, first month free in both, a rule that nobody ends up paying more than the winner. Sign-up: 3.4 % vs 3.1 %; revenue per visitor higher at €7.99; order frequency the same. Decision: €7.99 with the first month free. By 1 September: 5,400 members, ordering 34 % more often than similar customers.

  1. Research the range

    Survey: too cheap, cheap, expensive, too expensive.

  2. Agree the test with legal and finance

    Who sees which price, and how it's made fair.

  3. Test on new customers

    Never change existing customers' prices in a test.

  4. Run a fixed period

    Long enough for real decisions.

  5. Measure behaviour

    Sign-ups, revenue per visitor, retention, frequency.

  6. Decide on the whole picture

    And treat test customers fairly afterwards.

Vocabulary for the exam

Price sensitivity
How demand changes with price.
Van Westendorp
A survey method asking four price questions.
Revenue per visitor
Total revenue divided by visitors: combines price and conversion.
Willingness to pay
The most a customer would pay.
Price anchoring
Judging a price against a reference.
Grandfathering
Keeping existing customers on an old price.

# Larder Plus pricing test · 14 Jun – 12 Jul 2027 · owner: Grace · agreed: Lena, Marco, Femke

Research    Van Westendorp survey, 1,100 customers: acceptable range ≈ €5.00 – €9.00
Test        new sign-ups only, randomised: A €6.99 / B €7.99 · first month free in both
Fairness    if A wins, B members pay A's price from month 2; if B wins, A keeps €6.99 for 12 months
Measure     sign-up rate · revenue per visitor (12 months, est.) · orders per member per month
Guardrail   delivery cost per Plus order ≤ €4.20
Result      sign-up A 3.4 % · B 3.1 % · revenue per visitor B +6 % · frequency equal
Decision    €7.99, first month free (A's 610 members keep €6.99 for 12 months)
1 Sep       5,400 members · +34 % order frequency vs similar non-members

tools: a price sensitivity survey, the experiment platform with randomisation, revenue and retention reports, legal review.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Prices on evidence, not copying.
  • Marco and Priya answered with data.
  • Fairness built into the test.
  • Revenue per visitor as the decider.
  • A price that stuck.

What we lose

  • Legal and reputational care needed.
  • Weeks of testing before deciding.
  • Survey answers are only a start.
  • Long-term effects take months to see.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Copying a competitor

    Their price, your costs. Fix → research your customers.

  • Testing on existing customers

    Different prices for the same member. Fix → new sign-ups only.

  • Measuring sign-ups only

    Cheapest always wins. Fix → revenue per visitor and retention.

  • Unfair afterwards

    Test members overpay. Fix → a fairness rule agreed in advance.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • New products, subscriptions and plans.
  • Price changes.
  • Delivery fees and thresholds.
  • When leaders disagree about price.

Skip it when…

  • Prices fixed by contract or regulation.

8Self-check quiz

Why test on new sign-ups only?

Existing customers shouldn't see their price change in a test.

What decided the price?

Revenue per visitor, not sign-up rate alone.

What was the fairness rule?

Test members never end up paying more than the winning price (A keeps €6.99 for a year).

What happened by September?

5,400 members, ordering 34 % more often.

try it

Pick a price in your product. Ask twenty customers the four price questions, and sketch a test on new customers only: the two prices, the measure (revenue per visitor), the fairness rule, and who must agree.

remember!!

Ask the regulars, then count the takings.
