Accessibility as Product Quality

TL;DR

Accessibility means people with disabilities can use the product as well as anyone. Treat it as product quality, not a one-off project: fix the worst barriers first, test with real disabled users, and build it into the definition of done, automated checks and release tests. The law (in the EU, the European Accessibility Act and WCAG-based standards) is the minimum; the gains usually help everyone.

1The situation Larder, 16 August 2027

A customer who uses a screen reader writes to Sam: the swap screen reads the countdown aloud every second, and the three-minute window runs out before she can answer. An audit finds 41 issues. The European Accessibility Act has applied to online shops since June 2025; Larder has been out of line for two years without noticing.

  • Teams don't notice barriers they don't experience.
  • Time limits and visual-only cues exclude people.
  • Fixing late costs more than building in.

2Real-life analogy the ramp at the front door

A building with a ramp, wide doors and a lift designed in from the start is easier for wheelchair users, and also for parents with pushchairs, delivery workers and anyone with a sprained ankle. Adding them later costs far more, and looks it.

3How it solves it worst first, real users, part of done

Grace fixed the worst first: the swap timer now announces only at one minute and at the end, and customers can ask for more time (WCAG's timing rule). Noor and the team worked through the other 40 issues by priority and tested with five customers with disabilities, paid for their time. Accessibility joined the definition of done, with automated checks in CI and a screen-reader test before each release. By November all 41 were fixed; “more time” is used by 6 % of all customers.

  1. Audit against WCAG

    With an expert and automated tools.

  2. Fix the worst barriers first

    Ones that block a task completely.

  3. Test with disabled users

    Paid, regularly, on real tasks.

  4. Add it to the definition of done

    Every story, every release.

  5. Automate what you can

    Checks in CI; manual tests for the rest.

  6. Publish an accessibility statement

    And a way to report problems.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Everyone can order.
  • Better for all: 6 % use “more time”.
  • Legal, at last.
  • Quality built in, not bolted on.
  • Real users find real problems.

What we lose

  • Audit and fixes take sprints.
  • Testing with users costs time and money.
  • Design constraints on new features.
  • Ongoing checks per release.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A one-off project

    Fixed once, broken again. Fix → part of the definition of done.

  • Automated checks only

    Tools miss most barriers. Fix → test with real users.

  • Compliance only

    The minimum, grudgingly. Fix → quality for everyone.

  • Fixing the easy first

    Blockers wait. Fix → worst barriers first.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every customer-facing product.
  • Before new markets (rules differ).
  • Designing time limits, colours, gestures.
  • When complaints mention barriers.

Skip it when…

  • Never.

8Self-check quiz

What was the worst barrier?

The swap countdown read aloud every second, with no way to get more time.

How was it fixed?

Fewer announcements and a “more time” option.

How is accessibility kept?

In the definition of done, CI checks and release tests.

Who used “more time”?

6 % of all customers, not only screen-reader users.
