Product Sense

TL;DR

Product sense is the judgment that notices what matters before the data does: what feels wrong to a customer, what's missing, what would delight. It isn't a gift; it's trained: use your product as a customer, work shifts beside the people who run it, study other products, read raw signals (tickets, interviews), ask why behind numbers, and write predictions before tests so you learn where your judgment is good.

  • Family: growing yourself
  • Origin: product practice (e.g. Shreyas Doshi, Julie Zhuo)
  • Lives in: weekly habits, written down
  • Pairs with: customer interviews, support tickets as data, feedback loops with operations

1The situation Larder, 25 October 2027

A week after Antwerp opened, the dashboards say all is fine. Grace orders from Antwerp herself on Friday, as she does in every city, and notices what no number shows: the French swap message arrives as a cold one-liner, and the courier card uses a Dutch spelling. Noor asks: how do you notice these things?

  • Dashboards show what was measured, not what feels wrong.
  • Product people can drift away from the product.
  • Judgment that isn't trained or checked stays vague.

2Real-life analogy the chef's palate

A chef's palate isn't a gift. It's trained by tasting everything, every day: their own dishes as a guest would eat them, the competitors' food, ingredients on their own. Over years they taste the missing salt before anyone complains.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the product

3How it solves it habits, written down and checked

Grace wrote down her habits for Noor and the new product owner. Use it: an order every week, in a different city. Work a shift: picking at a store and an hour on support, monthly. Study others: a competitor teardown a month with the trio. Raw signals: ten tickets and two interview notes a week. Ask why behind every number. Predict before tests, then check: her hit rate is about 60 %, which is why she still tests. The Antwerp message was rewritten by Monday.

  1. Use the product as a customer

    Every week, the real way.

  2. Work beside operations

    A shift a month.

  3. Study other products

    A teardown with the team.

  4. Read raw signals

    Tickets, interviews, store feedback.

  5. Ask why behind numbers

    Then go and look.

  6. Predict, then check

    Write it down before every test.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Problems seen before the data.
  • Better ideas from first-hand experience.
  • Calibrated judgment: know when to trust it.
  • Teachable to others.
  • Closer to customers and operations.

What we lose

  • Hours every week.
  • One person's experience isn't data.
  • Overconfidence without checking.
  • Hard to measure.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Only dashboards

    Never using the product. Fix → order every week.

  • Anecdote as data

    “I saw it once.” Fix → check with data before big changes.

  • Never checking

    Always “right”. Fix → a prediction log.

  • Only your own product

    No outside view. Fix → teardowns of others.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every product person, always.
  • After launches in new places.
  • Training new product people.
  • When the team seems far from customers.

Skip it when…

  • Never.

8Self-check quiz

Is product sense a gift?

No: it's trained by habits.

What did Grace's own order find?

A cold French swap message in Antwerp.

Why write predictions down?

To learn how often your judgment is right (about 60 % for Grace).

Name three habits.

Order weekly, work a shift monthly, read ten tickets a week.

try it

This week, order (or use) your product as a real customer, read ten support tickets, and write one prediction before your next test. Note one thing you noticed that no dashboard shows.

remember!!

Train the palate: taste every day, as a guest would.
