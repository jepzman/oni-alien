Writing Product Principles

TL;DR

Product principles are a handful of beliefs about how your product treats customers and makes trade-offs, each written so it costs something (“even if…”). They let a growing team decide the way you would without you in the room, and they make disagreements faster to settle. Write them from real decisions, with the team, and use them out loud in refinement and reviews.

  • Family: scaling the product
  • Origin: product practice (e.g. Julie Zhuo, Marty Cagan); design principles
  • Lives in: one page, quoted in refinement, reviews and PRDs
  • Pairs with: strategy on a page, architecture principles (Ana), one-page PRD

1The situation Larder, 11 October 2027

Grace's area now has three product teams, a designer, and soon a second product owner. Decisions she used to make in a conversation are now made in rooms she isn't in, and come out different: a swap that defaults without asking, a Plus banner on the checkout page, a business feature only Korenwolf can use. The team can't read Grace's mind.

  • Growing teams can't all ask the product owner.
  • Strategy says where; principles say how.
  • Unwritten values drift.

2Real-life analogy the newspaper's style book

A good newspaper has a style book: a few rules with examples (name your sources, short sentences, correct mistakes prominently). Every journalist writes like the paper without asking the editor, and when two disagree, the style book settles it.

3How it solves it a few beliefs, each with its cost

Grace wrote six principles with the teams, from real decisions, each with what it costs: ask, don't assume (even if slower); honest over clever (no dark patterns, even if conversion drops); every order complete or explained; one product for homes and offices (no single-client features); fast for pickers is fast for customers; less, but better (we remove features). They're quoted in refinement and reviews; the checkout banner was removed citing principle two.

  1. Collect hard decisions

    Where you chose one good thing over another.

  2. Write the belief behind each

    One line.

  3. Add the cost

    “Even if…”: what you give up.

  4. Write them with the team

    So they're shared, not handed down.

  5. Keep five to eight

    Memorable, on one page.

  6. Use them out loud

    In refinement, reviews, PRDs.

# Ordering & apps · product principles · written with the teams, 11 Oct 2027

1  Ask, don't assume.            Customers decide swaps, even if it's slower.
2  Honest over clever.           No dark patterns, even if it costs conversion.
3  Every order complete or explained.  If we can't deliver it, we say so before the door.
4  One product for homes and offices.   No single-client features; settings for all.
5  Fast for pickers is fast for customers.  Picker time counts in every design.
6  Less, but better.             We remove what doesn't earn its place (slot waitlist, 2026).

Used   refinement: "which principle does this touch?" · reviews: cite by number
First use   Plus banner on checkout removed (#2), 13 Oct

tools: one page, the team's real decision history, refinement and review templates that ask which principle applies.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Decisions without Grace in the room.
  • Faster disagreements.
  • Consistent product across teams.
  • New people learn the product's values.
  • Trade-offs explicit.

What we lose

  • Principles can be ignored if not used.
  • Vague ones are useless.
  • Some conversion given up on purpose.
  • Need revisiting as the product grows.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Platitudes

    “Customer first.” Fix → a cost: “even if…”.

  • Too many

    Fifteen principles. Fix → five to eight.

  • Handed down

    Written alone. Fix → written with the team.

  • On a wall only

    Never quoted. Fix → use them in every review.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Growing product teams.
  • Before handing over part of the product.
  • When teams make inconsistent choices.
  • Onboarding new product people.

Skip it when…

  • A single small team that decides together daily.
