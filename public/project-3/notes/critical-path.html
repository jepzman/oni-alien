What Decides the Finish Date?

TL;DR

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent activities through a network. It decides the finish date: if any activity on it is late, the end is late by the same amount. Activities off it have float and can slip a little without harm. Find it with two passes, forward (earliest start and finish) and backward (latest), and watch it every week. Speeding up an activity that isn't on it buys nothing.

  • Family: by when?
  • Lives in: the network, with the critical chain marked in red
  • Pairs with: float and buffers, crashing, escalation

1The situation Larder, 20 February and 23 March 2026

With the network on the wall, everyone has a favourite worry. Joost: the plumber, who is slow. Tomás: the shelving, because Zuid's was late. Marco: the cold room, because it's the most expensive thing. Twelve activities, twelve worries, and only so much attention. Sanne asks a different question: of all of these, which ones actually decide the handover date?

  • Worry goes to what is slow, dear or familiar.
  • The date is decided by something else: the longest chain.
  • Effort spent on the wrong activity buys nothing.
meanwhile, in Ana’s notebook
  • Last Responsible Moment (Ana, vol. 2): Ana's last responsible moment asks how late a decision can be taken without costing anything.

2Real-life analogy the picnic

Three groups walk to a picnic by different paths that meet at the same spot. The picnic starts when the slowest group arrives. Making a fast group walk faster changes nothing; they just wait longer at the blanket. If you want lunch earlier, there's only one path worth hurrying, and you'd better know which one it is.

3How it solves it two passes, then watch the red chain

Sanne does the two passes by hand with Kees on 20 February (the chapter below shows them). The forward pass gives every activity its earliest start and finish; the last finish is day 25: Friday 3 April. The backward pass, from day 25, gives the latest each activity can start without moving it. Where earliest and latest are equal, there's no float: that's the critical path, A-B-E-F-G-H-K-L. The electrical inspection is on it. Joost's plumber isn't: plumbing and the screed have four days to spare. Marco's cold room is a special case: its chain also ends on day 25, held there by the delivery date, so it has no float either. Sanne marks the chain in red and from then on asks Kees one question every Thursday: is anything on the red chain moving? On 23 March the answer is yes.

  1. Put durations on the network

    From the three-point estimates.

  2. Forward pass: earliest start and finish

    Left to right; take the latest of the predecessors.

  3. Backward pass: latest start and finish

    Right to left; take the earliest of the successors.

  4. Float = latest − earliest

    Zero float: critical.

  5. Mark the critical path, and the near-critical ones

    A few days of float is near.

  6. Watch it every week

    A slip on it is a slip at the end.

Vocabulary for the exam

Critical path
The longest chain of dependent activities; it sets the end date.
Forward pass
Calculating earliest start and finish from the beginning.
Backward pass
Calculating latest start and finish from the end.
Float (slack)
How much an activity can slip without moving the end date.
Near-critical path
A chain with only a little float, which becomes critical easily.
Date constraint
A fixed date (a delivery) that holds an activity in place.

＋Going deeper the two passes by hand, what moves the path, and what the method ignores

Scheduling tools do the critical path in a millisecond, which is why few people can check it. This chapter does Rotterdam's by hand, then looks at three things the textbook version leaves out: calendars, paths that change, and the limits of the method.

1The forward pass

Start at day 1. For each activity, the earliest start is the day after the latest of its predecessors finishes; the earliest finish is start + duration − 1.

  • A starts day 1, finishes 3. B and C both wait for A: start 4. B finishes 7, C finishes 6.
  • E waits for B: 8–12. D waits for C: 7–13. F waits for E: 13–16. G: 17.
  • H waits for G (17) and D (13): take the later, so H starts 18 and finishes 20.
  • I waits for D (13), but the cold room arrives on Friday 27 March, day 20: start 21, finish 23. J waits for I (23) and G (17): 24–25.
  • K: 21–23. L: 24–25. Handover waits for J and L: day 25.

2The backward pass, and reading the result

Start at the end, day 25, and walk backwards. For each activity, the latest finish is the day before the earliest of its successors' latest starts; the latest start is latest finish − duration + 1.

  • L must finish by 25: start 24. K: 21–23. H: 18–20. G: 17. F: 13–16. E: 8–12. B: 4–7. A: 1–3.
  • D has two successors: H (latest start 18) and I (21). Take the earlier: D must finish by 17, so it may start as late as 11. Its earliest start was 7: 4 days of float. C, before it: latest 8–10, earliest 4–6: also 4.

Wherever latest equals earliest, the float is zero and the activity is critical.

3Calendars: how two days became five

The passes count working days. Dates come from laying those days on a calendar, and that's where 23 March got its sting. The inspector re-booked from Tuesday 24 to Thursday 26 March: two working days on the red chain. Laid on the builder's calendar, which has Easter Monday, 6 April off, the handover moved from Friday 3 April to Wednesday 8 April: two working days, five calendar days, over the three-day trigger.

The same calendar showed the cure: the builder working Saturday 28 and Sunday 29 March adds two days to the red chain exactly where they're needed (crashing, three notes on).

4Paths that change, and what the method ignores

  • Near-critical paths. C-D has 4 days of float. If the screed cures five days late, D becomes critical and pushes H. Watch chains with little float, not just the red one.
  • Paths become critical when you speed others up. After the weekend work, the builder's chain and the cold-room chain both end on day 25. No money could now bring the handover before 3 April: the delivery date holds it.
  • Resources. The method assumes people are there when the arrows allow. Rotterdam's first and second fix use the same electrician crew, which is fine because they're in sequence; two activities needing one crew at the same time would need re-planning (resource levelling, vol. 4).
  • Adaptive work has no critical path. The app's milestone (20 April) is fixed; the team manages scope inside it.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a short chain

  • Find the longest chain by eye.
  • Watch that chain.
  • No passes.

Standard

Rotterdam's fit-out

  • Two passes, by hand, with the supplier.
  • Critical and near-critical marked.
  • A weekly question: is anything red moving?

Heavy

large schedules

  • A scheduling tool with calendars and constraints.
  • Critical path and float reports weekly.
  • Schedule health checks by a scheduler.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Hurrying the wrong activity

    Paying the plumber to rush. Fix → only the critical path moves the end.

  • Forgetting the calendar

    Two days became five. Fix → lay working days on the real calendar.

  • Watching only the red chain

    A near-critical one overtakes it. Fix → watch anything with little float.

  • Trusting the tool blindly

    Missing links make a short, wrong path. Fix → check one pass by hand.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Predictive work with a fixed end date.
  • Before deciding where to spend effort or money.
  • Every week while the work runs.
  • When a slip is reported.

Skip it when…

  • Adaptive work: manage scope against a fixed date instead.

8Self-check quiz

What is the critical path?

The longest chain of dependent activities: it sets the end date.

Is the plumbing critical?

No: it has 4 days of float.

Why did a two-day slip make the handover five days late?

Two working days on the critical path, and Easter Monday was a day off for the builder: 3 April became 8 April.

Joost offers the plumber a bonus to finish two days early. What does it buy?

Nothing for the handover: plumbing isn't on the critical path.

After the weekend work, could more money bring the handover before 3 April?

No: the cold-room chain also ends on day 25, held by its delivery date.

Take your project's predictive work. Do a forward and a backward pass, by hand, on the eight to twelve biggest activities. Which chain is critical? Is the thing everyone worries about on it?
