Who Should Guess?

TL;DR

The people who will do the work make the best estimates, if they estimate independently first. Whoever speaks first sets an anchor that pulls every other number towards it. So: everyone writes a number privately, all reveal together, the highest and lowest explain their reasons (that's where the hidden risks are), then estimate again. For product work, teams do the same with story points and compare the total with their real velocity. The project manager asks the questions and keeps their own number to themselves.

  • Family: how big is it?
  • Lives in: a planning session; a record of rounds and reasons
  • Pairs with: let the team decide, MoSCoW, three-point estimates

1The situation Larder, 4 February 2026

This is Sanne's mistake for this collection. In the planning session of 4 February, before the platform estimate, she says to Eli, trying to help: “Zuid's set-up took about two weeks, didn't it?” Every estimate that follows is ten days. She has anchored the room. Eli had a much higher number in mind and a reason for it. He doesn't say it, because the project manager has just told everyone what the answer is.

  • The first number spoken pulls every other one towards it.
  • Agreement in the room hides the risks people know about.
  • A project manager's “just checking” is heard as a target.

2Real-life analogy the wine tasting

At a serious wine tasting, judges score each wine on a card, privately, before anyone speaks. Then they compare, and the judges with the highest and lowest scores explain what they tasted. Then they score again. If the most famous judge spoke first, everyone else's score would drift towards theirs, and the panel would taste one wine through one mouth.

3How it solves it independent first, reasons second, then the sum against capacity

Sanne sees what happened and fixes it the same afternoon. Platform set-up, again, as a small wideband Delphi: each person writes a number on a card; all turn them over together: 8, 10, 12, 24. Eli explains his 24: bottle deposits need their own VAT rate, and Rotterdam's zone edges cut through streets. Round two: 10, 12, 12, 14, and the three-point estimate (8, 12, 24) is recorded with its causes. For the apps, Ben's and Dee's teams use planning poker: the fourteen app Musts come to 61 points. With approvals running too, the teams have 8–10 points a sprint for Rotterdam: about seven sprints, and there are five before 20 April. Sanne doesn't argue with the sum. She takes it to Grace, and it becomes the reason for Grace's MoSCoW session on 3 March: 14 Musts become 6, 26 points, about three sprints.

  1. Invite the people who will do the work

    Not their managers instead.

  2. Keep your own number to yourself

    Ask questions, don't suggest answers.

  3. Estimate privately, reveal together

    Cards, fingers, an app.

  4. Ask the highest and lowest to explain

    Write their reasons down: they're risks.

  5. Estimate again

    Agreement after reasons, not before.

  6. Compare the total with real capacity

    Then take the gap to whoever owns scope.

Vocabulary for the exam

Anchoring
The pull of the first number heard on all later estimates.
Wideband Delphi
Estimating in rounds: private estimates, discussion of the spread, again.
Planning poker
A team estimates stories with cards, revealed together.
Story point
A relative size for a piece of product work, not hours.
Velocity
How many points a team actually finishes per sprint.
Expert judgment
Estimates from people who have done similar work.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small team

  • Everyone says a number at the same moment.
  • Outliers explain.
  • Agree.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Two rounds, written privately.
  • Reasons recorded as risks.
  • Totals compared with capacity.

Heavy

large estimates, contracts

  • Formal Delphi with an independent facilitator.
  • Estimates compared with a second, separate team.
  • Calibration against past actuals.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Anchoring

    The first number wins. Fix → private first; the project manager last, or never.

  • Managers estimate for teams

    Wrong numbers, nobody committed. Fix → ask the people who'll do it.

  • Averaging the outliers away

    The risk goes with them. Fix → make the high and low explain.

  • Points as hours

    Velocity becomes a target. Fix → points compare sizes; velocity is measured, not set.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any estimate a team will be held to.
  • When the room agrees suspiciously fast.
  • Product work: story points.
  • When one senior voice dominates.

Skip it when…

  • One person doing the work alone: ask them, privately, for three numbers.

8Self-check quiz

What is anchoring?

The pull of the first number heard on every later estimate.

Why do the highest and lowest estimates explain?

Their reasons are risks or shortcuts the others haven't thought of.

61 points, 8–10 points a sprint. How many sprints?

About six to eight: roughly seven.

Joost opens an estimating round with “the builder told me five weeks”. What does Sanne do?

Stop the round, ask everyone to write their own number first, and treat Joost's as one card among the rest.

The app sum didn't fit. Who decided what to cut?

Grace, who owns the app scope (MoSCoW, 3 March). Sanne brought the sum.

try it

At your next estimate, ask everyone to write their number privately and turn them over together. Ask the highest and lowest to explain. Write their reasons down as risks.

remember!!

Score on a card first. The famous judge speaks last.
