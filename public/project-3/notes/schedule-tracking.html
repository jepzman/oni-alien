a.k.a. monitoring and controlling the schedule

Are We Still on Time?

TL;DR

Track a schedule with the measure that fits each kind of work. Predictive work: are the milestones' forecast dates moving, and how much float is left on the critical path? Adaptive work: a burn-up of finished items against the plan line, and a forecast as a range from the real pace. Plot the forecasts week by week, because a line that creeps is a slip nobody has said out loud. And every slip ends in a decision: recover, re-plan, or accept.

  • Family: holding the line
  • Lives in: two charts on Thursday: the milestone trend and the burn-up
  • Pairs with: status reports, critical path, release plans

1The situation Larder, 13 March → 24 April 2026

On 13 March the steering group approves the baseline, and Femke asks: “how will I know, each week, whether the dates are holding?” It's a fair question with an awkward answer. Half of Rotterdam runs on a network of dated activities (the builder, stocking, hiring). The other half runs in sprints and story points with no dates inside them at all. One measure won't fit both, and two unrelated charts would leave Femke to do the sum herself.

  • A percentage of tasks says nothing about dates.
  • Sprints don't produce dates; activities don't produce velocity.
  • A single weekly snapshot can't show a slow creep.

2Real-life analogy the runner's kilometre markers

A marathon runner checks the watch at every kilometre marker: when did I expect to pass here, when did I actually, and at this pace, when will I finish? The finish is re-forecast forty-two times, not once at the end. Nobody waits for kilometre forty to discover they've been slowing down since twenty.

3How it solves it two charts, both forecasting, every Thursday

Sanne tracks the two halves on their own terms and puts both on one page. Predictive: each Thursday the leads give a forecast date for every milestone, and Kees says how much float is left on the red chain. The forecasts go on a milestone trend chart (in the chapter below): flat is good, a jump is news. Adaptive: a burn-up of must-haves done against the plan line, 33 on 26 February rising to 71 on 24 April. In March the line sags: 49 against 52 on 27 March. The forecast, from the real pace, is a range: 17 April to 3 May, with go / no-go on 24 April inside it. That range is what sends approvals in Rotterdam to June (change C-03). In April the pace rises: 55, 60, 69. The last two must-haves are done on 26 April, after go / no-go with conditions. At the steering group Femke sees one line per milestone: baseline, forecast, trend.

  1. Pick a measure for each kind of work

    Milestone dates and float; a burn-up.

  2. Collect forecasts on a fixed day

    From the people doing the work.

  3. Plot them over time, not just this week

    Trends show creep.

  4. Forecast as a range from the real pace

    Slowest and fastest recent weeks.

  5. For every slip: recover, re-plan or accept

    And say which.

  6. Report one line per milestone

    Baseline, forecast, trend.

＋Going deeper milestone trends, burn-ups, forecasting with a range, and float

The status page in Vol. 2 asked where the project would land. This chapter shows the instruments behind that answer for a hybrid schedule: a chart that catches creeping dates, a burn-up that can't hide scope changes, a forecast given as a range, and the one number to watch on a critical path.

1The milestone trend chart

Each Thursday, each milestone's owner gives a forecast date. Plot them: across, the day the forecast was made; up, the date forecast. Read the shape:

  • Flat: the date is holding. Fit-out done was flat on 3 April, except one point.
  • A jump: news, usually a single event. 23 March: the inspector. It came back down after the weekend work.
  • A slow climb: a slip nobody has announced. App ready crept from 20 to 22 April in late March. That creep, not a crisis, is what the chart is for.

Before 6 March, app ready was off the top of the chart: fourteen Musts didn't fit before 20 April. That's the estimating session's sum, seen as a trend.

2Burn-up, not burn-down

A burn-down shows work remaining falling to zero. A burn-up shows work done rising towards a separate scope line. The difference matters when scope changes: on a burn-down, adding scope looks like going backwards, and cutting scope looks like progress. On a burn-up, the scope line moves and everyone can see it.

Rotterdam's scope line stayed at 71 throughout: C-03 moved approvals out of the launch, but approvals was a Should, not one of the 71. What C-03 changed was the pace: Ben's team had a week back, and the next three weeks finished 6, 5 and 9.

3Forecast as a range

A single forecast from an average pace hides how unsure it is. Use the fastest and slowest recent weeks instead. On 27 March: 22 must-haves left; the last three weeks finished 7, 4 and 4. At 7 a week, done in about three weeks: 17 April. At 4 a week, in five and a half: 3 May. Go / no-go, 24 April, is inside the range.

That sentence is worth more than any colour. It says the date is possible but not safe, and it says how much faster the team must go (5½ a week) for it to be safe. The steering group's response, four days later, was to remove work rather than hope for speed.

4Float: the number to watch on a critical path

For predictive work, the single most useful weekly number is the float left on the critical path. It starts at zero on a tight plan like Rotterdam's (no buffer). Any negative number is a slip at the end of the same size: on 23 March, −2 working days. Recovery means getting back to zero (crashing, fast-tracking) or moving the baseline (a change).

Larger projects also measure schedule performance in money terms (earned value) and in time terms (earned schedule). Vol. 4 does those sums with Rotterdam's budget.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • One measure for all work

    Percentages of mixed things. Fix → milestones and float for predictive, burn-up for adaptive.

  • Only this week's snapshot

    Creep is invisible. Fix → plot the forecasts over time.

  • A point forecast

    False certainty. Fix → a range from the slowest and fastest weeks.

  • Burn-down hiding scope changes

    Cuts look like progress. Fix → burn-up with a scope line.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every week once there is a schedule baseline.
  • Hybrid projects with dates and sprints.
  • When a sponsor asks “are the dates holding?”
  • When forecasts have been flat for suspiciously long.

Skip it when…

  • Never; shrink it to one forecast per milestone.

8Self-check quiz

What does a slow upward line on a milestone trend chart mean?

A slip that hasn't been announced: the forecast is creeping.

Why a burn-up rather than a burn-down?

The scope line is separate, so scope changes are visible and can't look like progress.

22 left; recent weeks 7, 4 and 4. The forecast range?

About 3 weeks at 7 a week, 5½ at 4 a week.

What does −2 float on the critical path mean?

The end date will slip two working days unless something recovers it.

On 17 April the burn-up stands at 69, with go / no-go a week away and the last two must-haves hard. What does Sanne prepare?

A go / no-go recommendation with conditions: the two must-haves as named conditions with owners and a date (they were fixed on 26 April).

try it

Start a milestone trend chart today: for each of your project's milestones, write this week's forecast date in a column. Do it again next week, without changing last week's. In a month, read the shapes.

remember!!

Check the watch at every marker, and re-forecast the finish.
