How Much Can This Slip?

TL;DR

Float is how late an activity can be without moving something else. Total float: without moving the end date. Free float: without moving the next activity. Float on a chain is shared: whoever uses it first takes it from everyone after. A buffer is different: slack you add on purpose, visible, owned by the project manager, instead of hiding it inside estimates. Know where your float is, decide who may use it, and say plainly when there's none.

1The situation Larder, 24 February 2026

The plumber phones: could he start a week later? He has another job finishing. Joost: no, everything is tight. Kees: yes, plumbing has float. Both are partly right. Plumbing does have float, four days of it. But a week is five working days, and the four days aren't the plumber's alone: the floor screed comes after him, and it needs them too.

  • “There's float” is heard as “there's time”.
  • Float on a chain is shared, and the first to use it takes it.
  • Nobody knows how much slack the whole project has. (None.)

2Real-life analogy the morning bathroom

A family has thirty spare minutes between waking up and leaving for school. If the first person spends all thirty in the bathroom, the last person is late, even though nobody used anything but “spare” time. Spare time on a shared path belongs to the whole path. A sensible family says who gets how much, and keeps ten minutes nobody may touch.

3How it solves it total and free float, who may use it, and an honest “no buffer”

Sanne reads the float column with Kees. Total float: plumbing (C) and the screed (D) can each slip four days without moving the handover. Free float is the catch: the screed has four of its own, but plumbing has none. Any day the plumber slips is taken from the screed. So the answer is two days, not five: the plumber may start two days later, leaving two for the screed, which is the activity that can go wrong in a cold March. Kees agrees; the plumber agrees. And Sanne writes down the bigger fact the float column shows: the cold-room chain has none, the builder's chain has none, and there is no buffer between the handover and stocking. She asks the cold-room supplier to deliver two days early to create one; they can't (the panels leave the factory on the 26th). So she books a confirmation call for 20 March instead: an early warning where she couldn't buy a buffer.

  1. Read total float for every activity

    From the two passes.

  2. Read free float too

    That's what an owner can use alone.

  3. Decide who may use shared float

    Usually the riskier activity later in the chain.

  4. Look for chains with none

    Critical, or held by a fixed date.

  5. Add buffers on purpose, where you can

    Visible, owned by you.

  6. Where you can't buy a buffer, buy an early warning

    A check before the date.

Vocabulary for the exam

Total float
How long an activity can slip without moving the end date.
Free float
How long it can slip without moving the next activity.
Shared float
Float that belongs to a chain, not to one activity.
Buffer
Slack added on purpose and managed openly.
Project buffer
A buffer before the final date.
Feeding buffer
A buffer where a side chain joins the critical path.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • Ask “how much can this slip?” before saying yes.
  • Keep a little slack before the deadline.
  • Said, not written.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Total and free float read with the supplier.
  • Shared float allocated.
  • Buffers or early warnings where float is zero.

Heavy

large schedules

  • Float reports and float erosion trends.
  • Formal schedule contingency.
  • Critical-chain project and feeding buffers.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Float as free time

    “There's float, so yes.” Fix → check free float, and who comes after.

  • Padding estimates instead of buffers

    Hidden slack gets used anyway. Fix → honest estimates, visible buffers.

  • No buffer, not said

    Discovered in April. Fix → write “no buffer” as a weak point.

  • Nothing where you can't buy slack

    A surprise on the day. Fix → an early-warning check instead.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When someone asks to start late or finish late.
  • When a chain has a fixed date in it.
  • Before promising a date.
  • When float is getting used up week by week.

Skip it when…

  • Adaptive work: protect the sprint goal and the milestone instead.

8Self-check quiz

Total float or free float: how long can the screed slip without moving the next activity?

Free float: four days.

Why may the plumber only start two days late, not five?

Plumbing has no free float; its four days of total float are shared with the screed, which is riskier and needs some.

What's a buffer?

Slack added on purpose, visible and owned by the project manager.

The supplier couldn't deliver early. What did Sanne do instead?

A confirmation call on 20 March: an early warning where she couldn't buy a buffer.

On 10 March the screed contractor says the floor will cure two days slower in the cold. Does the handover move?

No: the plumber started two days late, which left two days of float, and the screed has just used them. Now that chain has none left either.

try it

For your project's critical deadline, write the float of the five activities nearest it. Which ones share float? Who should get it? Is there any buffer you could add on purpose, or an early warning you could book?
