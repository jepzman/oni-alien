a.k.a. “developing the scope structure”: the work breakdown structure (WBS)

How Do We Break It Down?

TL;DR

A work breakdown structure splits the whole result into smaller deliverables, level by level, until each piece (a work package) can be owned, estimated and checked. It is a tree of nouns, not a list of tasks. Its one hard rule: the children of every element must add up to 100 % of the parent, nothing more and nothing less. Most forgotten work is a hole in that rule, often one level further down than anyone looked.

  • Family: what, exactly?
  • Lives in: a tree on one page, plus a short dictionary entry per work package
  • Pairs with: story mapping, the scope statement, estimates

This is Sanne's mistake for this collection. On 17 February she breaks the launch down by workstream: nine branches, 71 must-haves, each with an owner. It is checked against the charter and against Joost's rows. On 20 March Eli asks who is ordering the store's internet line.   • The gap sat between the builder's work and Eli's.
  • The checks looked down one level, not two.
meanwhile, in Grace’s notebook
  • Story Mapping (Grace, vol. 3): Grace's story map of 16 February broke the approvals work into a backbone and 17 stories; it is the app branch's breakdown.

2Real-life analogy a dinner broken into dishes

A three-course dinner breaks down into a starter, a main and a dessert; the main into the roast, the potatoes and the gravy; each of those into something one cook can make and taste. The rule is simple: the parts must add up to the whole dinner. If they add up to slightly less, someone finds out at eight o'clock that nobody made the gravy, and everyone assumed somebody had.

3How it solves it nouns, level by level, until each piece can be owned and checked

On 17 February Sanne and the leads draw the tree. Level 1: the launch. Level 2: nine branches, one per workstream, plus project management (no must-haves, about 6 % of the effort, but real work). Level 3: the deliverables in each branch. The leaves are the 71 must-haves: fit-out 16, people 9, stock 10, platform 12, apps 6, payments and legal 9, marketing 5, opening and hypercare 4. The app branch is Grace's story map. Each work package gets a dictionary entry: what it is, owner, “done means”, estimate, what it waits for. The app branch is cut to 6 must-haves after Grace's session of 3 March, and the tree is baselined on 13 March. After 20 March, “store systems online” gets its missing child: the internet line, owner Eli.

  1. Start from the scope statement's deliverables

    Level 2.

  2. Break each into smaller deliverables

    Nouns, not verbs.

  3. Stop when a piece has an owner, an estimate and a check

    That is a work package.

  4. Check the 100 % rule at every level

    Charter, walk-through, suppliers' small print.

  5. Write a dictionary entry per work package

    Five lines each.

  6. Baseline it with the scope statement

    Then change it only on purpose.

＋Going deeper decomposition, the 100 % rule, and the dictionary

A WBS looks like the easiest thing in this book: draw a tree. The mistakes are in three places: breaking down by activity instead of by deliverable, stopping at the wrong depth, and trusting that the children add up to the parent. This chapter takes them in turn, with Rotterdam's missed line as the example.

1Nouns, not verbs

The commonest bad WBS is a list of phases: plan, buy, build, test. Every phase touches every deliverable, so nobody owns a result, and “build is 80 % done” means nothing. A WBS breaks down what is delivered: cold room, shelving, chargers, systems online. Each of those can have one owner and one check.

How deep? Stop when a piece is a work package: one owner, an estimate someone believes, and a check that says it's done. A common rule of thumb is that a work package takes between a day and two weeks of effort. Larger, and it hides too much; smaller, and the tree becomes a task list. In an adaptive branch, the work packages are features or stories, and the lower levels are filled in sprint by sprint (rolling wave).

2The 100 % rule, and three ways to test it

The rule: the children of every element must add up to all of the parent, including work nobody likes (project management, training, clean-up). A WBS that passes it at level 2 can still fail at level 4. Three checks catch most holes:

  1. Top down, against the charter.

    Every IN line has a branch; no branch is OUT. Rotterdam passed.

  2. Walk the finished result.

    A day in the life of the open store: a delivery arrives, a picker logs in, an order is paid. Rotterdam did this on paper; nobody imagined the handheld actually connecting.

  3. Read every supplier's small print.

    “By client”, “by others”, “excluded”, “not in scope”: each phrase is work that someone must own. Rotterdam skipped this one. The builder had written it down; Larder had not read it as a work package.

3The dictionary: five lines per work package

A tree shows the shape; the dictionary says what each leaf means. Five lines are enough: content (what's included, including from suppliers), owner, done means, estimate, waits for. Writing the content line is where small print gets read: 1.1.5's entry, written properly, would have had to say “internet line: …?” and someone would have asked.

Together, the scope statement, the WBS and the dictionary are the scope baseline. Rotterdam's was approved on 13 March. The internet line was added on 23 March as a correction inside an existing must-have: the count stayed 71, the promise didn't change, only the plan for keeping it did.

4WBS, story map, task list

Three tools break work down, at different levels:

  • The WBS breaks down the whole project's result. One per project.
  • A story map breaks down one product's journey into features and stories: here, Grace's map is the app branch.
  • A task list or sprint board breaks one work package into activities for this week. These are verbs, and they belong to the people doing the work.

The WBS is the one that stops at the work package. Below that, the team decides how; the schedule (next collection) turns work packages into activities with durations and links.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a short job

  • A list of deliverables with owners.
  • One question: “what's missing?”
  • No dictionary.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • A tree of nine branches and 71 must-haves.
  • Five-line dictionary entries.
  • Three checks of the 100 % rule.

Heavy

large or contracted work

  • A coded WBS used for cost and schedule.
  • A full dictionary under change control.
  • WBS reviews with every supplier.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Phases as branches

    Plan, build, test: nobody owns a result. Fix → break down by deliverable.

  • Too deep

    A task list in disguise. Fix → stop at owner + estimate + check.

  • The 100 % rule at level 2 only

    Holes hide lower down. Fix → three checks, at every level.

  • Supplier small print unread

    “By client” becomes nobody. Fix → turn every “by client” into a work package.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • After the scope statement, before estimating.
  • When work keeps falling between teams.
  • With every supplier's quote in hand.
  • When you need one view of the whole result.

Skip it when…

  • A single deliverable with one owner.

8Self-check quiz

What is a work package?

The lowest level of the WBS: one owner, an estimate, a check.

State the 100 % rule.

The children of each element add up to all of the parent: nothing more, nothing less.

Why nouns and not verbs?

Deliverables can have one owner and a check; activities touch everything and own nothing.

A new supplier quote for the store's alarm says “monitoring contract by client”. What does Sanne do?

Add a work package under the right branch, with an owner and a date, the day she reads it.

Adding the internet line on 23 March didn't change the 71. Why not?

It was a missing part of an existing must-have (store systems online), not a new promise.

try it

Draw your project's WBS to level 3. Then run the third check: read every supplier quote and contract for “by client”, “by others” and “excluded”. Is each of those a work package with an owner?
