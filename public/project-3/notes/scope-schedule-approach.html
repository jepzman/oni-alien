How Will We Pin It Down?

TL;DR

Before you write down scope or dates, decide how you will: what the unit of scope is and who accepts it, how far it will be broken down, which units and calendar the schedule uses, how much detail to plan now and how much later (rolling wave), how progress will be tracked and how changes are made. It fits on a page. Without it, every workstream plans in its own units, and no two plans can be added up.

1The situation Larder, 10 February 2026

Ten days before the plan is due, Sanne has four kinds of plan on her desk. Joost's spreadsheet: 140 rows, and no row says when it counts as done. The builder's quote: “about five weeks”, in one line. Ben's team: two-week sprints and no dates at all. The app scope: Grace won't settle it until 3 March. Each is a reasonable way to plan. None can be laid beside another, so nobody can say whether they add up to 27 April.

  • Rows, weeks, sprints and “later” are four different units.
  • Nobody has said what “done” means, or who says so.
  • Nobody has said how far ahead to plan in detail.

2Real-life analogy the map-maker's first decisions

Before drawing a line, a map-maker decides three things: the scale (how much detail), the legend (what each mark means) and the grid (the units). The town centre is drawn street by street; the hills beyond are outlines, filled in when someone walks there. Two maps with different scales can't be pinned edge to edge. Two plans with different units can't either.

3How it solves it one page of rules, before the first estimate

Sanne writes the rules before the plan. For scope: the unit is a must-have, each with an owner and a “done means” line; the must-haves are broken down by workstream; each is accepted by its owner at a gate; changes follow the three triggers. For the schedule: the predictive workstreams are planned in working days, Monday to Friday; the adaptive ones in two-week sprints. Detail is planned four weeks ahead and milestones beyond (rolling wave). And a calendar: the builder doesn't work on Easter Monday, 6 April; opening day, 27 April, is King's Day, when the store and couriers work and offices don't. That calendar line turns out to matter on 23 March.

  1. Choose the unit of scope

    And who accepts it.

  2. Decide how far to break it down

    Until each piece has an owner and a check.

  3. Choose the units of time

    Working days, sprints, or both.

  4. Write the calendar

    Holidays, shutdowns, who works when.

  5. Decide the planning horizon

    Detail near, outline far; when to fill in.

  6. Say how progress and change are handled

    Then start planning.

Vocabulary for the exam

Scope management plan
How scope will be defined, broken down, accepted and controlled.
Schedule management plan
How the schedule will be built, in what units and detail, and tracked.
Rolling-wave planning
Detailed planning for near work; outline planning for later work, filled in as it approaches.
Project calendar
The working days and hours the schedule counts in.
Unit of measure
What the plan counts in: days, story points, must-haves.
Planning horizon
How far ahead the detailed plan reaches.

SCOPE
  unit          a must-have: one line, an owner, a "done means" (71 of them)

SCHEDULE

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a short job for one team

  • Agree the unit and the definition of done.
  • One calendar.
  • Said, not written.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • One page of rules for scope and schedule.
  • Working days and sprints side by side.
  • Rolling wave, four weeks ahead.

Heavy

large or contracted work

  • Separate scope and schedule management plans.
  • Scheduling standards and tools.
  • Formal calendars per contractor.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Planning first, rules later

    Four units, no sum. Fix → a page of rules before the first estimate.

  • Detail to the horizon

    Week 14 planned by the hour, then wrong. Fix → rolling wave: detail near, outline far.

  • No calendar

    Holidays found by accident. Fix → write the working days and holidays down.

  • One method forced on all

    Sprints for the builder, Gantt for the app. Fix → both, in the same rules.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • At the start of planning.
  • When teams plan in different units.
  • When a hybrid project needs one view.
  • Before asking anyone for an estimate.

Skip it when…

  • A one-team job that already has a working rhythm: write the calendar only.

8Self-check quiz

What is Rotterdam's unit of scope?

A must-have with an owner and a “done means” line.

What is rolling-wave planning?

Detail for near work, outline for later work, filled in as it gets closer.

Why write a calendar?

So holidays and non-working days are in the plan, not discovered.

The builder says “five weeks”; Ben says “three sprints”. How does Sanne compare them?

In the same calendar: five weeks is 25 working days from 2 March; three sprints is six weeks from the sprint start. Then both are dates.

On 10 February Joost wants every activity to 27 April planned by the day. What does Sanne answer?

Detail for the next four weeks, milestones beyond. The far detail would be guesses, and it would be wrong by March.

try it

Write your project's rules on half a page: the unit of scope and who accepts it, the units of time, the calendar (with holidays), how far ahead you plan in detail, and how progress is counted. Which line did nobody agree before?
