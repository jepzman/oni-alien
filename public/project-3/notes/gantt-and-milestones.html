How Do We Show the Dates?

TL;DR

A schedule has different readers who need different levels. The people who decide need a milestone chart: a few dates that matter, and whether they're holding. The people who co-ordinate need a summary by workstream. The people who do the work need the detailed Gantt chart: bars on a calendar. Draw every level from the same network, so they can never disagree, and show each audience only the level it needs.

1The situation Larder, 20–27 February 2026

At the steering group of 27 February, Sanne's full schedule goes up on the screen: the builder's twelve bars, five other workstreams, sprints, deliveries, training groups. Nearly two hundred lines. Femke squints and asks one question: “are we opening on 27 April?” The answer is on the chart, somewhere. Nobody in the room can find it, and the next ten minutes are spent on a bar for chargers.

  • One chart for every audience serves none of them.
  • Detail drags the meeting down to the wrong level.
  • Charts drawn by hand drift away from the plan.
meanwhile, in Grace’s notebook
  • Roadmap vs Release Plan (Grace, vol. 2): on 9 March Grace and Sanne separate the roadmap (outcomes, for Priya) from the release plan (R1, R2, R3, for the teams).

2Real-life analogy three maps of one journey

For one journey you might carry three maps. A country map with the cities on it, for deciding where to go and roughly when. A road map with the route, for the driver. A street plan of the last kilometre, for finding the door. All three show the same journey. Nobody hands the passenger the street plan to ask whether they'll arrive tonight.

3How it solves it one network, three views, each for its reader

From the steering group of 13 March on, Femke sees five diamonds: fit-out done 3 April, stock in 17 April, app ready 20 April, go / no-go 24 April, opening 27 April, each with its baseline date, its forecast and a colour. The Thursday leads see a summary: one bar per workstream, the shared milestones, and the links between workstreams. Kees and his crew get the detailed Gantt for the fit-out: twelve bars over 25 working days, critical ones in red, float dotted, the cold room's bar starting on its delivery date. All three are generated from the same network in a scheduling tool, so a change on site moves the diamonds too. The adaptive teams don't get a Gantt at all: their view is the sprint board and the must-have burn-up.

  1. Build one network; generate every view from it

    Never redraw dates by hand.

  2. Give decision-makers milestones

    Five to ten, with baseline, forecast and colour.

  3. Give co-ordinators a summary

    A bar per workstream, the shared milestones.

  4. Give doers the detail

    Activities, the critical path, float.

  5. Mark today on every view

    And what has moved.

  6. Show adaptive work in its own form

    Boards and burn-ups, not bars.

Vocabulary for the exam

Gantt chart
A bar chart of activities against a calendar.
Milestone
A point in time when something important is finished; zero duration.
Milestone chart
A schedule showing only milestones.
Summary schedule
A higher-level schedule, a bar per workstream or phase.
Schedule baseline
The approved version of the schedule.
Today line
A vertical line marking the current date.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small project

  • A list of three to five dates.
  • A simple bar chart if it helps.
  • Updated by hand.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Milestones, summary and detail.
  • All from one network.
  • A view per audience.

Heavy

large programmes

  • Integrated master schedule in a tool.
  • Standard levels (1 to 4) of schedule.
  • Automatic reports per stakeholder group.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • The 200-line slide

    Nobody finds the answer. Fix → milestones for those who decide.

  • Hand-drawn Gantts

    They drift from the plan. Fix → generate every view from the network.

  • Milestones without forecasts

    Only the baseline, forever green. Fix → baseline and forecast side by side.

  • A Gantt for sprints

    Fake precision. Fix → boards and burn-ups for adaptive work.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every project with more than one audience.
  • Steering groups and sponsors: milestones.
  • Site and suppliers: detailed Gantt.
  • Whenever someone asks “when?”

Skip it when…

  • A single-team project: one simple view is enough.

8Self-check quiz

Which view does the steering group get?

The milestone chart.

Why generate every view from one network?

So they can never disagree, and a change moves all of them.

What is a milestone's duration?

Zero: it's a point in time.

Kees asks for the milestone chart instead of the Gantt. What's wrong with that?

He'd lose the activities, the critical path and the float he needs to run the site.

Ben asks for a Gantt of his sprints. What does Sanne offer instead?

The sprint board and the burn-up of must-haves; bars would pretend to know the order of work that's decided each sprint.

try it

Write down who reads your schedule. For each reader, sketch the one view they need, at the right level. Are all of them generated from the same plan?

remember!!

A country map for those who decide, a street plan for those who drive.
