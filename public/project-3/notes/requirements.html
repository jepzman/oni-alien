What Do They Actually Need?

TL;DR

A requirement is a condition the result must meet: what it must do, how well, under which rules. People rarely state requirements; they state solutions (“a second door”) or comparisons (“same as Zuid”). Eliciting means drawing out the need behind each: watch the work, ask about real moments, test prototypes, read what went wrong last time. Analysing means sorting, removing duplicates and conflicts, and tracing each one to something you'll deliver and a check that proves it.

  • Family: what, exactly?
  • Lives in: a requirements list with a trace to each must-have
  • Pairs with: customer interviews, prototype testing, the scope statement

1The situation Larder, 11–12 February 2026

Joost's spreadsheet is full of answers. “A second cold-room door.” “Forty chargers.” “Shelving the same as Zuid.” One row reads, in full, “same as Zuid”. Tomás laughs: Zuid is where 340 products opened without a price. Each row is a solution somebody already chose. Nobody wrote down what it was for, so nobody can tell whether it's the right one, or whether a cheaper one would do.

  • Solutions hide the need behind them.
  • “Same as last time” copies the mistakes too.
  • Without a need, there's no way to check the result.
meanwhile, in Grace’s notebook
  • Prototype Testing with Users (Grace, vol. 1): Grace's prototype test with 6 customers and 3 pickers (13–22 January) found the picker-side needs: glove-friendly buttons, answers applied at packing.

2Real-life analogy the doctor's first questions

A patient walks in and asks for a particular medicine. A good doctor doesn't write the prescription. They ask about the symptoms: what hurts, since when, what makes it worse. Sometimes the medicine the patient named is right. Often a different one is better, or cheaper, or safer. The prescription follows the diagnosis, never the request.

3How it solves it four ways of asking, then trace every need

Sanne uses four sources. She walks the Friday peak at Noord (17:30–19:00) with Tomás and watches where pickers wait. She runs a 90-minute workshop on 12 February with Tomás, Joost's operations lead, Sam and Lena, asking about real moments: “tell me about the last time a shift went wrong.” She takes the picker-side findings from Grace's prototype test. And she reads Zuid's opening as a list of requirements nobody wrote. Out come 96 requirements. Each “solution” row is turned back into its need: “a second door” becomes no more than two pickers waiting at the cold room at peak, met for launch by a wider door and a layout change. Then each must-have is traced: need, requirement, must-have, check, who accepts.

  1. Find everyone who will live with the result

    Not only those who asked.

  2. Watch the work at its worst moment

    A peak, a busy shift.

  3. Ask about real past moments

    “Tell me about the last time…”

  4. Turn each solution back into its need

    “What is it for?”

  5. Sort: must-have, helps, out

    Remove duplicates and conflicts.

  6. Trace each need to a deliverable and a check

    And name who accepts it.

Vocabulary for the exam

Requirement
A condition or capability the result must meet.
Elicitation
Drawing requirements out of people and evidence.
Business requirement
Why the organisation wants it: 500 orders a day.
Stakeholder requirement
What a group of people needs: pickers reach chilled goods fast.
Non-functional requirement
How well: speed, capacity, safety, temperature.
Transition requirement
What's needed to switch over: training, data.
Traceability matrix
A table linking each requirement to its deliverable, check and owner.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small, familiar change

  • Ask “what is it for?” of every request.
  • A short list of needs.
  • A check for each.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Observation, a workshop, existing findings.
  • Solutions turned back into needs.
  • A trace from need to check and owner.

Heavy

regulated or contracted work

  • A business analyst and formal elicitation.
  • A full traceability matrix under change control.
  • Requirements reviewed and signed per group.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Collecting solutions

    “A second door.” Fix → ask what it's for.

  • “Same as last time”

    Copies the mistakes. Fix → read last time as a list of requirements.

  • Asking only the sponsor

    The people who work there are missed. Fix → watch the work; ask them.

  • No check

    Nobody can prove it's met. Fix → write the check next to each requirement.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Before the scope statement.
  • When requests arrive as solutions.
  • When a similar project went wrong.
  • When many groups will live with the result.

Skip it when…

  • The needs are already known and written, and nothing has changed.

8Self-check quiz

Need or solution: “a second cold-room door”?

A solution. The need: no more than two pickers waiting at the cold room at peak.

Name two ways Sanne drew requirements out.

Watching the Friday peak at Noord; a workshop about real past moments (also: Grace's prototype test, Zuid's opening).

What does a trace link?

Need, requirement, must-have, check and who accepts it.

Priya asks for “a Rotterdam landing page like Amsterdam's”. What does Sanne ask?

What it is for. If the need is “people in Rotterdam can join the waitlist”, the existing page with a city field may meet it.

A requirement has no check. What's wrong?

Nobody can prove it's met, so it can't be accepted. Write the check, or it isn't a requirement yet.

try it

Take five items from your project's list. For each, ask “what is it for?” and write the need in one sentence. Then write how you'd check it's met. Did any item turn out to be a solution with a cheaper alternative?

remember!!

Ask about the symptoms before you write the prescription.
