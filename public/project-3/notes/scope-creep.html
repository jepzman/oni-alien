a.k.a. monitoring and controlling scope: scope creep and gold plating

Where Did That Come From?

TL;DR

Scope creep is scope growing through small additions that nobody decided. Each one is too small to bother anyone; together they eat the schedule. Its cousin, gold plating, is the team adding extras nobody asked for. The fix isn't to say no to everything: it's to make every addition a decision. Put each on a list, with who asked and what it costs, and decide it at a fixed time, at the right level. Watch the pace: creep usually shows up first as work slowing down for no visible reason.

  • Family: holding the line
  • Lives in: an extras list beside the must-have board, decided on Thursdays

1The situation Larder, 27 March 2026

This is Sanne's mistake for this collection. For two weeks the must-have count has risen only four a week, and the blocked column doesn't explain it all. On 27 March she asks Ben, Dee and Eli where their time went. Small things, they say. A promo code for Priya. Neighbourhood names in the zone picker. A dashboard for Yara's office. Nine additions since the baseline, none of them must-haves, none written down: about seven team-days, five of them from the three people the blocked must-haves were waiting for. Sanne had told everyone only the 71 counted. She had never said who could say yes to anything else.

  • Each addition was too small to ask about.
  • “Helps” rows looked free because nobody owned them.
  • The cost showed up only as a slower pace.
meanwhile, in Grace’s notebook
  • Feature Request Triage (Grace, vol. 2): since 2 March Grace has triaged every product request on Monday mornings: joined to an opportunity, a quick fix, parked or declined.

2Real-life analogy the household budget

Nobody decides to overspend. A coffee here, a magazine there, a small subscription. Each is too small to think about, so nobody does. At the end of the month the money is gone and the rent is short. Households that stay solvent don't ban coffee. They write everything down and look at the list once a week.

3How it solves it one list, a decision for every addition, at a fixed time

Sanne says it plainly at the Thursday meeting: the rule was hers, and it had a hole. Then she starts an extras list beside the must-have board and writes down all nine: who asked, who did it, the time it took. Five are kept, because they're done or nearly free (the promo code, English shelf labels, a tour video for training, Rotterdam test data, courier route cards). Four are stopped until after opening (a late Friday slot, neighbourhood names, the dashboard, extra email copy). The new rule: any addition goes on the list, however small, and is decided on Thursday. Sanne decides those inside her authority (no milestone, money or must-have moves); anything bigger is a change for Joost or the steering group. The rule catches its first one the same week: an in-store launch event, which becomes change C-05 and is rejected. The pace rises again in April, helped by C-03.

  1. Watch the pace

    Slowing for no visible reason is the first sign.

  2. Ask where the time went

    Without blame.

  3. Put every addition on one list

    Who asked, who did it, what it cost.

  4. Decide them at a fixed time, at the right level

    Small ones yourself; big ones as changes.

  5. Keep, stop, or move to later

    Out loud, to the person who asked.

  6. Tell teams who may say yes

    And that “no, but on the list” is always allowed.

Vocabulary for the exam

Scope creep
Uncontrolled growth of scope through small, undecided additions.
Gold plating
The team adding extras nobody asked for.
Scope baseline
The approved scope against which additions are measured.
Variance (scope)
The difference between what is being delivered and what was agreed.
Extras list
A visible list of every addition and its decision.
Opportunity cost
What the time spent on an extra could have done instead.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

one team

  • A product owner orders every request.
  • Nothing starts without a yes.
  • A short list.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • An extras list beside the must-haves.
  • Decided weekly at the right level.
  • Pace watched for creep.

Heavy

contracts

  • Every addition a formal change request.
  • Scope verified against the contract.
  • Claims for unapproved work rejected.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • “Only half a day”

    Nine of them is a week. Fix → every addition on the list.

  • Nobody owns the yes

    Teams say yes to whoever asks. Fix → say who decides, and when.

  • Banning extras

    People go round the rule. Fix → keep, stop or later, decided fast.

  • Gold plating

    The team adds polish nobody asked for. Fix → extras from the team go on the list too.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • From the day there is a scope baseline.
  • When the pace drops without a visible cause.
  • When many people can ask the same team for things.
  • Near a deadline.

Skip it when…

  • Purely exploratory work where scope is meant to grow.

8Self-check quiz

Scope creep or gold plating: Eli adds Rotterdam test data nobody asked for?

Gold plating (useful here, which is why it was kept).

What was the first sign of creep?

The pace dropped to four a week without enough blocked items to explain it.

Who decides a half-day addition that moves no milestone, money or must-have?

Sanne, at the Thursday meeting.

Why did Sanne call it her mistake?

She said only the 71 counted, but never said who could say yes to anything else.

On 2 April Priya asks Dee for “just a banner” in the app for Rotterdam. What does Dee say?

“Yes, on the list”: it's decided on Thursday. If it moves nothing, Sanne can approve it there.

try it

Ask your team where their time went last week. List every piece of work that wasn't in the plan, with who asked. For each, decide: keep, stop, or later. Then tell them who decides additions from now on.

remember!!
