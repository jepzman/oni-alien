How Long, Roughly?

TL;DR

There are three basic ways to estimate. Analogous: from a similar past job, adjusted (quick, rough). Parametric: a quantity times a rate you trust (good when the work is repetitive). Bottom-up: list the pieces and add them up (slower, most accurate, if the list is complete). Pick the method that fits the facts you have, say how sure each estimate is, and remember the trap: a rate tells you the effort, not the duration.

1The situation Larder, 3–5 February 2026

Femke stops Sanne in the corridor on 3 February: “Will it fit in fifteen weeks?” There is no plan yet. Sanne has three kinds of information: a store Larder opened before (Zuid, in 2024), a few rates from Noord (how long it takes to shelve a product), and teams who could list their work if she asked. She has a day, and one wrong answer would follow the launch to April.

  • Different parts of the work come with different facts.
  • A single number hides how sure it is.
  • Effort and elapsed time are easy to confuse.

2Real-life analogy three ways to time a drive

How long to drive to the coast? From memory: “last time it took two hours.” From a rate: 180 kilometres at 90 an hour. From the parts: the ring road at rush hour, the motorway, the coast road, each timed. Memory is quick and rough. The rate is good if the road is uniform. The parts are slowest to work out and most accurate, if you didn't forget the ferry.

3How it solves it match the method to the facts, and say how sure

Sanne uses all three, one per kind of fact. Fit-out, analogous: Zuid's took six weeks for 520 m², one week of it re-doing the floor; Rotterdam is 600 m², so five to seven weeks. Stocking, parametric: Noord's rate is about 2½ minutes per product (receive, place, label, scan), so 2,500 products is about 104 hours, fifteen person-days, four days for a team of four. But suppliers deliver in slots over ten working days, so stocking takes ten days: the rate gave the effort, the deliveries set the duration. Platform set-up, bottom-up: Eli lists nine pieces of work, half a day to three days each: twelve days. Her answer to Femke on 5 February: it fits, with no spare week, and the fit-out is the long pole.

  1. Look at what facts you have for each piece

    A past job, a rate, a list.

  2. Analogous where you have a similar job

    Adjust for size and differences.

  3. Parametric where the work repeats

    Quantity × a rate you trust.

  4. Bottom-up where the team can list the work

    Add up the pieces.

  5. Separate effort from duration

    Who, how many, and what else sets the pace.

  6. Write how sure each estimate is

    A range, not a point.

Vocabulary for the exam

Analogous estimate
An estimate based on a similar past piece of work.
Parametric estimate
A quantity multiplied by a rate per unit.
Bottom-up estimate
The sum of estimates for the smaller pieces.
Effort
Work time: person-hours or person-days.
Duration
Elapsed working time from start to finish.
Rough order of magnitude
A very early estimate, perhaps −25 % to +75 %.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small job

  • One method, the one with the best facts.
  • A range in one sentence.
  • No table.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • A method per workstream.
  • Effort and duration separated.
  • An estimates table with “how sure”.

Heavy

large or contracted work

  • Estimating databases and calibrated rates.
  • Independent estimates to compare.
  • Estimates reviewed and baselined formally.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Effort as duration

    “104 hours, so three days.” Fix → ask what else sets the pace.

  • The analogy that isn't

    Zuid had different floors. Fix → adjust for the differences, out loud.

  • Bottom-up with a missing piece

    The forgotten ferry. Fix → check the list against the WBS.

  • A number without a range

    Heard as a promise. Fix → always say how sure.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every estimate, at every stage.
  • Early: analogous for a quick answer.
  • Repetitive work: parametric.
  • Before a baseline: bottom-up.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip saying how sure. Skip bottom-up when the work can't yet be listed.

8Self-check quiz

Analogous, parametric or bottom-up: “Zuid took six weeks, so…”?

Analogous.

Stocking needs 104 hours of effort. Why does it take ten days?

Deliveries arrive in slots over ten working days; the deliveries, not the labour, set the pace.

Which method is most accurate, and when?

Bottom-up, if the list of pieces is complete.

On 5 February Joost says “the builder said five weeks, so let's write five weeks”. What does Sanne add?

The basis (the builder's list) and how sure it is; the analogy with Zuid says five to seven.

A parametric rate came from Noord's night shift. Is it safe for Rotterdam's first week?

Probably optimistic: new staff in a new store. Adjust it, and say so.

try it

Take your project's three biggest pieces of work. For each, write the estimate, the method, the basis in one line, and how sure you are. Then check one: is it effort or duration?

remember!!

A rate gives the effort. Something else sets the pace.
