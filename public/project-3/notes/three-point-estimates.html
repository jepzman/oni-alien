How Sure Are We?

TL;DR

Instead of one number, ask for three: the best case, the most likely and the worst case (with what would cause it). Combine them into an expected value and a spread; a common formula is (best + 4 × likely + worst) ÷ 6. Then you can say how likely each date is. Most estimates lean one way: more can go wrong than right, so the expected value is later than the most likely. A plan built on the most likely number is usually a plan you'll miss.

1The situation Larder, 6 February 2026

Two estimates matter more than the rest. Kees, the builder's site manager: the fit-out takes 25 working days. Eli: the platform set-up takes 12. Sanne asks each how sure they are. Both say “pretty sure”. Neither number says what could go wrong, or by how much, and the fit-out has no room: 25 days from 2 March is exactly 3 April.

  • A single number is heard as a promise.
  • “Pretty sure” can't be compared or added up.
  • What could go wrong is in people's heads, not in the plan.

2Real-life analogy the weather forecast

A weather forecaster doesn't say “it will rain at three.” They say “70 % chance of rain this afternoon.” Nobody thinks that's a weak forecast. It's an honest one, and people decide with it: take an umbrella, move the barbecue. A forecast that pretended to know the minute would be wrong most days, and nobody would plan around it.

3How it solves it three numbers, the causes of the worst, and the odds

Sanne asks Kees and Eli for three numbers and what would cause the worst. Kees: best 22, likely 25, worst 34 (the electrical inspection re-booked, materials late, the screed curing slowly in a cold March). Eli: 8, 12, 24 (deposits on bottles need their own VAT rate; zone edges are messy). The PERT sum gives the fit-out an expected 26 days, give or take 2, and the platform 13⅓, give or take 2⅔. Read as odds, the 25-day plan has about a 1 in 3 chance; the expected date is 7 April; 9 April is 84 % likely (Easter Monday is a day off for the builder). Sanne keeps 3 April as the target, because stocking needs it, and does two things: the plan says “no spare week” as its weak point, and she asks the builder in February what weekend work would cost. On 23 March that price is already on her desk.

  1. Ask for best, likely and worst

    From the person who does the work.

  2. Ask what would cause the worst

    That's a risk list for free.

  3. Compute the expected value and spread

    (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6; (P − O) ÷ 6.

  4. Turn it into odds for the dates that matter

    Your target, your promise.

  5. Decide what to promise and what to buffer

    Out loud.

  6. Prepare the response to the worst case

    Before it happens.

Vocabulary for the exam

Three-point estimate
An estimate given as best, most likely and worst case.
PERT
A method that weights the most likely value four times: (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6.
Triangular average
(O + M + P) ÷ 3: gives the extremes more weight.
Standard deviation (σ)
A measure of spread; for PERT, roughly (P − O) ÷ 6.
Confidence level
How likely an estimate is to be met: P50, P80, P90.
Skew
A distribution that leans: more room to be late than early.

＋Going deeper the formulas, the odds, adding estimates up, and what it can't fix

Three-point estimating takes five minutes to ask for and is often misused. This chapter shows the two common formulas side by side, how to turn the result into odds for a date, how to add several estimates together without fooling yourself, and what no formula can fix.

1Two formulas

The triangular average treats the three numbers equally: (O + M + P) ÷ 3. The PERT (beta) average gives the most likely value four times the weight: (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6. For the fit-out, triangular says 27, PERT says 26. Both are later than the most likely 25, because the worst case is further from it than the best case is.

Use PERT when the person estimating has done the work before and their “most likely” deserves trust; triangular when the three numbers are all guesses. The spread (one standard deviation) for PERT is about (P − O) ÷ 6: here, 2 days.

2From numbers to odds

A rough rule, borrowed from the bell curve: the expected value has about a 50 % chance; one spread later, about 84 %; two spreads later, about 98 %. For the fit-out, that's 26, 28 and 30 days.

The plan of 25 days sits half a spread below the expected value: about 31 %. That one sentence changed the February conversation. Nobody argued that the builder was wrong. They argued about what to do with a 1-in-3 date: keep it as the target, say plainly that there's no spare week, and price the rescue in advance.

The rule is approximate. Real delays bunch together (one wet week delays several activities at once), so the true odds are usually a little worse.

3Adding estimates up

When activities follow each other, their expected values add up. Their spreads don't: the squares of the spreads add, and you take the square root. The fit-out (26 ± 2) followed by stocking (best 9, likely 10, worst 14: expected 10.5 ± 0.8) gives 36.5 days, give or take √(4 + 0.7) ≈ 2.2, not 2.8.

Two lessons follow. A chain is more predictable than its parts suggest, if the parts vary independently. And the planned chain, built from the most likely values (25 + 10 = 35), has only about a 1 in 4 chance. Plans built on “most likely” numbers get less likely with every activity added.

4What no formula fixes

  • Optimism. If the worst case is “a bit late”, the worst case is wrong. Ask “what would make this take twice as long?”
  • Hidden padding. If people pad their “likely”, the numbers look safe and the time gets used anyway. Keep the estimate honest and put the slack in a visible buffer (two notes on).
  • Risks shared by many activities. A strike, a flu wave, a re-booked inspector. These move many estimates at once, which is what the risk register is for (vol. 5).

For big projects the next step is a Monte Carlo simulation: thousands of random runs through the whole schedule. Vol. 5 shows it in plain words.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • Ask “best and worst?” after every estimate.
  • Say the range.
  • No sums.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Three points for the activities that matter.
  • PERT and a spread.
  • Odds for the target date, and a prepared response.

Heavy

large, uncertain work

  • Three points for every activity.
  • Monte Carlo on the whole schedule.
  • Promised dates at a chosen confidence (P80).

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A worst case that isn't

    “Maybe a day late.” Fix → ask what would make it take twice as long.

  • Promising the most likely

    A plan you'll usually miss. Fix → say the odds; promise later or buffer.

  • Adding spreads up

    The chain looks worse than it is. Fix → add squares, take the root.

  • Formula over judgment

    Shared risks ignored. Fix → use the risk register as well.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Estimates that set a key date.
  • Work with a known worst case.
  • Before promising a date to a sponsor.
  • When someone says “pretty sure”.

Skip it when…

  • Small, familiar work where the range is narrow and nothing depends on it.

8Self-check quiz

Best 22, likely 25, worst 34. PERT average?

(22 + 100 + 34) ÷ 6 = 26.

Why is the expected value later than the most likely?

The worst case is further from the likely case than the best case is: the estimate leans late.

What chance did the 25-day plan have?

About 1 in 3 (31 %).

Two activities in a row: 26 ± 2 and 10.5 ± 0.8. What is the spread of the pair?

√(2² + 0.8²) ≈ 2.2, not 2.8.

Joost says “just tell the steering group 3 April, the odds will confuse them”. What does Sanne do?

Gives 3 April as the target and says, in one line, that it has about a 1 in 3 chance with no spare week, and what is ready if it slips.

try it

Take the estimate that sets your project's most important date. Ask its owner for best, likely and worst, and what would cause the worst. Work out the PERT average. What are the odds of the date you've promised?

remember!!

Say “70 % chance of rain”, not “rain at three”.
