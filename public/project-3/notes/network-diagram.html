What Waits for What?

TL;DR

Before a schedule has dates it needs order: which activities wait for which. Draw each activity as a box and each wait as an arrow: a network diagram. Most waits are finish-to-start (B can't start until A finishes). Some are mandatory (physics, law), some discretionary (a preference you could change), some external (a delivery, an inspector). A bar chart without these links looks like a plan, but can't answer the only question that matters when something slips: what moves with it?

  • Family: by when?
  • Lives in: a sheet of sticky notes and arrows, then the scheduling tool
  • Pairs with: the WBS, critical path, last responsible moment

1The situation Larder, 12–19 February 2026

This is Sanne's mistake for this collection. On 12 February she turns the builder's quote into a tidy bar chart: twelve bars, each with a start and an end, from 2 March to 3 April. Joost looks at it and asks the obvious question: “if the electrical inspection is a week late, when do we get the store?” Sanne looks at the bars and can't say. Nothing on the chart says which bars wait for the inspection and which don't.

  • Bars show when, not why then.
  • Without links, every slip has to be worked out by hand, and usually isn't.
  • Waits on things outside the project don't appear at all.

2Real-life analogy getting dinner ready

For a dinner party, the oven must be hot before the roast goes in: that's physics. The table can be laid any time: you usually do it before cooking, but that's a choice. And the guests arrive at eight because the invitation says so: that's outside your kitchen. A cook who knows which waits are which can rearrange the evening when the oven is slow. A cook with a list of times can't.

3How it solves it boxes, arrows, and the kind of each wait

On 19 February Sanne and Kees, the builder's site manager, spend two hours at the empty unit with sticky notes. Twelve activities go on the wall, A to L, and arrows between them. Most are finish-to-start. Each wait gets a kind. Mandatory: walls after first fix; the cold room energised only after the electrical inspection; nothing stands on the floor until the screed has cured (Kees counts the curing inside D's seven days). Discretionary: paint before shelving, so shelves don't get splashed; it could be changed if needed. External: the cold room can't be built before it arrives on 27 March. Now Joost's question has an answer: the inspection sits on a chain that runs straight to the handover. A week's slip there is a week's slip at the end.

  1. List the activities from the work packages

    Verbs now: build, test, paint.

  2. For each, ask: what must be finished first?

    Arrows, not dates.

  3. Mark each wait: mandatory, discretionary, external

    Only discretionary ones can be changed cheaply.

  4. Add lags where something must wait in between

    Curing, drying, notice periods.

  5. Check every activity has a way in and a way out

    No loose ends.

  6. Only then put durations and dates on it

    The next note.

Vocabulary for the exam

Dependency
A relationship where one activity waits for another.
Finish-to-start (FS)
B can't start until A finishes. The most common.
Start-to-start (SS)
B can't start until A starts.
Mandatory dependency
Required by physics, law or contract.
Discretionary dependency
Chosen because it's usually best; could be changed.
External dependency
A wait on something outside the project.
Lag
A required wait between two activities, such as curing time.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a few activities

  • Say what waits for what.
  • A sketch with arrows.
  • No tool.

Standard

Rotterdam's fit-out

  • A network of the predictive work, drawn with the supplier.
  • Kinds of wait marked.
  • External dates shown.

Heavy

large construction or engineering

  • A full precedence network in a scheduling tool.
  • All four dependency types, leads and lags.
  • Network checked for loose ends by a scheduler.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Bars without links

    Looks like a plan; can't answer “what if?”. Fix → draw the arrows first.

  • Loose ends

    An activity that leads nowhere. Fix → every box has a way in and out.

  • Treating a preference as a law

    No room to re-order when late. Fix → mark which waits are discretionary.

  • Forgetting outside waits

    Deliveries, inspectors, permits. Fix → draw them, with their dates.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any predictive work with more than a handful of steps.
  • Work with outside dates (deliveries, inspections).
  • Before promising a date.
  • When someone asks “what if X is late?”

Skip it when…

  • Adaptive work planned sprint by sprint: order the backlog instead.

8Self-check quiz

What kind of wait is “paint before shelving”?

Discretionary: usually best, but it could be changed.

And “cold room energised only after the inspection”?

Mandatory (by law).

What is a lag?

A required wait between activities, like the screed's curing time.

Why couldn't Sanne answer Joost from the bar chart?

It had no links, so it couldn't show which bars wait for the inspection.

The shelving supplier offers to deliver a week early. Does that help the handover?

No: shelving waits for paint, not for its delivery. The arrow, not the delivery, sets its start.

try it

Take the predictive part of your project. Write each activity on a sticky note and draw what waits for what. Mark each arrow M (mandatory), D (discretionary) or E (external). Which E arrows have no date on them yet?

remember!!

The oven before the roast; the guests at eight. Know which wait is which.
