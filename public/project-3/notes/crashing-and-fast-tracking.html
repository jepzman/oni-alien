a.k.a. schedule compression: crashing and fast-tracking

Can We Go Faster?

TL;DR

Two ways to shorten a schedule without cutting scope. Crashing: add money or people to activities on the critical path (overtime, weekend work, extra crews), cheapest days first. Fast-tracking: overlap activities that were planned in sequence, which is usually free but adds risk and rework. Both only help on the critical path, and both stop helping when another path becomes critical. Spend nothing on activities that aren't.

  • Lives in: a short table of options: days gained, cost per day, risk
  • Pairs with: critical path, escalation, change control

1The situation Larder, 23 March 2026

The inspector has re-booked, and the handover is forecast for 8 April. Within the hour, everyone has an idea. Joost: pay the plumber a bonus. Marco: have the cold-room fitters work Saturday. Tomás: start stocking in the finished half of the store. Ben, half joking: get a faster electrician. Some of these cost money and buy nothing. Sanne has two hours before her message to Joost.

  • Ideas for speed rarely start from the critical path.
  • Money spent off the path disappears.
  • Overlapping steps is free until it isn't.

2Real-life analogy late for a train

A family is late for a train. They can pay for a taxi instead of the bus: faster, and it costs money. Or they can pack in the car on the way instead of before leaving: faster and free, but something might get left behind. Either works only on the part of the journey that's actually making them late. A taxi doesn't help if the problem is the queue at the ticket office.

The taxiCrashing: the builder works the weekend of 28–29 March

3How it solves it test each idea against the critical path, then price the days

Sanne tests each idea against the red chain. The plumber: not critical, and finished two weeks ago. The cold-room fitters: their chain isn't late; working Saturday moves nothing. A faster electrician: the delay is the inspector's date, which no money moves. What can be shortened is the work after the inspection: paint, shelving and snagging, on the critical path. Two options. Crash: the builder works Saturday 28 and Sunday 29 March on paint and finishes: two days for €5,000, €2,500 a day, priced in February. Fast-track: start stocking the dry half while the chilled half is finished: two days, no cost, but pickers working among builders, which is how Zuid went wrong. She recommends crashing, keeps fast-tracking as a fallback, and notes the limit: after the weekend, the cold-room chain is critical too, so 3 April is as early as money can make it. Joost decides at 16:00 (change C-02).

  1. List every idea for going faster

    From everyone.

  2. Strike out anything off the critical path

    It buys nothing.

  3. Strike out anything outside your control

    The inspector's date.

  4. Price each remaining day

    Cost per day gained.

  5. Weigh fast-tracking's risk honestly

    Free isn't free if it causes rework.

  6. Check the new critical path after each step

    Stop when another path takes over.

Vocabulary for the exam

Schedule compression
Shortening the schedule without reducing scope.
Crashing
Adding resources (money, people, overtime) to critical activities.
Fast-tracking
Doing activities in parallel that were planned in sequence.
Cost per day
The price of each day gained by crashing.
Crash limit
The point where an activity can't be shortened further.
Rework
Work done again because something was done too early or wrong.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small team behind schedule

  • Ask what's on the critical path.
  • Overtime or re-ordering, agreed with the team.
  • No table.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Every idea tested against the critical path.
  • Days gained, cost per day, risk.
  • Decided by the sponsor over the threshold.

Heavy

large contracts

  • Formal time-cost analysis.
  • Acceleration clauses and claims.
  • Re-baselining after major compression.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Crashing off the path

    The plumber's bonus. Fix → critical path only.

  • Fast-tracking as free

    Rework and accidents. Fix → price the risk.

  • Crashing past the limit

    Another path is now critical. Fix → re-check after each step.

  • Pricing it on the day

    No crew available. Fix → ask for prices before you need them.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A forecast date is later than the promise.
  • When someone proposes paying to go faster.
  • Before cutting scope.
  • When a phase must finish before a fixed date.

Skip it when…

  • When scope can move instead and the sponsor prefers that: then it's a change, not compression.

8Self-check quiz

Crashing or fast-tracking: weekend work by the builder?

Crashing.

Crashing or fast-tracking: stocking while the store is being finished?

Fast-tracking.

Why did paying the plumber buy nothing?

Plumbing wasn't on the critical path (and was already finished).

€5,000 for two days. What's the cost per day?

€2,500.

After the weekend work, Femke asks if more money could open the store on 2 April. What does Sanne answer?

No: the cold-room chain, held by its delivery on 27 March, also ends on 3 April. It's now critical too.

try it

Take a deadline your project is at risk of missing. List every idea for going faster. Strike out those not on the critical path. Price each remaining day, and write the risk of each overlap.

remember!!

A taxi on the wrong leg of the journey doesn't catch the train.
