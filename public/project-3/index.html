What, and By When

Volume 3 of The Project Manager's Notebook: the Scope and Schedule domains of the PMBOK Guide (eighth edition), all nine of their processes. 15 notes in four collections: pinning down what will be delivered, estimating how big it is, turning work into dates, and holding scope and dates where they were promised. Every number comes from one worked example, the Rotterdam launch, and its template card is a page of the Rotterdam project file. Four notes go further with a going deeper chapter: the work breakdown structure, three-point estimates, the critical path and schedule tracking.

What, exactly? pin down what will be delivered, and what won't

  1. 01How Will We Pin It Down?Decide, before you start, how scope and dates will be defined, detailed, measured and changed.p. 01
  2. 02What Do They Actually Need?Draw needs out of the people who will live with the result, and trace each one to something you'll deliver and check.p. 02
  3. 03What's In, and What's Out?One page that names the deliverables, how each will be accepted, and what is deliberately left out.p. 03
  4. 04How Do We Break It Down? + goes deeperBreak the whole result into smaller deliverables until each piece can be owned, estimated and checked.p. 04

How big is it? estimate honestly, as a range, with the people who do the work

  1. 05How Long, Roughly?Three ways to estimate: from a similar past job, from a rate, or by adding up the parts.p. 05
  2. 06How Sure Are We? + goes deeperAsk for the best case, the likely case and the worst case, and turn them into a range with odds.p. 06
  3. 07Who Should Guess?Let the people who do the work estimate, independently first, so nobody's number anchors the rest.p. 07

By when? turn work and estimates into dates you can defend

  1. 08What Waits for What?Draw which activities wait for which, including waits on things outside the project.p. 08
  2. 09What Decides the Finish Date? + goes deeperThe longest chain of dependent activities sets the end date. Find it, and watch it.p. 09
  3. 10How Much Can This Slip?Float is how late an activity can be without moving the end; a buffer is slack you put in on purpose.p. 10
  4. 11How Do We Show the Dates?A milestone chart for the people who decide, a Gantt chart for the people who do the work.p. 11
  5. 12Can We Go Faster?Two ways to shorten a schedule: pay to speed up critical work, or overlap work that was meant to follow.p. 12

Holding the line keep dates and scope where they were promised, or move them on purpose

  1. 13Are We Still on Time? + goes deeperTrack predictive work by its milestones and float, adaptive work by a burn-up, and forecast both every week.p. 13
  2. 14Is This What We Asked For?The people who asked for each deliverable formally accept it against criteria agreed in advance.p. 14
  3. 15Where Did That Come From?Small, unapproved additions eat the schedule quietly. Make every addition a decision.p. 15

★The nine project ratings what a good project practice is judged on

Every note scores the pattern from 1 (poor) to 5 (great) on the same nine things, comparing an app without the pattern to the same app with it.

Value focus
Keeps the project aimed at the benefit, not at the paperwork.
Predictability

Risk reduction
Trouble is found while it is still cheap.
Transparency
Anyone can see where the project really is.
Stakeholder confidence

Team ownership

Adaptability

Quality built in

first law

A date is only as good as the list of work under it and the longest chain through that work. Find both before you promise.
