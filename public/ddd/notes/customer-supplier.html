Customer–Supplier & Conformist

TL;DR

When one context depends on another, decide which kind of dependency it is. Customer–supplier: the downstream team's needs go into the upstream team's planning, interfaces are agreed and protected by contract tests. Conformist: the downstream simply adopts the upstream's model, because it can't influence it (a big vendor, a legacy system) or doesn't need to. Both work; not knowing which you're in doesn't.

  • Family: context relationship
  • From: Evans (2003), ch. 14
  • Decides: who has a say in the interface
  • Pairs with: contract testing, anti-corruption layer

1The problem why do we even need this?

Two teams depend on each other, but nobody has agreed who can ask whom for what.

  • Requests sit in another team's backlog for months.
  • The downstream team hacks around missing data.
  • The upstream team changes things without knowing who uses them.

2Real-life analogy the bakery and the supermarket

A restaurant chain asks its bakery for a new bread size, and the bakery puts it in its plan: the chain is a valued customer. A corner café buys standard loaves at the supermarket: it can't ask for anything, so it conforms and plans its sandwiches around what's on the shelf.

  • The chain and bakery are customer–supplier.
  • The café is conformist.
  • Both work, because each knows where it stands.

3How it solves it name the relationship, then act like it

Customer–supplier needs upstream goodwill: management must make serving downstream teams part of the upstream team's job. Conformist needs a decent upstream model: if it's poor, conforming spreads the mess, so put an anti-corruption layer in between.

  1. Find the direction

    Which context's changes affect the other? That one is upstream.

  2. Ask about influence

    Can the downstream team realistically change the upstream's plans? If yes, customer–supplier is possible.

  3. For customer–supplier

    Downstream needs go into upstream planning; agree interfaces; downstream writes contract tests that run in upstream's pipeline.

  4. For conformist

    Use the upstream model directly; keep a thin wrapper so a later ACL is possible.

  5. Check the model quality

    If conforming would spread a confusing model through your code, translate instead (ACL).

  6. Write it on the context map

    So everyone knows the deal, and revisit it when teams or vendors change.

Vocabulary for the exam

Customer–supplier
Upstream plans with downstream's needs in mind; interfaces are negotiated.
Conformist
Downstream adopts the upstream model without translation or influence.
Upstream / downstream
Who affects whom when they change.
Consumer-driven contract
A test written by the consumer that the provider must keep passing.
Veto
A downstream's power to block breaking changes; customer–supplier gives some.
Separate ways
Choosing not to integrate at all when it costs more than it gives.

import { pactWith } from "jest-pact";

pactWith({ consumer: "ordering", provider: "pricing" }, (provider) => {
  test("a quote includes the discount per line", async () => {
    await provider.addInteraction({
      uponReceiving: "a quote for a basket",
      withRequest: { method: "POST", path: "/quotes" },
      willRespondWith: { status: 200, body: {
        lines: [{ sku: "BIKE-42", price: { amount: 49900, currency: "EUR" },

    });
    // ... call the client and assert
  });
});

tools: consumer-driven contract tests (Pact, Spring Cloud Contract), a shared roadmap or planning ritual between the two teams, and the context map to record which relationship applies.

then & now still core

Then Evans, 2003

Evans named these as patterns of team relationships: customer–supplier when the upstream team can be persuaded to care, conformist when it can't, both with notes on politics and acceptance tests.

Now 2026

Unchanged in substance. Consumer-driven contract testing (Pact, 2013) made the “acceptance tests” part practical, and the rise of SaaS made conformist the everyday relationship with vendors.

Verdict: Still core. Every team that depends on another is in one of these, whether it says so or not.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Clear expectations between teams.
  • Customer–supplier: downstream needs get planned, not ignored.
  • Conformist: no translation code at all.
  • Contract tests catch breaking changes before release.

What we lose

  • Customer–supplier needs upstream time and goodwill.
  • Conformist lets another model shape your code.
  • Politics: priorities between teams must be settled by someone.
  • Relationships change when teams or vendors do.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Customer–supplier in name only

    The upstream team never actually plans for downstream needs. Fix → make serving downstream part of the upstream team's goals, with a manager who arbitrates.

  • Conforming to a mess

    A legacy model's odd names and codes spread through the new system. Fix → conform only to good models; otherwise add an anti-corruption layer.

  • No contract tests

    The upstream team “promised” not to break things, and did. Fix → consumer-driven contracts in the upstream's pipeline.

  • Assuming influence over vendors

    Waiting for a SaaS vendor to add a field. Fix → with vendors you are conformist (or ACL); plan accordingly.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any dependency between two teams' contexts.
  • A downstream team that keeps waiting on, or being broken by, an upstream one.
  • Integrating a vendor or legacy system (usually conformist or ACL).

Skip it when…

  • Two contexts owned by one team (just change both).
  • When integration costs more than it gives: choose separate ways.

8Self-check quiz

What's the difference between customer–supplier and conformist?

In customer–supplier, the downstream's needs shape the upstream's plans. In conformist, the downstream adopts the upstream model with no say.

When is conformist a good choice?

When the upstream model is decent and you can't or don't need to influence it, such as a well-designed vendor API.

What protects a customer–supplier relationship technically?

Consumer-driven contract tests run in the upstream's pipeline.

What do you do if the upstream model is poor and you can't change it?

Put an anti-corruption layer between it and your model.

what would you do?Your Ordering team has waited four months for the Pricing team to add a field. Pricing says their roadmap is set by another director. What do you do?

Escalate it as a relationship question, not a ticket: either make it customer–supplier (a shared planning slot and someone who arbitrates priorities), or accept conformist and work around it properly (compute it in an ACL from data you do get), and record the decision on the context map.

try it

For one dependency your team has on another team, write down: who is upstream, whether you can influence their plans, and what happens when they change something. Then name the relationship and add one contract test for the field you rely on most.

remember!!

Know whether you're the restaurant chain or the corner café, and plan like it.
