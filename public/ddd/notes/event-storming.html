Event Storming

TL;DR

Put the people who know the process and the people who build it in one room with a very long wall. Everyone writes domain events on orange notes, in the past tense (“Order Placed”), and sticks them on a timeline. Then add the commands, actors, policies and hot spots. In a few hours you see the whole flow, the gaps, the arguments and the likely context borders.

  • Family: discovery workshop
  • Invented by: Alberto Brandolini (2013)
  • Takes: 2–4 hours to a few days
  • Finds: events, language, borders, hot spots

1The problem why do we even need this?

Interviews one department at a time produce long documents, and nobody sees the whole process until it is built.

  • Each department describes only its own step.
  • The rules between steps (“refund only after the return is checked”) fall through the gaps.
  • Disagreements between departments surface in testing or in production.

2Real-life analogy planning a wedding on the wall

A family plans a wedding with sticky notes on a wall: “venue booked”, “invitations sent”, “replies received”, “seating plan drawn”, in the order they happen. Aunt Maria immediately sees the seating plan can't come before the replies. The argument happens on the wall, months early, not on the day.

  • Each note is a domain event, in the past tense.
  • The wall is the timeline.
  • Aunt Maria's objection is a hot spot.

3How it solves it a timeline of the business, in orange

There are three common formats. Big picture: the whole business, many departments, to find borders and problems. Process level: one process in detail, with commands, policies and read models. Design level: down to aggregates and the code. Start big, then zoom in.

  1. Invite the right people

    Domain experts from each step, developers, a product owner, and one facilitator. Eight to fifteen people.

  2. Chaotic exploration

    Everyone writes orange events in the past tense and sticks them roughly in time order. No discussion yet.

  3. Enforce the timeline

    Sort the notes together, remove duplicates, and argue about order. The arguments are the gold.

  4. Mark hot spots

    Every question, conflict or “it depends” becomes a pink note. Don't solve them yet.

  5. Add commands, actors and policies

    What triggers each event, who does it, and which automatic rules connect events.

  6. Draw the borders

    Where the language or the experts change, draw a line: candidate bounded contexts. Photograph everything.

Vocabulary for the exam

Domain event
Something that happened that the business cares about, in the past tense (orange).
Command
A request to do something, which may cause an event (blue).
Actor
The person or role that issues a command (small yellow).
Policy
An automatic reaction: “whenever X, then Y” (lilac).
Hot spot
A question, risk or disagreement (pink, at an angle).
Read model
The information someone needs to decide (green).

tools: a long roll of paper, orange, blue, yellow, lilac and pink sticky notes, and markers in person; Miro, Mural or FigJam templates remotely; Brandolini's book and the DDD Crew's EventStorming glossary for the colours.

then & now matters more now

Then Brandolini, 2013 (not in Evans)

The 2003 book had no workshop format. Evans described “knowledge crunching”: developers and experts talking, sketching and refining a model over weeks, often with UML diagrams.

Now 2026

Event storming (2013) and domain storytelling (2021) are now the standard ways to start. They put events, not classes, at the centre, which also suits event-driven systems. Remote boards made them possible for distributed teams.

Verdict: A newer tool for an old goal. It made DDD's first step faster and much more inclusive.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • The whole process visible in one afternoon.
  • Gaps and conflicts found before code exists.
  • Shared language emerges from the notes.
  • Candidate borders for contexts and teams.

What we lose

  • Getting the right people in one room is hard.
  • Needs a good facilitator, or it turns into a meeting.
  • The wall isn't documentation; someone must write it up.
  • Remote sessions are harder to keep lively.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Only developers in the room

    The wall shows what developers think the business does. Fix → no domain experts, no session; reschedule rather than guess.

  • Events in the wrong tense

    Notes say “create order” or “order screen”. Fix → past tense, business words: “Order Placed”.

  • Solving hot spots on the spot

    Two hours lost on one pink note. Fix → mark it, move on, and pick the top hot spots at the end.

  • The wall dies

    Photos sit in a chat thread nobody opens. Fix → turn it into a glossary, a context map and the first event names in code within a week.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Starting a new product or a major rewrite.
  • Before drawing bounded contexts or service borders.
  • A process that crosses several departments and keeps breaking.

Skip it when…

  • A small, well-understood feature inside one team.
  • When the experts can't attend (use interviews and domain storytelling instead).

8Self-check quiz

What colour and tense are domain events in event storming?

Orange, in the past tense: “Payment Taken”.

What is a hot spot?

A pink note marking a question, conflict or risk that needs an answer later.

What is a policy?

An automatic rule connecting events to commands: “whenever payment is taken, reserve stock”.

How does event storming help find bounded contexts?

Where the language or the experts change along the timeline, there is probably a border.

what would you do?Your team wants to run event storming for a new returns process, but the warehouse and finance leads “don't have time”. Developers suggest doing it without them. What do you do?

Don't storm without experts: the result would be developers' guesses. Shorten it to a focused two-hour process-level session, book it with the warehouse and finance leads' managers, and prepare the room so their time is used well.

try it

Run a one-hour mini event storm on a process you know (ordering food, onboarding a colleague). Write every event in the past tense on its own note, put them in time order, mark three hot spots, and draw one line where the language changes.

remember!!

Orange notes, past tense, one long wall: the arguments you have there are the bugs you won't ship.
