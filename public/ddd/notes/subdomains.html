Core, Supporting & Generic Subdomains

TL;DR

Split the business into subdomains and sort them: core (what makes you different, so build it carefully with your best people), supporting (needed and specific to you, but not special, so keep it simple) and generic (every business has it, so buy or rent it). Then spend your modelling effort where it makes a difference.

  • Family: strategic design
  • From: Evans (2003), ch. 15 “Distillation”
  • Modern tools: core domain charts, Wardley maps
  • Pairs with: build vs buy, bounded context

1The problem why do we even need this?

Teams naturally polish what is fun or familiar, not what the business competes on.

  • Months go into a home-made login, invoicing or search engine.
  • The rules that make customers choose you are rushed and tangled.
  • Every part gets the same “full DDD” treatment, or none does.

2Real-life analogy the bakery and its suppliers

A famous bakery makes its own sourdough from a secret starter: that's why people queue. It buys flour and boxes, and uses a standard card terminal and accounting package. Baking boxes in-house would be effort with no customer benefit.

  • The bread is the core domain.
  • Flour and boxes are supporting: needed, bought or kept simple.
  • The card terminal is generic: everybody has the same one.

3How it solves it sort, then spend effort accordingly

A subdomain is a part of the business (the problem space). A bounded context is a part of the software (the solution space). They often line up one to one, but they don't have to.

  1. List the business capabilities

    What does the company do, in verbs: sell, price, ship, bill, support, recommend.

  2. Ask “does this set us apart?”

    Would a customer pick us because of it? If yes, it's a candidate for core.

  3. Ask “could we buy this?”

    If a SaaS or library does it well enough, it's generic.

  4. Place each on a chart

    Differentiation on one axis, complexity on the other. Talk it through with the business.

  5. Match effort to the type

    Core: rich model, tests, senior people. Supporting: simple code, maybe CRUD. Generic: buy, integrate behind an adapter.

  6. Revisit yearly

    Today's core can become generic (search, recommendations) as the market catches up.

Vocabulary for the exam

Domain
The whole area of business the software serves.
Subdomain
One part of it, such as pricing or shipping.
Core domain
The subdomain that gives the business its edge.
Supporting subdomain
Specific to the business, needed, but not a differentiator.
Generic subdomain
A solved problem every business shares.
Distillation
Evans's term for separating the core from everything else.

then & now matters more now

Then Evans, 2003

Evans put “distillation” near the end of the book, in part IV, so many readers never reached it. He described the core domain, generic subdomains and a “domain vision statement” to keep the team focused.

Now 2026

Today it's usually the first thing taught. With SaaS and open source for almost every generic capability, “buy the generic, build the core” is a daily decision. Core domain charts, Wardley maps and team topologies use the same idea.

Verdict: More useful now than in 2003, because there is so much more you can buy instead of build.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Effort goes where it matters to customers.
  • Less code to own: generic parts are bought.
  • Clear reason for where to use rich modelling and where not.
  • A shared picture with the business of what really counts.

What we lose

  • Hard conversations: someone's favourite system is “generic”.
  • Vendor dependence for generic parts.
  • The sort changes over time, and needs revisiting.
  • Not every company agrees on what its core is.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Everything is core

    Every team claims its area is strategic, so nothing is prioritised. Fix → ask “would a customer choose us for this?” and accept a short core list.

  • Building generic things

    A custom identity system or CMS eats a year. Fix → buy or use open source; build only the glue.

  • Full DDD everywhere

    Aggregates and events for a settings page. Fix → use rich models in the core; plain CRUD is fine elsewhere.

  • A one-time exercise

    The chart from three years ago still says search is core. Fix → revisit at least yearly, and when the strategy changes.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Planning where to put your strongest people and most care.
  • Deciding build vs buy for a new capability.
  • Before splitting a system into contexts or services.

Skip it when…

  • A tiny product where everything is the core and the team is three people.
  • Prototypes whose business model isn't known yet (learn first).

8Self-check quiz

What are the three kinds of subdomain?

Core (sets you apart), supporting (specific to you but not special) and generic (solved problems every business has).

Where should a team use its richest modelling?

In the core domain.

What's the usual approach for a generic subdomain?

Buy it, rent it as a service or use open source, behind an adapter.

What's the difference between a subdomain and a bounded context?

A subdomain is part of the business problem; a bounded context is part of the software solution. They often, but not always, line up.

what would you do?Your team wants to build its own feature-flag service and search engine “to keep control”. Meanwhile the delivery-slot pricing, which the CEO says wins customers, has no tests. What do you propose?

Classify the subdomains with the business. Search and feature flags are generic: use a product or open source behind an adapter. Delivery-slot pricing is core: move the strongest developers there and give it a proper model and tests.

try it

List eight to ten capabilities of a product you know. For each, answer two questions with someone from the business: “would a customer choose us for this?” and “could we buy it?”. Place them on a two-axis chart and write one line on how much effort each deserves.
