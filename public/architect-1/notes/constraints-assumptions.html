Constraints vs Assumptions

TL;DR

Split everything you “know” about a project into two lists. Constraints are fixed rules you must design within: a date, a contract, a law, a budget, a skill set; record where each comes from and who could change it. Assumptions are things you believe but haven't checked; give each an owner, a test and a deadline. Plans fail on assumptions written as if they were facts.

1The situation Larder, the kick-off document

Larder's kick-off document lists “facts”: Rotterdam opens 27 April (true: the store lease is signed), StockPilot stays until June 2027 (true: the contract), and StockPilot's API can serve six cities. That last one is a belief, written exactly like the facts, and the Rotterdam plan rests on it. A spike in November finds the API allows 10 requests per second per account. Three weeks of plan, built on a guess.

  • Beliefs written as facts never get checked.
  • Real constraints are sometimes softer than they look (“we must use X”: says who?).
  • When an assumption fails late, it fails during delivery, the most expensive moment.

2Real-life analogy planning a hike

Planning a mountain hike, some things are fixed: the park gate closes at 20:00, the permit is for six people. You plan around them. Other things you believe: the bridge on the trail is open, there's water at the hut. A good hiker calls the ranger about the bridge and carries water anyway.

3How it solves it two lists, two different treatments

For every statement ask: is this given, or do we believe it? Constraints get a source (the lease, the contract, GDPR) and the name of whoever could relax them. Assumptions get an owner, a test and a date. Every assumption either becomes a fact, or becomes a risk or a design change.

  1. List what you “know”

    Dates, budgets, vendors, laws, skills, volumes, user behaviour, partner capabilities.

  2. Ask “given or believed?”

    Given → constraint. Believed, even strongly → assumption.

  3. Challenge the constraints

    “Says who?” Some are preferences. Record the source and who could change it.

  4. Give every assumption a test

    An owner, how to check (spike, vendor call, data), and the date it must be known by.

  5. Rank by impact if wrong

    Test first the assumptions that would sink the plan.

  6. Review weekly

    Move checked assumptions to facts, failed ones to risks or decisions (ADRs).

Vocabulary for the exam

Constraint
A fixed rule the design must obey: law, date, contract, budget.
Technical constraint
A fixed technical choice: “must run in the EU region”.
Organisational constraint
Team size, skills, budget, process.
Assumption
Something believed true but not yet checked.
Validated assumption
Checked and confirmed: now a fact.
RAID log
Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies: a common project log.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Beliefs get checked before the plan depends on them.
  • False constraints exposed: “we must use X” sometimes turns out to be a preference.
  • Clear design space: the real limits are written down, with their source.
  • Earlier bad news: a failed assumption in November beats one in April.
  • Honest plans: stakeholders see what the plan rests on.

What we lose

  • Upkeep: the log needs a weekly look.
  • Uncomfortable: challenging a “constraint” can step on toes.
  • Testing costs: spikes and vendor calls take time.
  • Can't list everything: unknown unknowns remain.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Assumptions written as facts

    “The API supports six cities.” Nobody asks how we know. Fix → for every statement ask “given or believed?”

  • Never testing

    The assumptions list is long and every status is “open”. Fix → owner, test and date for each; test the high-impact ones first.

  • Accepting fake constraints

    “We must use microservices” turns out to be one person's preference. Fix → ask “says who?” and record the source.

  • Logging, not acting

    A failed assumption is marked ✗ and the plan carries on. Fix → every failed assumption becomes a risk, a decision or a re-plan.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • The start of any project with dates, vendors, contracts or legal rules.
  • When the plan depends on a partner or vendor you don't control.
  • Before committing to a date or a budget.
  • Whenever someone says “obviously” or “surely”.

Skip it when…

  • Small, internal changes with nothing external.
  • When an existing RAID log already covers it: use that.
  • Trivial assumptions with no impact if wrong.

8Self-check quiz

What's the difference between a constraint and an assumption?

A constraint is a given rule you must design within; an assumption is something believed but not yet checked.

What four things does every assumption need?

An owner, a way to test it, a date to know it by, and its impact if wrong.

What do you record for each constraint?

Its source and who could change it.

What happens when an assumption fails?

It becomes a risk, a design decision (an ADR) or a re-plan, never just a ✗ in the log.

try it

Take your project's plan or kick-off deck and highlight every sentence that's a belief rather than a fact. Pick the one with the highest impact if wrong, and find a way to test it this week: a call, a spike, a query on real data.

remember!!

Plan around constraints. Test assumptions before the plan leans on them.
