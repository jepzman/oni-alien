Stakeholder Map

TL;DR

Before you design anything, list everyone who can affect the system or is affected by it: not just product and engineering, but legal, finance, operations, support, the people on the shop floor and outside vendors. Place each on a power × interest grid, decide how you'll work with each group (manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor), and revisit it monthly. Late surprises come from people nobody thought to ask.

  • Origin: Mendelow's power/interest grid (1991); stakeholder theory
  • Lives in: a one-page register next to the architecture docs
  • Pairs with: business drivers, decision rights (RACI), discovery workshops

1The situation Larder, week three

Ana's first design for the six-city platform is reviewed by Priya (product) and the tech leads. Everyone approves. Then, one by one, the people who weren't in the room find out. Lena, the data protection officer, says customer addresses can't go to the new analytics tool without a contract. Tomás, who runs the Noord dark store, says pickers wear gloves, so the new tap-to-confirm scanning flow won't work. Marco, the CFO, asks why the cloud estimate doubled. Three weeks of rework, all avoidable.

  • The architect talks to the people who are easy to reach: product and engineering.
  • Legal, finance and operations hear about it late, and late is when “no” is most expensive.
  • The people who use the system every day (pickers, couriers, support agents) are rarely asked at all.

2Real-life analogy planning a street party

To hold a street party you need a permit from the council: they hardly care, but they can cancel it. The organising committee cares and decides. The neighbours can't stop it, but they'll live with the noise, so you tell them early. The bus company only needs a notice that the route is closed. Same party, four very different conversations.

Translating it to the job

The council that issues the permitLegal, compliance, security, the board: high power, low day-to-day interest

3How it solves it list them, place them, plan the conversation

The map is cheap: one afternoon in week one. Brainstorm names (people, not departments), ask each person you meet “who else should I talk to?”, then place everyone by power (can they block, fund or change it?) and interest (how much does it change their day?).

  1. Brainstorm wide

    Users, buyers, operators, support, legal, security, finance, vendors, regulators, other teams. Write names, not job titles.

  2. Snowball

    End every early conversation with “who else should I talk to?” Add the answers.

  3. Place on the grid

    Power: can they block, fund or overrule? Interest: how much does this change their work?

  4. Plan the engagement

    For each group: what they need to hear, how often, in what format, and from whom.

  5. Find what each one fears

    One line each: “Lena fears a GDPR breach”, “Joost fears a slower picking line”.

  6. Revisit monthly

    People move quadrants: Lena's interest jumps the day personal data is involved.

Vocabulary for the exam

Stakeholder
Anyone who can affect, or is affected by, the system.
Power / interest grid
A 2×2 that places stakeholders by influence and concern.
Manage closely
High power, high interest: involve in decisions.
Keep satisfied
High power, low interest: short, early, targeted updates.
Keep informed
Low power, high interest: demos, notes, ask for feedback.
Engagement plan
How and how often you work with each stakeholder.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Fewer late vetoes: legal, finance and operations are in from week one.
  • Better requirements: the people who use the system tell you what really hurts.
  • The right message for each person: the CFO gets cost, the store gets workflow.
  • A shared list the whole team can use, not just the architect.
  • Trust: people who were asked are far more likely to support the result.

What we lose

  • Time: a day to build, an hour a month to maintain, plus the conversations.
  • Politics on paper: a written power ranking can offend if shared carelessly.
  • More voices mean more opinions to reconcile.
  • Easy to go stale: a map from month one misleads in month six.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Departments, not people

    “Legal” is on the map, but nobody knows which lawyer. Fix → write names; a department can't answer a question.

  • Only the loud and the senior

    The map has the C-suite and nobody who uses the system. Fix → add the operators: pickers, couriers, support agents. Visit them.

  • Sharing the grid as-is

    Someone sees themselves in “monitor” and is offended. Fix → share the engagement plan; keep the power ranking as a working note.

  • Draw once, never update

    A new regulation makes Lena critical; the map still says low interest. Fix → review monthly and at every big change of scope.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • The start of any project that crosses teams or departments.
  • Anything touching personal data, money, contracts or regulated work.
  • When you're new to an organisation and don't yet know who matters.
  • Before a big decision that will need sponsors to back it.

Skip it when…

  • A small change inside one team that owns everything it touches.
  • A short spike or prototype with no users yet.
  • When the full map exists already: update it instead of starting again.

8Self-check quiz

What do the two axes of the grid measure?

Power: can they block, fund or change it. Interest: how much it affects their work.

Lena (DPO) has high power and low interest. How do you work with her?

Keep her satisfied: short, early, targeted updates, especially before anything touches personal data.

Why write names instead of departments?

A department can't answer questions or sign off; a named person can.

Why revisit the map monthly?

People change quadrants as the project changes: interest jumps when it touches their area.

try it

For your current project, list 15 stakeholders by name in ten minutes, including at least three people who use or operate the system every day. Place them on the grid. Pick the one person in “keep satisfied” you've never spoken to, and book 20 minutes with them this week.

remember!!

The people who can say no and the people who have to live with it are rarely the people in the room.
