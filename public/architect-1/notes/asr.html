Architecturally Significant Requirements

TL;DR

Most requirements fit into almost any architecture. A few don't: they're costly to change later, touch many parts, carry high value or risk, or demand an unusual quality. Those are architecturally significant requirements (ASRs). Find them early, often buried in support tickets and side remarks rather than the backlog, write each one with a measure and an owner, and design for them first. The rest will fit inside.

  • Origin: Len Bass et al., Software Architecture in Practice; Eoin Woods, Rozanski
  • Lives in: a short list at the top of the architecture overview
  • Pairs with: business drivers, quality attribute scenarios, ADRs

1The situation Larder, 340 stories

Larder's backlog has 340 user stories, nearly all marked “high”. The loudest one, ML-powered recipe ideas, gets three design meetings. Meanwhile ticket #4127 from the Noord store says the Wi-Fi drops about once a week and pickers stand idle for 20 minutes. That one quietly decides how the picker app must be built, and nobody in the design meetings has read it.

  • A flat backlog hides which requirements shape the structure and which are decoration.
  • The significant ones often come from operations, legal or support, not from product.
  • Found late, they mean rework of the foundations, not a new feature.

2Real-life analogy the lift shaft

When you design a house, most wishes can change any time: paint, curtains, taps. A few decide the structure: a lift for grandma needs a shaft, a recording studio needs a stronger floor, a basement must be dug first. Mention those after the concrete is poured and they cost ten times more, or can't happen at all.

3How it solves it filter, then design for the few

Run every candidate through four questions. If any answer is yes, it's a candidate ASR. Then hunt where they hide: support tickets, incident reports, contracts, legal advice, operations complaints, and the answers to “what would make this project fail?”

  1. Gather widely

    Backlog, drivers, incident reviews, support tickets, contracts, legal and security advice, stakeholder interviews.

  2. Apply the four questions

    Costly to change? Touches many parts? High value or risk? Tough or unusual quality? Any yes → candidate.

  3. Make each one measurable

    “Works offline” becomes “picker app keeps working 30 minutes without Wi-Fi”.

  4. Name the source and owner

    Where it came from (ticket #4127) and who can confirm it (Tomás).

  5. Keep the list short

    Usually 5–10. If everything is significant, nothing is.

  6. Design for them first

    Each ASR should be traceable to a decision (an ADR) and, later, a test.

Vocabulary for the exam

ASR
Architecturally significant requirement: one that shapes the architecture.
Functional requirement
What the system does (“customer can reorder”).
Quality attribute
How well it does it (fast, available, secure).
Constraint
A fixed rule the design must obey.
Traceability
Linking each ASR to its source, decision and test.
Utility tree
A ranked tree of quality goals and scenarios, from ATAM.

tools: a short Markdown list is enough. Requirements tools (Jira, Azure DevOps) can tag ASRs with a label so they stand out; Structurizr and arc42 both have a place for them.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Focus: design effort goes to the few requirements that shape the system.
  • Hidden needs found early: offline stores, legal rules, peak days.
  • Simpler designs: everything else just has to fit inside.
  • Traceable decisions: each big choice points to the ASR it serves.
  • Better conversations: stakeholders see why their need changes the design.

What we lose

  • Judgement call: what counts as significant can be argued.
  • Digging takes time: tickets, incidents, contracts and interviews.
  • False confidence: a missed ASR is still missed.
  • Can be gamed: everyone wants their request labelled significant.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Only reading the backlog

    The significant needs live in tickets, incidents and contracts. Fix → read support, incident and legal sources, and ask operations.

  • Everything is significant

    The list has 40 items and decides nothing. Fix → apply the four questions strictly; aim for 5–10.

  • Vague ASRs

    “Must be scalable” is on the list. Fix → make each one measurable with a quality attribute scenario.

  • Found, then forgotten

    The list exists but no decision points to it. Fix → link each ASR to an ADR and, later, a fitness function.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A new system, a rebuild, or a big change of scale or market.
  • When the backlog is long and flat.
  • Before choosing datastores, boundaries, hosting or vendors.
  • When incidents keep coming from the same structural weakness.

Skip it when…

  • Small features inside an established architecture.
  • Prototypes meant to be thrown away.
  • When the list exists: review it at each big change instead.

8Self-check quiz

Name the four questions that flag an ASR.

Is it costly to change later? Does it touch many parts? Is it high value or high risk? Is it an unusual or demanding quality?

Where do ASRs often hide?

In support tickets, incident reports, contracts, legal advice and operations complaints, not just the backlog.

Why is “must be scalable” a poor ASR?

It isn't measurable; turn it into a scenario with a load, a context and a response measure.

Why keep the list to five to ten?

If everything is significant, nothing guides the design.

try it

Read the last 30 support tickets or incident reports for your system. List every need that the current architecture makes hard to meet. Run each through the four questions. Did any of them make it into your backlog?
