Quality Attribute Scenarios

TL;DR

Words like fast, available, secure, scalable mean different things to everyone. Rewrite each one as a quality attribute scenario: a source sends a stimulus to part of the system (the artifact) in a given environment; the system gives a response that you can check with a response measure. Now “fast” is a sentence you can agree on, design for and test.

  • Origin: SEI, Bass, Clements & Kazman; used in ATAM (2000)
  • Lives in: a scenario table next to the ASRs

1The situation Larder, “fast and highly available”

Larder's old requirements document says the system “must be fast and highly available”. Priya reads that as under two seconds, Ben as under 200 ms, Marco as faster than the competition, Eli as fast on a normal day. On King's Day 2025 checkout timed out for 40 minutes, and in the review, everyone could argue the requirement had been met.

  • Adjectives can't be designed for, tested or signed off.
  • The hard part, under what conditions?, is always missing.
  • Without a measure there's no way to tell when you've done enough.

2Real-life analogy the car spec sheet

No carmaker prints “a fast car” on the spec sheet. They print 0–100 km/h in 7.5 seconds, measured from standstill, flooring the pedal, with two people on board, on a dry road. The stimulus, the conditions and the measure are all there, so a magazine can test it and prove it wrong.

3How it solves it six parts, one testable sentence

The six parts force the missing questions: who triggers it, how much, to which part, under what conditions, and how we'll know. Write two or three per important quality; the environment (“on King's Day”, “during a deploy”, “with one zone down”) is usually where the real requirement hides.

  1. Pick the qualities that matter

    Use the drivers and ASRs: performance, availability, modifiability, security, privacy…

  2. Write the stimulus and source

    Who or what triggers it, and how much: “3,000 checkouts/s from customers”.

  3. State the environment

    Normal day, peak, during a deploy, a zone down, a new city being added.

  4. Define the response and measure

    What should happen and the number that proves it: p95, %, minutes, euros, days.

  5. Rank them

    With stakeholders, rate each by business value and technical difficulty (H/M/L).

  6. Make them tests

    High-value scenarios become load tests, chaos drills and fitness functions.

Vocabulary for the exam

Stimulus
The event that arrives: a request, a failure, a change request.
Source
Who or what produces the stimulus.
Artifact
The part of the system that is stimulated.
Environment
The conditions: normal, peak, degraded, during a deploy.
Response measure
The number that shows the response was good enough.
p95
95 % of requests are faster than this.

QAS-1 Performance (ASR-3)                               value H · difficulty M
  Source:      customers on King's Day
  Stimulus:    3,000 checkout requests/s (5× normal peak) for 2 hours
  Artifact:    checkout and payment path
  Environment: normal operation; one of two app servers lost
  Response:    order confirmed, payment taken exactly once
  Measure:     p95 < 800 ms · 0 lost or double-charged orders

QAS-2 Availability (ASR-2)                              value H · difficulty M
  Source:      the dark store network
  Stimulus:    store Wi-Fi drops
  Artifact:    picker app
  Environment: Friday peak, 40 open pick lists
  Response:    pickers keep picking; changes sync when Wi-Fi returns
  Measure:     30 min offline with no lost picks · sync done < 2 min after

QAS-3 Modifiability (ASR-1)                             value H · difficulty H
  Source:      operations team
  Stimulus:    “open Utrecht”
  Artifact:    stores, zones, hours, prices, VAT configuration
  Environment: normal week, no developer involved
  Response:    the city is set up and testable
  Measure:     ≤ 1 day of configuration · 0 code changes or deploys

tools: a Markdown table or the arc42 “quality requirements” section. k6, Gatling or Locust turn performance scenarios into tests; chaos tools (Chaos Mesh, AWS FIS) test availability scenarios.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • One meaning for “fast” and “available”, agreed by business and engineering.
  • Testable: scenarios become load tests, drills and fitness functions.
  • Know when to stop: the measure says when the design is good enough.
  • Conditions made explicit: peak days and failures stop being surprises.
  • Better trade-offs: ranked scenarios show which quality wins a conflict.

What we lose

  • Effort: good scenarios take discussion and real numbers.
  • False precision: early numbers are guesses and need revisiting.
  • Easy to write too many: dozens of low-value scenarios.
  • Hard for some qualities: usability or evolvability resist a single number.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Missing the environment

    “p95 under 800 ms”, but on which day, under what load? Fix → always state the conditions: peak, degraded, during a deploy.

  • Numbers from nowhere

    “99.999 %” because it sounds good. Fix → derive measures from drivers and data; ask what each nine costs.

  • Scenarios that never become tests

    A beautiful table nobody checks. Fix → turn the high-value ones into load tests, drills and fitness functions.

  • Only performance

    Ten latency scenarios, none for change, security or operations. Fix → cover the qualities your drivers care about, including modifiability.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any quality that drives the architecture: performance, availability, security, change.
  • When requirements say “fast”, “robust” or “scalable” with no numbers.
  • Before choosing between options with different quality trade-offs.
  • As input to an architecture review (ATAM, risk-storming).

Skip it when…

  • Qualities nobody is worried about for this system.
  • Tiny internal tools where “good enough” is obvious.
  • When you can't get any data yet: write a hypothesis and revisit after a spike.

8Self-check quiz

Name the six parts of a quality attribute scenario.

Source, stimulus, artifact, environment, response, response measure.

Which part most often hides the real requirement?

The environment: peak day, degraded mode, during a deploy.

Write “the picker app must be reliable” as a scenario.

When store Wi-Fi drops during Friday peak, pickers keep picking; 30 minutes offline with no lost picks, synced within 2 minutes after.

Why rank scenarios by value and difficulty?

So the team focuses on high-value, hard ones first and knows which quality wins a conflict.

try it

Find one adjective in your project's requirements (“fast”, “secure”, “scalable”). Rewrite it as a six-part scenario. Then ask two stakeholders separately what the response measure should be. If their numbers differ, you've just found a conversation that needed to happen.

remember!!

Not “fast”: who, how much, where, under what conditions, measured how.
