Domain Discovery (Event Storming)

TL;DR

Get the people who do the work and the people who build the software in one room with a long paper roll and orange sticky notes. Everyone writes the events that happen in the business, in the past tense (“order placed”, “item out of stock”), and puts them on the wall in time order. Gaps, arguments and question marks become hot spots. In a few hours you get a shared picture of the domain, the problems nobody owns, and the first hints of where the boundaries are.

  • Family: understanding the problem
  • Origin: Alberto Brandolini, “Introducing Event Storming” (2013)
  • Lives in: a paper roll, then photos and a digital board
  • Pairs with: bounded contexts, stakeholder map, discovery workshops

1The situation Larder, “how does an order work?”

Ana asks four people how an order works. Ben talks in tables, Joost in store releases, Tomás in scans and shelves, Sam in refunds. Each story is right, and none of them covers the whole. The hole in the middle is substitutions: when an item is out of stock, pickers guess a replacement, customers complain, and support refunds by hand. 6 % of orders end in a refund ticket, and nobody owns the problem.

  • Interviews one by one give you pieces, never the whole flow.
  • The worst problems sit between departments, where nobody looks.
  • Engineers learn the domain from the database, which is the least reliable source.

2Real-life analogy the storyboard

Before shooting a film, the crew pins a storyboard on the wall: every scene on a card, in order. The director, actors, camera crew and editor stand in front of the same wall. Missing scenes and scenes that don't make sense jump out, and someone sticks a question mark on them. Fixing a storyboard costs a pen; fixing a shot film costs a reshoot.

3How it solves it events first, everything else after

Start with chaotic exploration: everyone writes events at once, no discussion. Then enforce the timeline: sort them left to right, remove duplicates, argue. Then add hot spots, the commands that trigger events, the actors who issue them, and policies (“whenever X, then Y”).

  1. Invite the right mix

    6–12 people: the ones who do the work (store, support, ops) and the ones who build it. A facilitator who isn't the architect helps.

  2. Prepare the wall

    6+ metres of paper, plenty of orange stickies and markers. No chairs: people should stand and move.

  3. Chaotic exploration (30 min)

    Everyone writes events in the past tense and sticks them roughly in time order. No debate yet.

  4. Enforce the timeline (45 min)

    Walk left to right, merge duplicates, order them, and mark disagreements with a pink hot spot.

  5. Add commands, actors, policies

    Blue: what triggered it. Small yellow: who. Lilac or green: the rule that reacts to it.

  6. Harvest

    Photograph everything. Pick the top three hot spots, name owners, and sketch the first boundaries.

Vocabulary for the exam

Domain event
Something that happened that the business cares about, in the past tense.
Command
A request or decision that causes an event (“offer substitute”).
Actor
The person or role who issues a command.
Policy
A reaction rule: “whenever X happens, do Y”.
Hot spot
A pink note marking a conflict, question or pain.
Big-picture storming
The wide, whole-business version; design-level storming goes deeper on one area.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • A shared picture of the whole process in hours, not weeks of interviews.
  • Problems between departments (like substitutions) become visible and get owners.
  • Engineers learn the domain from the people who live it.
  • Boundary hints: clusters of events suggest bounded contexts.
  • Energy and trust: operations staff are heard, often for the first time.

What we lose

  • Expensive room: a half day of 10 busy people.
  • Needs facilitation: without it, the loudest voice wins.
  • Messy output: a wall of stickies needs harvesting into decisions.
  • Remote is harder: digital boards lose some of the energy.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Only engineers in the room

    The wall shows tables and APIs, not the business. Fix → half the room must be people who do the work.

  • Starting with the solution

    People write “call payment API” instead of “payment authorised”. Fix → events only, in the past tense, in business words.

  • No harvest

    A great afternoon, then the stickies go in the bin. Fix → photograph, list the top hot spots, name owners and dates before leaving.

  • The architect facilitates and talks

    Ana runs the session and steers it to her design. Fix → use a neutral facilitator so the architect can listen.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A new domain you don't know, or a team that doesn't share one picture.
  • Before drawing service or module boundaries.
  • When the worst problems sit between departments.
  • Onboarding a team onto a complex business process.

Skip it when…

  • A simple CRUD tool with obvious behaviour.
  • When the domain experts can't attend: you'll only storm your assumptions.
  • When you need detailed requirements for one screen: use a design-level session or a story map.

8Self-check quiz

Why write events in the past tense?

They describe facts the business cares about (“order placed”), not solutions or wishes.

What is a hot spot?

A pink note marking a conflict, gap, question or pain point on the timeline.

Who must be in the room?

People who do the work (operations, support, store) as well as the people who build the software.

What should you leave the room with?

Photos, the top hot spots with owners and dates, and first hints of boundaries.

try it

Run a 45-minute mini event storming on one process you know (for example, “a customer gets a refund”) with two colleagues from outside engineering. Silent writing for 10 minutes, sorting for 25, then mark the hot spots. Did you find a step nobody owns?

remember!!

Put the whole film on the wall before you shoot. The pink notes are where the money is.
