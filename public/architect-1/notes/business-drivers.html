Business Drivers & Goals

TL;DR

A business brief like “make it scalable” can't guide a design. Turn it into three to five business drivers: the outcomes the business needs, each with a measure, today's value and a target date. Agree them with the sponsors, write them on one page, and check every big design choice against them. Drivers tell you what to optimise, and just as usefully, what not to bother with.

1The situation Larder, the brief

Femke's brief to Ana is one sentence: “Make the platform scalable for six cities.” In the first meeting the engineers are already discussing Kubernetes, microservices and a million users. But six cities at Amsterdam's rate is about 40,000 orders a day, roughly five orders a second at peak: one Postgres database handles that easily. What really hurt was that opening Amsterdam's second dark store took five months of code changes. “Scalable” meant opening cities fast, not handling traffic.

  • Vague words (“scalable”, “modern”, “robust”) mean something different to everyone.
  • Engineers fill the gap with the interesting technical problem, not the business one.
  • With no measure, nobody can tell if the architecture worked.

2Real-life analogy the satnav

A satnav can't plan a route to “somewhere nice”. Type “Antwerp by 18:00, avoid tolls, fuel under €40” and it gives you one route, and every turn can be checked against it. The destination and the limits decide the road, not the other way round.

3How it solves it from one-line brief to measurable drivers

Ask “why?” until you reach something the business measures. “Scalable” → why? → “to open cities” → why does that hurt today? → “five months per store”. That's a driver: new city in eight weeks. Then ask what each driver asks of the design.

  1. Collect the goals

    Read the business plan, the investor deck and the OKRs. Ask the sponsor: “what must be true in two years?”

  2. Ask “why?” until it's measurable

    Keep asking until the answer has a number someone already tracks.

  3. Write each driver as a measure

    Name, today's value, target, date, owner: “express on time: 88 % → 95 % by Q4 2026, Joost”.

  4. Keep it to three to five

    More than five and they stop deciding anything. Rank them.

  5. Note the consequence

    For each driver, one line on what it means for the architecture.

  6. Get them signed off

    Sponsors agree the list and the order. Re-check at every big decision.

Vocabulary for the exam

Business goal
What the organisation wants to achieve (six cities).
Business driver
An outcome that must happen for the goal, with a measure.
Measure / KPI
The number that tells you if a driver is met.
Baseline
Today's value of the measure.
Target
The value to reach, by a date.
Five whys
Asking “why?” repeatedly to get from a vague wish to a real need.

Goal: six cities by end 2027 (Series B condition). Sponsors: Femke, Marco, Joost.

| # | Driver            | Measure                   | Today    | Target | By      |
|---|-------------------|---------------------------|----------|--------|---------|
| 1 | Open cities fast  | weeks, lease to launch    | 20       | ≤ 8    | Q3 2026 |
| 2 | Keep the promise  | express orders on time    | 88 %     | 95 %   | Q4 2026 |
| 3 | Unit economics    | cost per order            | €4.10    | €2.80  | Q4 2027 |
| 4 | Stay trusted      | lost / double-charged     | ~3/month | 0      | always  |

Owners: 1–2 Joost · 3 Marco · 4 Priya

What each means for the architecture
1 → a new city is configuration, not code (stores, zones, hours, prices, VAT)
2 → courier dispatch must react in seconds, not a 30-second cron job
3 → prefer simple, managed services; no platform team we can't afford
4 → order + payment must survive crashes without loss or duplicates

Not a driver (yet): handling more than ~10 orders/s. Don't design for it.

tools: one page in the repo or the wiki. Some teams use an OKR tool or a business model canvas as input; the drivers page links to them rather than copying.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • A shared definition of success that business and engineering both understand.
  • A tie-breaker for every design debate: which option serves driver 1?
  • Less over-engineering: “not a driver” is permission to keep things simple.
  • Measurable: you can tell, later, whether the architecture worked.
  • Sponsors stay engaged because the design speaks their language.

What we lose

  • Hard conversations: sponsors may disagree on the order.
  • Numbers can be guesses at first, and need correcting.
  • Risk of tunnel vision: what isn't on the list may still matter (security, accessibility).
  • Goals change: the page has to be maintained.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Drivers without numbers

    “Be fast”, “be reliable”, “be modern”. Fix → ask “why?” until there's a measure, a baseline and a target.

  • Twelve drivers

    Everything is a priority, so nothing decides anything. Fix → three to five, ranked. The rest are context.

  • Technical drivers in disguise

    “Use microservices” is listed as a business driver. Fix → that's a solution; ask what outcome it was meant to achieve.

  • Set once, forgotten

    The drivers page is from the kick-off and nobody reads it. Fix → open it at every big decision and every quarterly review.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any new system, platform rebuild or major change of direction.
  • When the brief is one sentence or a buzzword.
  • When engineering and business seem to be solving different problems.
  • Before a big build vs buy, cloud or vendor decision.

Skip it when…

  • A small feature where the product goal is already measured.
  • When the drivers already exist in a product strategy: link them, don't duplicate.
  • Throwaway experiments with a one-week life.

8Self-check quiz

Why is “make it scalable” not a business driver?

It has no measure and means different things to different people; it's a wish, not an outcome.

What five things make a driver usable?

A name, a measure, today's value (baseline), a target with a date, and an owner.

Why keep only three to five drivers?

With more, they stop deciding anything; ranking a few gives a real tie-breaker.

What's the value of writing “not a driver” lines?

They give permission to keep things simple and stop over-engineering for needs nobody has.

try it

Take your project's brief and ask “why?” five times about its vaguest word. Write the result as one driver with a measure, today's value, a target and a date. Show it to the sponsor and ask: “if we hit this number, did we win?”

remember!!

Ask why until you hit a number. Then design for the number, not the word.
