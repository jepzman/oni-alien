NFR Checklist

TL;DR

Non-functional requirements (NFRs) are the qualities nobody asks for until they're missing: backups, logging, accessibility, alerting, privacy, cost. Keep a short checklist of quality questions, grouped (run, protect, change, operate, people, cost), and walk it with stakeholders at every kick-off. Each item gets an answer, an owner, or a reason it doesn't apply. It isn't the requirements; it's how you avoid forgetting them.

  • Lives in: a reusable template; one filled copy per project
  • Pairs with: ASRs, quality attribute scenarios, go-live readiness

1The situation Larder, the 2024 launch

When Larder launched in 2024, the team built the shop, orders and delivery, and did it well. What they didn't build was everything nobody had asked for: backups were never test-restored, logs kept customer addresses for two years, the app was unusable with a screen reader, and there was no alerting. One Saturday the database disk filled up and orders failed for three hours before anyone noticed.

  • Product backlogs are full of features; qualities have no natural owner.
  • Each gap is cheap to fix early and painful to fix after an incident.
  • Experienced teams forget too: under deadline pressure, “we'll do it later” wins.

2Real-life analogy the pre-flight checklist

A pilot with 10,000 hours still reads the pre-flight checklist before every take-off: fuel, flaps, instruments, doors. The checklist doesn't teach anyone to fly. It's there because skilled people forget simple things under pressure, and in aviation simple things kill. Checklists are short, grouped, and read out loud.

3How it solves it short, grouped, walked out loud

Keep it to about 20 questions in six groups, each phrased as a question with a default answer (“backups: restored in a test at least monthly?”). At kick-off, walk it with product, operations and security in an hour. Open items become ASRs, quality scenarios or plain tasks with owners.

  1. Start from a quality model

    ISO 25010 or your own: performance, reliability, security, maintainability, usability, portability…

  2. Phrase each as a question

    “Who is paged when checkout fails?” beats “observability”.

  3. Group and trim to ~20

    Run, protect, change, operate, people, cost. Short enough to finish in an hour.

  4. Walk it at kick-off

    With product, operations, security, support. Mark ✓ answered, ? open, – not relevant (with why).

  5. Turn open items into work

    Significant ones become ASRs and scenarios; the rest become tasks with owners.

  6. Improve after incidents

    Every postmortem asks: which question would have caught this? Add it.

Vocabulary for the exam

NFR
Non-functional requirement: a quality, not a feature.
Quality model
A standard list of qualities, such as ISO/IEC 25010.
Operability
How easy the system is to run, watch and fix.
Accessibility
Usable by people with disabilities (WCAG).
RPO / RTO
How much data you can lose / how long recovery may take.
Retention
How long data and logs are kept before deletion.

tools: a Markdown template in a shared repo works best. ISO/IEC 25010 and the arc42 quality tree are good starting lists; the AWS, Azure and Google Cloud Well-Architected frameworks have long question sets to borrow from.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Fewer forgotten basics: backups, alerting, retention, accessibility.
  • Shared ownership: each quality gets a named owner at kick-off.
  • Cheap: an hour per project, reused every time.
  • Learns: every incident can add a question.
  • Feeds the design: open items become ASRs and scenarios.

What we lose

  • Can become box-ticking: “✓” without a real answer.
  • Bloat: lists grow to 200 items and nobody finishes them.
  • Not a design: it finds questions; someone still has to answer them.
  • One size doesn't fit all: needs trimming per project.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A 200-item checklist

    Nobody finishes it, so it's skipped. Fix → about 20 questions; link to longer lists for specialists.

  • Ticking without answering

    “Backups ✓” but nobody has ever restored one. Fix → each ✓ needs a short answer or evidence.

  • Only engineers walk it

    Privacy and accessibility get guessed. Fix → walk it with operations, security, legal and support.

  • A static list

    The same incident happens twice. Fix → after every postmortem, add the question that would have caught it.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every project kick-off and every major change of scope.
  • Before go-live, as a final check (with the go-live readiness list).
  • When onboarding a new team to architecture work.
  • After incidents, to improve the list itself.

Skip it when…

  • Prototypes that won't see real users or data.
  • As a replacement for real scenarios: it finds questions, it doesn't answer them.
  • Tiny changes inside a system that already passed it.

8Self-check quiz

What is an NFR checklist for?

To make sure quality questions are asked and owned at kick-off, so nothing important is forgotten.

Why phrase items as questions?

A question (“who is paged when checkout fails?”) forces a real answer; a word (“observability”) invites a tick.

How long should the checklist be?

About 20 questions in a few groups, short enough to finish in an hour.

How does the checklist improve over time?

After every incident, add the question that would have caught it.

try it

Take the six groups above and write one question per group for your current system. Answer them honestly with ✓, ? or –. For every “?”, write a name next to it. Which one would hurt most if it happened this weekend?

remember!!

It's not about skill. It's about pressure. Read the checklist every time.
