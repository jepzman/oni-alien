Architecture Styles Compared

TL;DR

Every architecture style (layered monolith, modular monolith, microservices, event-driven, serverless) is great at some things and costly at others. Don't argue favourites. List the qualities your drivers and ASRs need, add your real team size, skills and budget, score each style against them, and pick the simplest one that fits. Most systems mix styles: a main shape plus a few parts that need something different.

  • Origin: Mark Richards & Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture (2020), style ratings
  • Lives in: an options table in the decision's ADR
  • Pairs with: options analysis, modular monolith, ASRs

1The situation Larder, meeting number six

The question is simple: what shape should the six-city platform have? The team splits into camps. Ben wants microservices (“Netflix does it”), Eli wants serverless (“we only pay for use”), Cho wants everything event-driven on Kafka. Each argues from a favourite and a conference talk. Three weeks and six meetings later, there's no decision.

  • Styles are compared to each other, not to the system's needs.
  • Team size, skills and budget are left out, though they often decide it.
  • Without shared criteria, the most confident voice wins, or nobody does.

2Real-life analogy choosing a vehicle

A florist in the city centre uses a cargo bike, a plumber a van, a removals firm a lorry, a coal mine a train. None of them is the “best vehicle”. Each is the right one for a load, a distance, the streets, a budget and whoever drives it. Buying a lorry to deliver flowers is expensive and slow.

3How it solves it score against drivers, pick the simplest fit

Use the qualities from your drivers and ASRs as columns, add team fit and cost, and score each style roughly (good / medium / poor). The aim isn't a precise number; it's a conversation about the system instead of about favourites. Then check: is one part different enough to deserve its own style?

  1. List the deciding qualities

    From drivers and ASRs: simplicity, deployability, scalability, availability, cost, team fit.

  2. Add the people and money facts

    Team size, skills, hiring budget, ops maturity. These often decide.

  3. Score each style

    Good / medium / poor for each quality, using a shared reference (Richards & Ford's ratings help).

  4. Pick the simplest that fits

    When two fit, take the one with fewer moving parts.

  5. Look for the odd part out

    A part with very different needs (real-time, huge spikes) may deserve its own style.

  6. Record it as an ADR

    The table, the choice and when to revisit (e.g. “at 30 engineers”).

Vocabulary for the exam

Layered monolith
One deployable app, split into technical layers (UI, logic, data).
Modular monolith
One deployable app, split into business modules with strict boundaries.
Microservices
Many small services, each deployed and owning data separately.
Event-driven
Parts react to events on a broker instead of calling each other.
Serverless
Functions and managed services; the provider runs the servers.
Architecture quantum
A part that can be deployed independently with its own qualities.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Decisions end: criteria replace favourites.
  • Fit to the real team: size, skills and budget are part of the choice.
  • Simpler systems: the simplest fit usually wins.
  • Mixing on purpose: odd parts get a different style, deliberately.
  • A record: the table goes into the ADR, with a revisit trigger.

What we lose

  • Scores are rough and can be argued.
  • Weights can be gamed to favour a chosen answer.
  • Takes a workshop to agree the qualities and scores.
  • Hides detail: “microservices” covers many very different designs.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Fashion-driven

    “Everyone uses microservices now.” Fix → score against your drivers and team, not other companies'.

  • Ignoring the team

    A 14-person team picks a style built for 400. Fix → make team size and skills a weighted column.

  • Reverse-engineered weights

    Weights tweaked until the favourite wins. Fix → agree the qualities and weights before scoring any style.

  • One style for everything

    A real-time part is forced into a batch-friendly shape. Fix → check for the odd part out and give it its own style.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Choosing the shape of a new system or a rebuild.
  • When a team is split between favourite styles.
  • When the team, scale or business has changed a lot since the last choice.
  • Explaining the choice to sponsors and new joiners.

Skip it when…

  • Small systems where a plain monolith is obviously enough.
  • When the organisation has a standard platform that already decides it.
  • Detailed design inside one module: that's a different decision.

8Self-check quiz

Why score styles against drivers rather than against each other?

Because no style is best in general; the right one depends on the system's qualities, team and budget.

Which non-technical facts often decide the style?

Team size, skills, hiring budget and how mature the operations are.

When two styles fit, which should you pick?

The simpler one, with fewer moving parts.

What's the “odd part out” check?

Looking for a part with very different needs that deserves its own style, like Larder's real-time Dispatch.

try it

List five qualities your system needs most, plus “fits our team” and “low run cost”. Score three styles against them in 20 minutes with a colleague who disagrees with you. Did the scores change either of your minds?

remember!!
