Risk-Storming

TL;DR

Don't write the risk list alone. Put the architecture diagram on a wall, gather everyone who builds, runs or depends on the system, and have each person silently stick coloured notes on the parts they think are risky (red = high, amber = medium, green = low). Then compare: clusters and disagreements are where the conversation goes. Rank by likelihood and impact, and give the top few an owner, an action and a date.

  • Family: validating early
  • Origin: Simon Brown, risk-storming (riskstorming.com, ~2014)
  • Lives in: a 60–90 minute workshop; results in the risk register
  • Pairs with: system context & C4 diagrams, constraints vs assumptions, risk register

1The situation Larder, before committing to Rotterdam

Before committing to the Rotterdam plan, Ana writes a risk list at her desk. Six items, all technical: schema migrations, a Django upgrade, test coverage. Missing: Tomás knows the store Wi-Fi drops; Cho knows the payment provider has short outages on Friday evenings; Joost lies awake over the dispatch cron job; and Lena and Marco know the Rotterdam date collides with the StockPilot contract talks. The worst risks are in other people's heads.

  • One person sees risks through one lens, usually their own speciality.
  • In open discussion, the first or loudest voice anchors everyone else.
  • Risks that nobody owns stay on the list and never get handled.

2Real-life analogy the home safety walk

Before a baby arrives, a family walks through the house with stickers, each marking the dangers they see. Get down to toddler height and you find sockets and sharp corners; grandma spots the loose rug on the stairs; the cook sees pan handles sticking out. Nobody alone finds them all. Together, in an hour, they find most.

3How it solves it diagram, silence, compare, act

The key is the silent, individual first round: ten minutes where everyone marks risks before hearing anyone else. Only then compare. Clusters show shared worries; a single red sticky from the one person who runs the stores is often the most important finding of the day.

  1. Draw the diagram

    A context or container diagram everyone can read, printed big or on a shared board.

  2. Invite the mix

    Engineers, operations, security, product, and the people who run the business process.

  3. Silent round (10 min)

    Each person writes risks on red, amber or green stickies and places them on the diagram.

  4. Compare and cluster (20 min)

    Group duplicates, discuss differences: “why did you mark that red?”

  5. Rank (15 min)

    Place the clusters on likelihood × impact. Pick the top five.

  6. Assign and track (15 min)

    Each top risk gets an owner, an action, a date, and goes into the risk register.

Vocabulary for the exam

Risk
Something that might happen and would hurt the goals if it did.
Likelihood
How probable the risk is.
Impact
How badly it would hurt if it happened.
Mitigation
An action that lowers likelihood or impact.
Risk owner
The person responsible for watching and handling one risk.
Risk register
The tracked list of risks, owners, actions and status.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • More risks found: every role sees different ones.
  • Less anchoring: the silent round stops the loudest voice steering.
  • Shared ownership: people own the risks they raised.
  • Quick: 60–90 minutes for a whole-system view.
  • Visible on the design: risks sit on the parts they affect.

What we lose

  • Needs a good diagram first, readable by non-engineers.
  • A busy room: an hour or more of many people's time.
  • Subjective ratings: likelihood and impact are guesses.
  • Easy to stop at the wall without owners and actions.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Talking before marking

    The architect explains the risks first, and everyone agrees. Fix → silent individual round first; discuss only after.

  • Only engineers

    All the stickies are technical. Fix → invite operations, security, legal and the business.

  • No owners

    A beautiful wall of stickies and no next step. Fix → top five each get an owner, action and date before people leave.

  • Once only

    Run at kick-off, never again. Fix → repeat at milestones: after the skeleton, before go-live.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Before committing to a plan, date or budget.
  • After the first architecture diagram exists.
  • At milestones: after the walking skeleton, before go-live.
  • When a team is new to the system or the domain.

Skip it when…

  • Tiny changes with obvious risks.
  • When there's no diagram yet: draw the context diagram first.
  • As a replacement for threat modelling: security needs its own deep dive.

8Self-check quiz

Why start with a silent, individual round?

So everyone marks their own risks before being anchored by the first or loudest voice.

What do the sticky colours mean?

Red = high risk, amber = medium, green = low.

What should every top risk leave the room with?

An owner, an action and a date, recorded in the risk register.

Why put risks on the diagram rather than in a list?

They sit on the parts they affect, so clusters and blind spots are visible.

try it

Print your system's context or container diagram. Ask three people from different roles to spend five minutes, separately, marking risks on their own copy. Lay the copies side by side. Which risk appears on only one copy, and who marked it?

remember!!

Same house, different eyes. Mark in silence, then compare, then assign.
