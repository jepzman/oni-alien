the solution architect's notebook · book 1 · vol. 1

From Requirements to Architecture

Volume 1 of The Solution Architect's Notebook: how an architect gets from a vague business plan to a first architecture people can build, without guessing. 15 notes in four collections: understand the problem and the people, find the requirements that really shape the system, choose the first shapes, and test the riskiest ideas before they're expensive. Every note follows Larder, a grocery delivery start-up growing from one city to six, and ends with a try it exercise for your own project.

the story so far

October 2025. Larder delivers groceries in Amsterdam from two dark stores and has just promised its investors six cities by the end of 2027. Ana joins as its first solution architect, a week before Grace joins as product owner. This volume follows Ana's first four months, from a one-line brief to a walking skeleton for Rotterdam in January 2026.

Understanding the problem who cares, what they want, and where the system ends

  1. 01Stakeholder MapList everyone who can affect or is affected by the system, and plan how to work with each.p. 01
  2. 02Business Drivers & GoalsTurn the business plan into a few measurable goals that every design choice can be checked against.p. 02
  3. 03System Context DiagramOne picture of the system as a box, with the people and systems around it, so everyone agrees where it ends.p. 03
  4. 04Domain Discovery (Event Storming)Map the business as a timeline of events on sticky notes with the people who do the work.p. 04

Requirements that shape architecture find the few needs that decide the design

  1. 05Architecturally Significant RequirementsFind the few requirements that would change the architecture, and design for those first.p. 05
  2. 06Quality Attribute ScenariosTurn “fast” and “reliable” into testable sentences: a stimulus, a context and a measurable response.p. 06
  3. 07Constraints vs AssumptionsSeparate the fixed rules you must obey from the beliefs you still have to check.p. 07
  4. 08NFR ChecklistA short list of quality questions to ask on every project, so nothing important is forgotten.p. 08

First shapes pick a starting structure you can grow

  1. 09Architecture Styles ComparedCompare monolith, modular monolith, microservices, event-driven and serverless against your drivers.p. 09
  2. 10Modular Monolith FirstStart as one deployable app with strict internal modules; split out a service only when you must.p. 10
  3. 11Bounded ContextsDraw boundaries where a word changes meaning, and give each area its own model and owner.p. 11
  4. 12Walking SkeletonBuild the thinnest end-to-end slice through every part first, then add flesh.p. 12

Validating early test the risky ideas while they're still cheap

  1. 13Spike vs Prototype vs PoCPick the right small experiment: a spike answers a question, a prototype tests an idea, a PoC proves it works.p. 13
  2. 14Fitness FunctionsTurn architecture rules into automated checks that run in CI, so the design can't quietly decay.p. 14
  3. 15Risk-StormingThe team marks risks on the architecture diagram together, then decides what to do about the biggest.p. 15

?How every note is laid out same eight sections, every time

  1. The situation: a scene from the case study where it goes wrong, with a doodle.
  2. Real-life analogy: an everyday version, plus a “translating it to the job” table.
  3. How it solves it: diagrams, steps, vocabulary and a template card.
  4. Gains vs losses: the trade-off spread.
  5. Ratings: nine qualities, before → after.
  6. Common pitfalls: each with its fix.
  7. When to use it: and when to skip it.
  8. Self-check quiz: tap a question to see the answer.

★The nine architect ratings what a good architecture practice is judged on

Every note scores the pattern from 1 (poor) to 5 (great) on the same nine things, comparing an app without the pattern to the same app with it.

Clarity
A newcomer can understand the system and the reasoning behind it.
Stakeholder alignment
Business, product and engineering share the same what and why.
Decision speed
How quickly the team can choose and get back to building.
Risk reduction
The big risks are surfaced and handled before they bite.
Adaptability
How cheaply the direction can change later.
Traceability
Months later, anyone can find why something was done.
Cost awareness
Money and effort are part of the decision, not a surprise.
Low overhead
Time and ceremony it takes. High = light.
Team autonomy
Teams can decide and act without waiting for the architect.

first law

Everything in architecture is a trade-off. If you think you've found one that isn't, you haven't found the cost yet.
