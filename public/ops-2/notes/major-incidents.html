When Is It a Major Incident?

TL;DR

A major incident is the rare one that hurts the whole business: a SEV1 at the worst time, or one that drags on. The normal incident process doesn't scale to it, because leaders, support and stores all need the lead at once. Define when it's major in advance, and add a practised plan on top of SEV1: a senior lead who only leads, a liaison for leadership, customer messages, a decision log, a fix team protected from questions, and a review with leadership afterwards.

1The situation Larder, Friday 18 December 2026

At 18:05 on the Friday before Christmas, two days after the database primary was resized in a hurry, the managed database fails over to its replica in about 90 seconds. Larder Core's three VMs keep their connections pointed at the old primary, and checkout fails. Dee declares SEV1 at 18:09 and Eli takes the lead. Then the SEV1 process drowns. Femke phones Eli three times in ten minutes, Sam's team has 300 chats, and store leads ask in four different groups. Eli spends half the incident answering questions. Checkout is back at 18:43: 38 minutes, and about 900 orders lost or late.

  • The SEV1 roles work for 20 minutes on a Tuesday, not for the whole company at Christmas.
  • Leadership wants information, and the only source is the lead.
  • Customers see failing payments and no message.

2Real-life analogy the house fire and the chemical spill

A house fire is handled by one fire crew following its routine. A chemical spill near a school switches on a different, practised plan: a commander at a separate post, a liaison who keeps the mayor informed so the mayor doesn't phone the commander, a press officer, and a log of every decision. The routine doesn't scale to the rare, huge event, and that's exactly why the major plan exists and is rehearsed.

3How it solves it a practised plan on top of SEV1, and leadership in the review

Eli writes the major incident procedure between 21 and 23 December. It is a SEV1, plus:

  • When it's major (any one is enough): a SEV1 in a critical window (Friday 17:00–20:00, Korenwolf's 07:00–09:00, campaign days); any SEV1 lasting more than 30 minutes; or more than one city down.
  • A senior lead (Eli or Ana) who does nothing but lead.
  • A leadership liaison who updates Femke every 15 minutes, so she doesn't need to call the lead. Femke agreed to this in the review.
  • Customer messages: an in-app banner and the status page, from templates.
  • A decision log: every choice, with its time and reason.
  • A protected fix team: nobody asks them anything except the lead.

The review is held on 22 December, with Femke there. It doesn't end with actions alone. It ends with options for leadership: how fast must failover be at the moments that matter, and what is that worth? Ana turns that into her options page, and on 12 January Femke chooses option B (ADR 0017): failover in under 2 minutes, tested monthly, and no changes in critical windows. The procedure is practised for the first time in a game day in January (vol. 3).

  1. Define “major” before it happens

    Critical windows, duration, breadth.

  2. Name a senior lead pool

    Two or three people who only lead.

  3. Add a leadership liaison

    Leaders get updates without calling the lead.

  4. Template customer messages

    In-app and status page, ready to send.

  5. Keep a decision log

    Choices, times, reasons.

  6. Review with leadership, and practise

    Options to decide, and a drill twice a year.

Vocabulary for the exam

Major incident
An incident with very high impact on the business, handled by a separate procedure.
Critical window
A time when outages hurt most.
Leadership liaison
The person keeping executives informed, so the lead can lead.
Decision log
A timed record of decisions during the incident.
Failover
Switching to a standby when the main component fails.
Game day
A planned exercise that rehearses an incident (vol. 5).

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • No definition

    People argue whether it's “major” at 18:10. Fix → write the triggers in advance.

  • Leaders call the lead

    The lead can't lead. Fix → a liaison, agreed with leaders beforehand.

  • A plan never practised

    Nobody remembers it at Christmas. Fix → drill twice a year.

  • A review with only engineers

    Actions, but no decisions about money or risk. Fix → leadership present, with options.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A SEV1 at the worst moment.
  • Leaders and customers swamp the incident lead.
  • Before peaks (Christmas, King's Day).
  • When a business client's mornings are on the line.

Skip it when…

  • Normal SEV1s and SEV2s: the usual roles are enough.

8Self-check quiz

When is an incident major at Larder?

A SEV1 in a critical window, or lasting over 30 minutes, or with more than one city down.

What does the leadership liaison do?

Updates Femke and leaders every 15 minutes, so they don't need to call the lead.

What caused the 38 minutes on 18 December?

The database failed over in about 90 seconds, but Core's VMs kept connections to the old primary until each was restarted by hand.

How does a major incident review differ?

Leadership is present, and it ends with options for them to decide, not only engineering actions.

What came out of it?

Ana's options page and Femke's choice of option B (ADR 0017): failover under 2 minutes, tested monthly, no changes in critical windows.

what would you do?At 07:40 on a Tuesday the van routes for Korenwolf won't load and drivers are waiting. It's a SEV1 for business deliveries only. Is it major?

Yes: it's a SEV1 in a critical window (Korenwolf's 07:00–09:00). Senior lead, a liaison for Femke and Mo, a message to Korenwolf's office managers, and a decision log.

try it

Write down your critical windows: the hours when an outage would hurt most. Then write who would lead, who would keep your leaders informed, and what customers would see, if your main service went down in one of them. Plan a one-hour drill.

remember!!

The rare, huge incident needs a different, practised plan: a lead who only leads, a liaison for leaders, and a message for customers.
