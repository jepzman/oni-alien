Who Needs to Know?

TL;DR

During an incident, the people affected need short, regular updates from you, before they make up their own story. Decide who needs to know (support, the front line, leaders, customers), where each group reads it, and a fixed format: what customers see, what we're doing, what you should do, when the next update is. Send it on schedule even when nothing has changed. Silence is the worst update.

1The situation Larder, 3–6 July 2026

After the 3 July stock sync incident Eli asks how each group heard about it. Sam's support team heard from customers complaining. The stores heard from empty shelves. Femke heard on Monday, from a customer's tweet. Three groups, three different stories, all late, and none of them from us. And from September, Korenwolf's three office managers will need to know too.

  • Support can't help customers without knowing what's happening.
  • Stores lose time working around a problem nobody has named.
  • Leaders lose trust when they hear it from outside.

2Real-life analogy the station departure board

When a train is delayed, a good station shows the same short message on the board, in the app and over the speakers: “Utrecht 18:12 delayed, signal failure, next update 18:30”. At 18:30 there's an update, even if all it says is “no change”. Passengers can live with a delay. What they can't stand is silence.

3How it solves it four audiences, fixed places, four lines, on schedule

Eli and Sam write the communication part of the incident process in August:

  • Support reads the pinned update in #incident, and Sam's team can copy it straight into replies.
  • Stores get a message in the store leads' group from the communication person.
  • Femke and the leaders get a short direct message for SEV1, within 15 minutes.
  • Business customers see a status page, launched on 1 September for office managers before Korenwolf's contract starts. It shows incidents that affect business ordering or deliveries.

Every update has the same four lines: what customers see · what we're doing · what you should do · next update at. It goes out every 15 minutes for SEV1 and every 30 for SEV2, even when nothing has changed. Templates are pinned, so the communication person fills in blanks rather than writing prose under stress.

  1. List who is affected by your services

    Support, front line, leaders, customers.

  2. Give each audience one place to read

    A channel, a group, a page. Not all of them.

  3. Use a four-line format

    Customers see · we're doing · you should do · next update.

  4. Write in their words

    “Payment fails for some customers”, not “PSP 502s”.

  5. Send on schedule, even with no change

    The promised time is the promise.

  6. Close it clearly

    “Fixed at 19:10; what we'll do next: review on Tuesday.”

Vocabulary for the exam

Status page
A public or customer-facing page showing current incidents and their status.
Update cadence
How often updates are sent: 15 or 30 minutes.
Holding statement
A first short message when you don't know much yet.
Audience
A group that needs updates: support, stores, leaders, clients.
Resolution notice
The last update: fixed, when, what happens next.
Jargon
Words only engineers use; keep them out of updates.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Updating only when there's news

    Silence reads as “nobody's on it”. Fix → promise a time and keep it.

  • Jargon

    “502s from the PSP gateway”. Fix → write what customers see.

  • Too many places

    Some people read the old one. Fix → one place per audience.

  • The fixer writes the updates

    Fixing slows down; updates stop. Fix → a separate communication role.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • People affected hear from someone other than you.
  • Support doesn't know what to tell customers.
  • Business clients depend on you.
  • Every SEV1 and SEV2.

Skip it when…

  • SEV3 and SEV4 with no customer impact: a line in the ticket.

8Self-check quiz

What are the four lines of every update?

What customers see, what we're doing, what you should do, next update at.

Why send an update when nothing changed?

Silence reads as nobody being on it; the promised time is the promise.

Who should write updates during an incident?

The communication person, not the fixer or the lead.

What was Larder's status page for?

Business customers (office managers) to see incidents affecting business ordering or deliveries.

How did Femke hear about 3 July?

On Monday, from a customer's tweet: the thing the new process prevents.

what would you do?At 18:45 the next update is due, but the fixers have found nothing new. What does the communication person send?

The same four lines, updated: still affected, still working on it (what's being tried now), same advice, next update at 19:15. Short, on time.

try it

For your most important service, list who needs to know when it breaks, where each group would read an update, and write your own four-line template. When did your last incident's first update go out?

remember!!

People forgive delays; they don't forgive silence. Four lines, one place each, and a promised next time.
