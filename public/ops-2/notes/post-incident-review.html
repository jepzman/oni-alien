What Can We Learn from It?

TL;DR

A post-incident review turns an incident into lessons. Build the timeline, and at each decision ask what the person knew and why their action made sense at the time, not who got it wrong. People act on the information, tools and instructions they have, so that's what you change. Keep it short (45 minutes), led by someone who wasn't in it, and end with at most three actions with owners and dates.

1The situation Larder, Monday 24 August 2026

On the Monday after the StockPilot outage someone says in the corridor: “Cho restarted the sync four times, that made it worse.” Cho hears it. The question going round is who did what wrong. Nobody is asking why restarting four times looked like the right thing to do at 17:41. The answer is that it's exactly what runbook RB-09 said.

  • Blame teaches people to hide what they did.
  • The real causes (the runbook, the missing vendor sheet) stay where they are.
  • The next person on call follows the same runbook into the same wall.

2Real-life analogy aviation's safety reports

After a near miss, airline pilots write down what happened and why it made sense at the time, and in most systems they can do it without fear of punishment. Investigators then look for what in the cockpit, the checklist or the training led there, and change that. Flying got very safe this way. If pilots were blamed, the reports would stop, and so would the learning.

3How it solves it timeline, why it made sense, three actions, no names as causes

The first review is on Wednesday 26 August: 45 minutes, facilitated by Ana, who wasn't in the incident. It has five parts:

  1. Timeline, from the pager log and the scribe's notes.
  2. At each decision: what did the person know, and why did the action make sense? Cho explains: the sync was stale, RB-09 said to restart it, and the restart seemed to work for a minute each time.
  3. What helped: our own alert, 40 minutes before StockPilot's status page.
  4. What got in the way: RB-09 didn't say “unless the vendor is down”, there was no vendor sheet, and nobody knew StockPilot's hours.
  5. At most three actions, each with an owner and a date: the vendor sheet (Eli, 28 Aug), splitting RB-09 into “stuck” and “vendor down” (Cho, 28 Aug), and the low-stock switch (Ben, 18 Sep).

The review is shared with everyone in engineering, and with Sam and Joost. Its language rule is strict: no person is ever a cause. “Cho restarted four times” becomes “RB-09 led on-call to restart a sync whose vendor was down”. From now on every SEV1 and SEV2 gets a review within a week.

  1. Book it within a week

    While memory is fresh, after people have slept.

  2. Facilitate with someone not involved

    They ask; they don't judge.

  3. Build the timeline from records

    Pager log, scribe notes, chat.

  4. Ask “why did it make sense?” at each decision

    What did they know? What did the tools say?

  5. List what helped, too

    Keep what worked.

  6. End with at most three actions

    Owners and dates; share the write-up widely.

Vocabulary for the exam

Post-incident review (postmortem)
A structured look back at an incident to learn from it.
Blameless
Assuming people acted reasonably on what they knew; looking for causes in the system.
Timeline
What happened when, in order, from records.
Decision point
A moment where someone chose what to do next.
Contributing factor
Something that made the incident possible or worse; there are usually several.
Facilitator
The person running the review, ideally not involved in the incident.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A person as root cause

    “Human error” ends the search too early. Fix → ask what made the action make sense.

  • Twelve actions

    None of them get done. Fix → at most three, owned and dated.

  • Facilitated by the boss

    People defend instead of describe. Fix → someone not involved, not senior.

  • Shared with nobody

    Only the room learns. Fix → publish it to everyone affected.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every SEV1 and SEV2.
  • A SEV3 that keeps happening.
  • When the corridor has already found someone to blame.
  • Near misses worth learning from (this volume).

Skip it when…

  • SEV4: a line in the ticket is enough.

8Self-check quiz

What does “blameless” mean?

Assuming people acted reasonably on what they knew, and looking for causes in tools, runbooks and information.

Why should the facilitator be someone who wasn't involved?

They can ask neutral questions; people involved, or seniors, tend to steer and defend.

What question is asked at each decision point?

What did the person know, and why did their action make sense at the time?

Why at most three actions?

More rarely get done; three owned, dated actions do.

What was the cause in the 21 August review, if not Cho?

RB-09 had only a restart path, no vendor sheet existed, and nothing stopped customers ordering stale stock.

what would you do?In a review, someone says “the real problem is that Dee didn't check the logs.” What does the facilitator ask?

What did Dee know at that point, and what made checking the logs not the obvious step? Was it the runbook, the alert, the dashboard? The fix goes there.

try it

Take your last incident and write its timeline. At each decision write what the person knew and why their action made sense. What do you find in the tools, runbooks and information, rather than in the people?

remember!!

Ask why it made sense, not who got it wrong. Blame makes people hide the truth; the system is what you can fix.
