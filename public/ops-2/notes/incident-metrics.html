Which Incident Numbers Mean Anything?

TL;DR

Incident numbers should answer three questions: how much did customers suffer, how fast did we respond, and are we learning? Count incidents by severity, measure harm in orders or customer-minutes, and use medians and the worst case per severity rather than one average (MTTR) across everything, because one long, minor incident makes the average lie. Show the trend, and say what changed.

1The situation Larder, early November 2026

After the 29 October incident, Femke asks for “the incident numbers”. Eli's first draft says “mean time to recovery: 48 minutes”. It looks bad, and it's misleading. One four-hour SEV3 in a back-office report pulls the average up, while most incidents took around 20 minutes. And the 25-minute SEV1 that stopped Dispatch is just one number among many. Nothing in the draft says what customers felt, or whether anything is getting better.

  • Averages hide both the worst incidents and the typical ones.
  • Counting all incidents together mixes a printer with a city.
  • Speed is measured; harm and learning aren't.

2Real-life analogy the hospital's average wait

A hospital proudly reports an average waiting time of 42 minutes. It looks fine, because hundreds of quick visits for small things hide the three chest-pain patients who waited six hours. The meaningful question is by severity: how long did the red patients wait? And what changed this month so they'll wait less next month?

3How it solves it harm, response, recovery by severity, and learning, on one page

From November Eli sends Femke one page a month, with five parts:

  • Count by severity: SEV1, SEV2, SEV3, with six months of trend.
  • Customer harm: orders affected (failed, late or with wrong stock) and customer-minutes. This is the number Femke reads first.
  • Time to acknowledge: the median, and the worst case with its name. From 14 minutes in June to 4 in November, after escalation and test pages.
  • Recovery by severity: the median and the longest, each with its name. There is no overall average.
  • Learning: reviews held, actions done, problems closed.

Each part has one sentence on what changed and why. Eli keeps MTTR in a footnote, to explain why it isn't on the page: averages of skewed numbers mostly measure luck. Femke says the harm line is the first incident number she has understood.

  1. Count by severity, never all together

    A printer isn't a city.

  2. Measure harm in customers' units

    Orders affected, customer-minutes.

  3. Use medians and the worst case

    Name the worst; don't average it away.

  4. Track time to acknowledge

    It's the part on-call controls most.

  5. Show the learning loop

    Reviews, actions, problems closed.

  6. Write what changed

    One sentence per number.

Vocabulary for the exam

MTTA
Mean time to acknowledge: from alert to someone saying “I've got it”.
MTTR
Mean time to recovery (or repair): from start to service restored.
Median
The middle value; not pulled around by one extreme incident.
Customer-minutes
Customers affected × minutes affected: a rough measure of harm.
Skewed distribution
Most values small, a few very large: averages mislead here.
Leading indicator
A number that moves before the outcome does (actions done, time to acknowledge).

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • One average for everything

    One long minor incident dominates. Fix → by severity, medians and the worst.

  • Counting incidents as the goal

    People stop declaring them. Fix → count harm; more declared SEV3s can be good.

  • Speed without harm

    Fast recovery from incidents that hurt thousands. Fix → orders affected first.

  • Numbers without a story

    Leaders guess at the cause of a trend. Fix → one sentence on what changed.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Leaders ask for “the incident numbers”.
  • MTTR is the only number you have.
  • You want to show whether reviews are working.
  • Before agreeing service levels (vol. 4).

Skip it when…

  • Very few incidents: describe each one instead of counting.

8Self-check quiz

Why can MTTR mislead?

Incident durations are skewed; one long minor incident pulls the average far from what typically happens.

What does Larder measure harm in?

Orders affected and customer-minutes.

What replaces the overall average?

Median and longest recovery, per severity, with names.

Why track time to acknowledge?

It's the part of the response on-call controls most, and it moved from 14 to 4 minutes.

Why is counting incidents a dangerous goal?

People stop declaring them; more honestly declared SEV3s can be a good sign.

what would you do?Femke asks why there were more SEV3s this month than last. What might be a good answer, and what do you check?

It may be good news: the new desk and tiers record things that used to be fixed quietly. Check harm (orders affected) and the SEV1–2 trend: if those fell, more declared SEV3s mean better visibility, not worse service.

try it

Take last quarter's incidents. Compute the average recovery time, then the median and the longest per severity. How different is the story? Estimate orders or users affected for the top three.

remember!!

Averages hide the worst night and the typical one. Count by severity, measure harm in customers, and show what changed.
