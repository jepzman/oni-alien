Who Is in Charge?

TL;DR

In an incident, name four roles in the first five minutes: an incident lead who coordinates and decides but doesn't fix; one or two fixers; someone on communication; and a scribe who writes the timeline. It looks like overhead for a team of six. It isn't: without a lead, four people fix the same thing, nobody tells the stores, and afterwards nobody knows what happened.

1The situation Larder, Friday 3 July 2026

At 17:40 the stock sync stops, and the app starts showing items in stock that aren't. Eli, Ben, Cho and Dee all jump into the chat and all debug at once. Two of them restart the same service a minute apart, and one changes a batch size without saying so. Nobody tells the stores: Tomás finds out at 18:20, when Noord's pickers keep finding empty shelves. It's over at 18:50, and nobody can say which change fixed it.

  • Everyone fixes; nobody coordinates.
  • Changes collide and can't be undone, because nobody knows what was changed.
  • The people affected are told last, or not at all.

2Real-life analogy the kitchen fire drill

When a pan catches fire in a busy restaurant, the head chef takes charge and stops cooking. One cook deals with the fire. The floor manager calms and informs the guests. Someone writes down the times for the insurance. Everyone else stays at their station. Before they practised it, all eight cooks ran to the same pan.

3How it solves it four roles, named in five minutes; the lead doesn't fix

From 13 July every SEV1 and SEV2 starts the same way. The first responder declares it in #incident with a level, and names four roles:

  • Incident lead: by default the on-call primary. Keeps the picture, decides, asks “what do we know, what are we trying next, who's doing it?” every 10 minutes. Doesn't type commands.
  • Fixers: one or two people working the problem, who say every change in the channel before making it.
  • Communication: tells Sam, the stores and (for SEV1) Femke, on the schedule for that level.
  • Scribe: writes the timeline with times, as it happens.

In a small incident one person can hold two roles, but never lead and fix at once. Handing over the lead is said out loud. The first use is Friday 17 July: the same kind of sync failure, Dee as lead, Ben fixing, Cho on communication. The stores are told at minute 6, and it's fixed in 23 minutes.

  1. Declare the incident and its level

    In one channel, with a time.

  2. Name the lead

    By default the on-call primary.

  3. Name fixers, communication, scribe

    Small incidents can double up, never lead + fix.

  4. Fixers say each change before making it

    No secret changes.

  5. The lead asks three questions every 10 minutes

    What do we know? What next? Who's doing it?

  6. Hand over the lead out loud

    “Dee, you have the lead.” Written in the timeline.

Vocabulary for the exam

Incident lead (commander)
The person coordinating the response and making decisions.
Fixer (operations)
A person working directly on the problem.
Communication lead
The person keeping everyone affected informed.
Scribe
The person writing the timeline during the incident.
Declare
Officially start an incident, with a level, in a known place.
Freelancing
Fixing without telling the lead: the cause of colliding changes.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • The lead fixes too

    Heads down in logs, nobody keeps the picture. Fix → the lead doesn't type commands.

  • Silent changes

    Two fixes collide; nobody knows what worked. Fix → say it in the channel before you do it.

  • Seniority decides the lead

    The CTO-equivalent turns up and takes over without saying so. Fix → the lead changes only by an explicit handover.

  • Roles for SEV4

    Process for nothing. Fix → roles for SEV1 and SEV2 only.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • More than one person is working an incident.
  • Changes have collided before.
  • The people affected heard late.
  • Afterwards, nobody can reconstruct what happened.

Skip it when…

  • SEV3 and SEV4: one person fixes it and writes a line.

8Self-check quiz

What are the four roles?

Incident lead, fixers, communication, scribe.

Why doesn't the lead fix?

Someone has to keep the whole picture and decide; heads-down fixing makes that impossible.

What must fixers do before changing something?

Say it in the channel, so changes don't collide and can be undone.

Who is lead by default at Larder?

The on-call primary.

What changed between 3 July and 17 July?

Same kind of failure: stores told at minute 6 instead of minute 40, fixed in 23 minutes instead of 70.

what would you do?During a SEV2, Eli joins the channel and starts suggesting fixes directly to the fixer, while Dee is lead. What should happen?

Eli gives his suggestions to Dee, the lead, or asks her for the lead in an explicit handover. Two people steering the fixer is how changes collide.

try it

Replay your last incident on paper. Who was leading? Who told the people affected, and when? Was there a timeline? Write who would have held each of the four roles.

remember!!

Someone has to lead and not fix. Name the roles in the first five minutes, and say each change before you make it.
