Was It a Change?

TL;DR

Many incidents start with a change: a deploy, a config edit, a network rule, a feature flag, a vendor's maintenance. So the first question in every incident is “what changed in the last two hours?”, and to answer it in a minute you need every change in one visible feed, with time, what, who and how to undo it. Undoing the change often restores service faster than diagnosing the system.

1The situation Larder, Thursday 29 October 2026

At 18:10 the pipeline applies a network rule tidy-up that Eli wrote and had approved that afternoon. It removes a rule Dispatch needs to reach the database. Bram, three weeks into the job and on call, checks Dispatch, then the database, then the containers, and all of them look healthy. Eli is on a train with no signal. Nobody asks what changed. At 18:35 Bram finds the change in the pipeline's history and reverts it. Couriers are assigned by hand for most of those 25 minutes.

  • Changes are scattered: pipeline history, chat, the cloud console.
  • “What changed?” isn't the first question in any runbook.
  • The change feed has been an open action since July (A-19).

2Real-life analogy the noise after the garage visit

Your car starts making a new noise the day after a garage visit. A good mechanic doesn't start with “what could it be?”, because there are a hundred possibilities. The first question is “what did we touch last?”. Most new noises come from the last thing someone changed, and putting that back is usually faster than understanding the whole engine.

3How it solves it one feed of every change, and “what changed?” first

Bram finishes A-19 on 30 October, the day after. From then on every change posts one line into #incident: deploys, infrastructure and network changes, feature flag changes (including automatic ones), and vendor maintenance notices. Each line has the time, what, who, and how to undo it.

Every runbook and the incident template now start with the same question: what changed in the last two hours? If something did, and it's plausibly related, undo it first and diagnose afterwards, because a revert is usually faster and safer than a fix under pressure.

The review of 29 October (facilitated by Ana) adds one more finding. The tidy-up was a normal change applied at 18:10, the start of the evening peak. That question, when changes may run, goes to volume 3. In November 4 of 9 incidents followed a change, and each time the change was found in under 2 minutes.

  1. Collect every kind of change in one feed

    Deploys, infrastructure, network, flags, vendors.

  2. One line each: time, what, who, undo

    The undo is the important part.

  3. Put the feed where incidents happen

    The incident channel, not a separate tool.

  4. Ask “what changed?” first

    In every runbook and the incident template.

  5. Undo first, diagnose after

    If the change is plausibly related.

  6. Look at when changes run

    A change at the peak is its own risk.

Vocabulary for the exam

Change
Adding, modifying or removing anything that could affect a service.
Change feed
A running list of every change, visible to on-call.
Revert (roll back)
Undoing a change to the last known good state.
Change-related incident
An incident caused or triggered by a change.
Mean time to innocence
Time spent proving it wasn't your part; a sign of missing information.
Correlation
Two things happening together; a good place to start, not yet proof.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Only deploys in the feed

    Network, flags and console clicks are missed. Fix → every kind of change, including vendors'.

  • No undo written

    Reverting needs the author, who's on a train. Fix → each line says how to undo.

  • Diagnosing before undoing

    Twenty minutes understanding what a revert fixes in one. Fix → undo first if it's plausibly related.

  • Blaming the author

    Eli wrote the rule; Eli isn't the cause. Fix → ask why a peak-time change looked fine.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Incidents where nobody asked what changed.
  • Changes come from several tools.
  • New people join on-call.
  • Before setting change rules (vol. 3).

Skip it when…

  • Never: even one deploy a week belongs in a feed.

8Self-check quiz

What's the first question in every Larder incident now?

What changed in the last two hours?

What does each change-feed line contain?

Time, what, who, and how to undo it.

Why undo before diagnosing?

A revert is usually faster and safer than a fix under pressure; diagnose once customers are fine.

Why did Bram take 25 minutes on 29 October?

Changes weren't visible in one place and no runbook asked “what changed?”, so he checked healthy systems one by one.

What other finding did the review make?

A normal change ran at 18:10, at the start of the evening peak: when changes may run is a question for vol. 3.

what would you do?Checkout errors jump at 19:05. The feed shows a flag turned on at 19:02 and a deploy at 17:30. What do you do?

Turn the flag off first (it's the closest change and easy to undo), say so in the channel, and watch errors. If they don't drop within a few minutes, look at the deploy next.

try it

Write down every way a change can reach your production systems: pipelines, consoles, flags, vendors. How many of them would on-call see in one place, in one minute? Pick the most hidden one and make it post to a shared channel.

remember!!

The first question is what changed?, and the fastest fix is often undo it. Make every change visible, with its undo.
