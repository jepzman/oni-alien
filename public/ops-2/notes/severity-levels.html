How Bad Is It?

TL;DR

A severity level says how bad an incident is, and therefore who is called, how fast, and who must be told. Define three or four levels in advance, by customer harm (not by which system broke or how busy people feel), write down what each level triggers, and agree one rule: when unsure, pick the higher one. Then nobody spends the first fifteen minutes of an incident arguing about its size.

1The situation Larder, Friday 12 June 2026

At 18:02 on a Friday about 30 % of iDEAL payments start failing at the payment provider. Cho, on call, sees that cards still work and calls it small. Ben points out that iDEAL is how most Dutch customers pay, and it's the Friday peak. They argue for 14 minutes about whether to wake Eli, tell Sam and call the provider. About 160 orders fail before the provider fixes it at 19:10.

  • Everyone judges size by their own corner.
  • What to do follows from the size, so arguing about the size means nothing gets done.
  • Support and the stores hear about it last.

2Real-life analogy triage at the emergency department

An emergency department has four triage colours on the wall: red is seen now, orange within ten minutes, yellow within an hour, green can wait. A nurse decides in about a minute, by what the patient has, not by how loudly they complain or which doctor is free. Nobody debates it at the door, and the colour decides everything that follows.

3How it solves it four levels by customer harm, and what each one triggers

On Monday 15 June Eli brings a one-page draft to tech leads, and Femke and Sam join for it. The levels are set by customer harm:

  • SEV1: a city can't order, pay, pick or dispatch, or more than 10 % of orders are failing. An incident lead at once, Femke told within 15 minutes, updates every 15 minutes.
  • SEV2: one store, one payment method or one business client's delivery at risk, or 2–10 % failing. On-call leads, Sam is told, updates every 30 minutes.
  • SEV3: degraded, with a workaround. Fixed in working hours.
  • SEV4: no customer impact. A ticket.

Two rules go with it: whoever acknowledges the alert sets the level within 5 minutes, and when unsure, pick the higher one, because lowering it later is cheap. 12 June would have been SEV2 in one minute: call the provider, tell Sam, update every 30 minutes. In the next four months Larder has 2 SEV1s, 9 SEV2s and about 30 SEV3s, and nobody argues about size again.

  1. Describe harm in customers' terms

    Orders failing, a city or store stopped, a client's delivery.

  2. Use three or four levels, no more

    Each one must trigger something different.

  3. Write what each level triggers

    Who is paged, who is told, how often to update.

  4. Let the first responder set it

    Within 5 minutes, without asking permission.

  5. When unsure, go higher

    It's cheap to lower; expensive to be late.

  6. Re-check the level as you learn

    And note every change in the timeline.

Vocabulary for the exam

Severity
How bad an incident is for customers.
Impact
How many people or orders are affected.
Urgency
How fast the harm grows if nothing is done.
Incident
An unplanned interruption or loss of quality of a service.
Escalating the severity
Raising the level when it turns out worse.
Declaring
Saying out loud, in the incident channel, that an incident is on and at what level.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Levels by system

    “Database = SEV1” even when nobody notices. Fix → levels by customer harm.

  • Too many levels

    People argue about SEV3 vs SEV4. Fix → three or four, each triggering something different.

  • Asking permission to declare

    The first responder waits for a senior. Fix → whoever acknowledges sets it.

  • Never lowering it

    Everything stays SEV1 for the record. Fix → change it as you learn, and note why.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Incidents start with an argument about size.
  • Support or leaders hear about outages late.
  • Before sharing on-call with new people.
  • You need to decide who is woken up for what.

Skip it when…

  • Never: even a two-person team gains from one shared definition.

8Self-check quiz

What should severity be based on?

Customer harm: how many orders, stores or clients are affected, and how badly.

Who sets the severity, and how fast?

Whoever acknowledges the alert, within 5 minutes.

What's the rule when you're unsure?

Pick the higher level; lowering it later is cheap.

What level would 12 June have been?

SEV2: one payment method failing for part of the customers.

Why only four levels?

Each level must trigger something different; more levels just add arguments.

what would you do?At 08:10 on a Tuesday Rotterdam's handhelds can't sync, but the offline mode is working and orders are being picked. What level, and why?

SEV3 (degraded with a workaround), but watch it: offline mode lasts about two hours, so if it isn't fixed by then it becomes SEV2 (a store stopped). Note the time.

try it

Write your own four levels in one sentence each, by customer harm. For your last three incidents, which level would each have been, and what would that have changed in the first fifteen minutes?

remember!!

Decide how bad it is before the incident, by customer harm. When unsure, go higher.
