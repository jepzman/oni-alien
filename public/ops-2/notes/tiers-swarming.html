Who Fixes What?

TL;DR

Tiers (first line, second line, specialists) work well for known problems: the first line fixes them from a list. For new problems, passing a ticket from tier to tier is slow, and everyone guesses. Swarming works better there: the people who might know look at it together, in one place, and one of them takes it by name. Use both, and make every new fix a known one for next time.

1The situation Larder, early September 2026

From 1 September Sam's support team is the first line for store tickets, so on-call isn't interrupted for every printer. In the first week Rotterdam reports a label printer printing blank labels. Sam's team passes it to on-call, on-call passes it to Dee (“printers talk to the picker app”), and Dee passes it to Eli (“probably the network”). Four hops and two days, and the fix had been known since May: power-cycle the printer and reload the roll.

  • Each hop adds hours, and each person starts again from zero.
  • The first line can't fix what nobody has written down for them.
  • “Not mine” becomes a way of handling tickets.

2Real-life analogy the nurse's card and the specialists' room

At a good clinic, a nurse at the front treats common things straight away, from a protocol card. Unusual cases see a doctor. A truly strange case gets the specialists into one room together, instead of being sent from department to department for a week while each one says “not ours”.

3How it solves it tiers for the known, swarming for the new, then make it known

Eli and Sam agree three steps on 14 September:

  1. Known? Sam's team checks the known-error list (next notes). If there's a match, they fix it or guide the store through it at once.
  2. New? No passing on. The ticket goes into #help-stores, where on-call and anyone who might know (Dee for devices, Eli for networks, the store lead) look at it together. One person takes it by name and keeps it until it's fixed or deliberately handed over with a conversation.
  3. Fixed, and could it happen again? The fix is added to the known-error list, so next time it's step 1.

In October Sam's team fixes 38 % of store tickets alone, and the median number of hops per ticket drops from 2.4 to 1.1. Incidents keep their own roles (this volume). This is for everyday tickets.

  1. Give the first line a list of known fixes

    Short, searchable, kept current.

  2. Let them fix, not just forward

    With the access and the steps they need.

  3. Swarm the new in one place

    The people who might know look together.

  4. One owner by name

    Keeps it until it's fixed or handed over in a conversation.

  5. Turn new fixes into known ones

    Each fix that could recur is added to the list.

  6. Measure hops and first-line fixes

    Fewer hops, more fixed at the front.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Forwarding as a reflex

    “Not mine” is a way of closing tickets. Fix → no forwarding: swarm, then one owner.

  • A first line without a list

    They can only pass things on. Fix → give them known fixes and the access to apply them.

  • Swarming everything

    Five people on every printer. Fix → swarm the new; tiers for the known.

  • New fixes never written down

    The same swarm next month. Fix → add it to the list before closing.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Tickets hop between people for days.
  • A first line exists but rarely fixes things.
  • The same problems keep coming back.
  • Engineers are interrupted by known issues.

Skip it when…

  • During an incident: use incident roles instead.
