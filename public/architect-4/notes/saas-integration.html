SaaS & Vendor Integration

TL;DR

SaaS and vendor systems run on someone else's schedule: webhooks go quiet, rate limits bite, sandboxes differ from production, and outages land at month end. Integrate as if you don't control them, because you don't: verify and de-duplicate webhooks and poll as a fallback; stay below rate limits with a queue; test against the sandbox and production; watch the status page and alert on silence; keep it all behind an anti-corruption layer with an exit plan.

1The situation Larder, 6 September 2027

On 31 August, month end, the billing service's webhooks stop for nine hours. “Invoice paid” updates never arrive, and Korenwolf's account shows paid invoices as unpaid; Mo gets the call. The same month, WMS B's rate limit (20 requests a second) was hit during the Utrecht move, and its sandbox behaved differently from production. Larder had integrated systems it doesn't control as if it did.

  • Webhooks can stop silently; nothing tells you.
  • Vendor limits and sandboxes differ from what you tested.
  • Outages hit at the worst moments: peaks, month ends.

2Real-life analogy hiring the caterers

For a wedding you hire caterers; you don't control their kitchen. So you agree arrival times, check deliveries against the order, keep their emergency number, have a backup for the cake, and don't let them rearrange your venue. You trust them, and you plan as if one thing will go wrong, because one usually does.

3How it solves it plan for the vendor's bad day

Ana and Cho turned the lessons into a checklist for every vendor integration. Webhooks are verified, de-duplicated, and backed by a poll every 15 minutes when they go quiet; an alert fires on silence. Calls go through a queue below the rate limit. Tests run against the sandbox and read-only production checks. The vendor's status page feeds Larder's alerts. Applied to billing in September: a repeat of 31 August would now be caught within fifteen minutes.

  1. Assume webhooks fail

    Verify signatures, de-duplicate, poll as a fallback, alert on silence.

  2. Respect the limits

    Queue and back off below the vendor's rate limit; know the bulk options.

  3. Test beyond the sandbox

    Contract checks against production's read-only endpoints.

  4. Watch the vendor

    Status page subscriptions, support contacts, escalation path.

  5. Keep it behind an adapter

    Anti-corruption layer; your own model inside.

  6. Have an exit

    Data exports, notice periods, an exit plan.

Vocabulary for the exam

Webhook
A vendor's HTTP call to you when something happens.
Rate limit
The maximum calls a vendor accepts per period.
Polling fallback
Asking the vendor periodically in case webhooks are missed.
Silence alert
An alert when expected events stop arriving.
Sandbox
A vendor's test environment; often not identical to production.
Status page
The vendor's public page of incidents and uptime.

# Vendor / SaaS integration checklist              owner: Ana · used for every new vendor

WEBHOOKS    [ ] verify signatures  [ ] store event ids, ignore duplicates
            [ ] poll every 15 min for changes since the last event (fallback)
            [ ] alert if no event for N minutes when some are expected (billing: 60)
LIMITS      [ ] client-side queue below the rate limit (WMS B: 15 of 20 req/s)
            [ ] backoff + retry on 429/5xx  [ ] bulk endpoints for migrations
TESTING     [ ] sandbox for development  [ ] read-only contract checks on production
OPERATIONS  [ ] status page → our alerts  [ ] support contacts + escalation path
            [ ] service levels written down (and what we do when they're missed)
BOUNDARIES  [ ] one adapter, anti-corruption layer (no vendor words outside)
            [ ] exit plan: data export tested, notice period, alternatives
Applied     billing (Sep 2027) · WMS B (Sep 2027) · payment providers (Oct 2027)

tools: webhook signature libraries, an inbox table for de-duplication, a queue with rate limiting, status page subscriptions (Statuspage feeds), contract tests; vendor sandboxes as a start, not the finish.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Silent failures caught within minutes.
  • No duplicate effects from repeated webhooks.
  • Rate limits respected, even during migrations.
  • Outages are someone's alert, not a client's call.
  • Exits stay possible.

What we lose

  • Extra code: polling, queues, checks.
  • More monitoring to tune.
  • Production checks need care and permission.
  • Not every vendor offers what you need.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Webhooks only

    No fallback when they stop. Fix → poll for changes and alert on silence.

  • Trusting the sandbox

    It works in test; production differs. Fix → add read-only production contract checks.

  • Ignoring limits

    A migration hits 429s at peak. Fix → queue below the limit; use bulk endpoints.

  • Vendor words inside

    The vendor's model spreads through the code. Fix → an anti-corruption layer at the border.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every SaaS or vendor integration.
  • Payments, billing, warehouses, identity: anything critical.
  • Before a migration that hammers a vendor's API.
  • After a vendor outage surprised you.

Skip it when…

  • Non-critical tools where a day's delay doesn't matter (still keep an exit).

8Self-check quiz

Why poll if you have webhooks?

Webhooks can stop silently; polling catches what was missed.

How do you handle a rate limit?

A client-side queue below the limit, with backoff on 429s.

Why isn't the sandbox enough?

It often behaves differently from production; add read-only production checks.

What's a silence alert?

An alert when expected events stop arriving.

try it

Pick your most critical vendor integration. Ask: if its webhooks stopped for nine hours, when would we know? If its rate limit halved, what would break? Fix the worse answer this sprint, using the checklist.

remember!!

You don't control the caterers' kitchen: check deliveries, know their number, keep a backup.
