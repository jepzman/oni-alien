Backend for Frontend

TL;DR

One general-purpose API serving every client becomes a compromise: chatty for mobile, wrong-shaped for portals, and risky to change. A backend for frontend (BFF) is a small backend per kind of client, shaped for exactly what that client's screens need, and owned by that client's team. The core services stay general; each BFF composes them. Use it when clients really differ, and keep BFFs thin: shaping, not business rules.

1The situation Larder, 7 June 2027

Larder's clients have multiplied: the customer apps, the picker app, and since May a business portal where office managers manage standing orders. All share one general API in Larder Core. The customer app's order screen makes seven calls over 4G and takes 2.8 seconds; the portal needs completely different shapes; and every change for one client risks breaking another.

  • Mobile screens make many calls and download data they don't use.
  • Each client's needs pull the shared API in different directions.
  • Client teams wait on the core team for every shape change.

  • Self-Service for Business Customers (Grace, vol. 6): The same day, Grace reviews the portal's first month: 80 % of office changes now made there.

2Real-life analogy three pass windows

A hotel kitchen has three pass windows: one for the dining room (plated), one for takeaway (boxed), one for room service (on covered trays). Same kitchen, same food; each window prepares it for where it's going. Nobody plates a takeaway order and boxes it again at the door.

3How it solves it one backend per client type

ADR 0022 created two BFFs: a mobile BFF owned by Dee's team, with one endpoint per screen, and a portal BFF owned by Ben's team for office managers. The picker app kept its offline sync service. Business rules stayed in the core modules; BFFs only compose and shape. The order screen went from seven calls and 2.8 s to one call and 0.6 s, and Dee's team now changes its own screens without waiting.

  1. Check the clients really differ

    Different screens, networks, users and release rhythms.

  2. One BFF per kind of client

    Not per screen, not per team; per client type.

  3. Give it to the client team

    They change it with their screens.

  4. Keep it thin

    Compose and shape; no business rules.

  5. Keep the core general

    Module APIs stay client-agnostic.

  6. Measure the win

    Calls per screen, payload size, time to first render.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Fast screens: one call, small payloads.
  • Client teams move alone.
  • Core stays general and stable.
  • Changes for one client can't break another.
  • Clear ownership per client type.

What we lose

  • More services to run.
  • Duplicated composition across BFFs.
  • Business logic creeps in if not watched.
  • Overkill for one or two similar clients.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Business rules in the BFF

    Refund logic in the mobile BFF. Fix → keep rules in the core; BFFs shape only.

  • A BFF per screen

    Twelve tiny backends. Fix → one per kind of client.

  • Owned by the core team

    Client teams still wait. Fix → the client team owns its BFF.

  • BFF reading tables

    Bypassing the modules. Fix → call module APIs only.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Clients with very different needs (mobile, web portal, partners).
  • Chatty mobile screens on slow networks.
  • Client teams that release independently.
  • When the general API keeps growing special cases.

Skip it when…

  • One client, or clients that need the same shapes.
  • Small teams where one API is easy to adapt.

8Self-check quiz

What is a BFF?

A thin backend shaped for one kind of client, owned by that client's team.

What should a BFF not contain?

Business rules: those stay in the core.

How many BFFs?

One per kind of client, not per screen.

What improved for the order screen?

From seven calls and 2.8 s to one call and 0.6 s.

try it

Pick your slowest mobile screen. Count the calls it makes and the bytes it downloads versus what it shows. Sketch the single endpoint a BFF would offer for it, and estimate the saving before deciding.
