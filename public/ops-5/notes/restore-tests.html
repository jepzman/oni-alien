Can We Actually Restore?

TL;DR

A green “backup succeeded” every night proves only that something was written somewhere. A backup is real once it has been restored, checked and timed. Make restoring fast by design (snapshots and logs rather than huge copies), check the result with scripts, keep a copy in another region, restore a random backup every month, and run a full timed drill every quarter.

1The situation Larder, January 2028

Eli takes apart the 3 hours 40 minutes of 10 January. Copying the backup to a new server: 1 h 10. Restoring and replaying the logs: 1 h 50. Checking by hand that the data looked right: 40 minutes. Backups had run every night since 2024 and shown green every morning. Nobody had ever restored one end to end.

  • “Backup succeeded” measures writing, not recovering.
  • The slowest parts (copying, checking) were never designed.
  • All backups lived in the same region as the database.

2Real-life analogy the spare tyre in the boot

The spare tyre has been in the boot for years. The day you need it, it's flat, the jack is missing and the wheel nuts are too tight to undo. A spare only counts if you've checked it, and changed a wheel once, on a dry afternoon, when nothing depended on it.

3How it solves it fast by design, checked by script, practised on a schedule

Eli and Bram change four things from February:

  • Fast by design: restores use storage snapshots plus logs, not full dumps, so nothing large has to be copied.
  • Checked by script: row counts per table, yesterday's orders, the latest payment, the latest stock change. A restore passes or fails in minutes.
  • Another region: backups are copied to Paris daily (Ana's DR tiers).
  • Practised: every month a random backup of a tier 1 or 2 database is restored to a scratch server and checked automatically, with the time recorded. Every quarter there's a full drill onto fresh infrastructure, timed with a stopwatch.

The times: 3 h 40 on 10 January, 48 minutes at the February monthly test, 22 minutes at the 18 April drill. That's still outside tier 1's 15 minutes for a full rebuild, but a replica failover (under 2 minutes) covers tier 1 in almost every real failure. Restoring from scratch is now an hour's problem, not an afternoon's.

  1. Time your first full restore

    On fresh infrastructure, end to end.

  2. Find the slow parts

    Copying, replaying, checking.

  3. Design them faster

    Snapshots and logs; scripted checks.

  4. Keep a copy elsewhere

    Another region, another account.

  5. Restore a random backup monthly

    Automatically checked, timed.

  6. Drill quarterly

    Full rebuild, stopwatch, written result.

Vocabulary for the exam

Backup
A copy of data kept to recover from loss.
Restore
Bringing data back from a backup into a working system.
Snapshot
A point-in-time copy of storage, fast to create and restore.
Write-ahead log (WAL)
The database's record of changes, replayed to reach a point in time.
Point-in-time recovery
Restoring to a chosen moment using a snapshot plus logs.
Restore drill
A planned, timed restore onto fresh infrastructure.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Trusting “backup succeeded”

    It measures writing, not restoring. Fix → restore and time it.

  • Checking by eye

    Slow and unreliable. Fix → scripted checks.

  • Backups in the same place

    A region problem takes both. Fix → a copy elsewhere.

  • Testing only the newest backup

    Older ones may be broken. Fix → random backups.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Nobody has done a full restore.
  • Recovery targets exist (tiers).
  • Auditors or clients ask how you know backups work.
  • After changing databases or backup tools.

Skip it when…

  • Data you can rebuild from elsewhere in minutes.

8Self-check quiz

Where did the 3 h 40 go?

Copying the backup (1 h 10), restoring and replaying (1 h 50), checking by hand (40 min).

What made restores faster?

Snapshots plus logs instead of copies, and scripted checks.

How often are restores tested?

A random tier 1–2 backup monthly; a full timed drill quarterly.

What was the April drill's time?

22 minutes.

Why restore a random backup, not the newest?

Older backups can be broken too; you may need one.

what would you do?The monthly restore check fails: yesterday's orders are missing from the restored copy. What do you do?

Treat it as an incident (SEV3, maybe SEV2): find why the backup is incomplete, check other backups, fix it, and run the test again. A failed restore test is a gift: you found it on a quiet day.

try it

Restore your most important database from last night's backup onto a fresh server, with a stopwatch. Where did the time go? Write one script that checks the result.

remember!!

Nobody needs backups; everybody needs restores. Restore, check and time one every month.
