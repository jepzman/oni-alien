Breaking Things on Purpose

TL;DR

Some failures can only be understood by causing them. A game day is a planned, announced session where you break something on purpose, in daylight: one experiment at a time, each with a hypothesis of what should happen, a small blast radius, a stop button and a watcher. Then you compare what happened with what you believed, and turn every gap into an action. It's the only way “it should be fine” becomes “we've seen it”.

1The situation Larder, February 2028

By February Larder tests two failures every month: database failover and restores. Everything else is belief. What happens if a Core VM disappears at the busy hour? If WMS B stops sending events? If a store loses its line and its 4G? If the payment provider slows down? Each answer is “it should be fine”, and nobody has seen it.

  • Alerts, runbooks and fallbacks are trusted without being exercised.
  • People practise incidents only during real ones.
  • New stores inherit gaps nobody has looked for.

2Real-life analogy the fire brigade's training fire

Fire brigades don't learn on the bad night. They set real fires in a training building, on a planned day, with a safety officer and water ready, and practise exactly what they'll do. Afterwards they talk through what went well and what didn't. The fire is small, controlled and announced, and it's real enough to teach.

3How it solves it a hypothesis, a small blast radius, a stop button, a review

From March, Larder holds a game day on the second Thursday at 10:00, announced to Sam and the stores. Each experiment has a hypothesis (“if core-2 stops, checkout keeps working with no errors customers can see”), a small blast radius, a stop button and a watcher. There's one experiment at a time.

Game day 1, Thursday 9 March:

  • core-2 stopped: checkout is fine, as believed, but the alert links the wrong runbook.
  • WMS B events stopped: the absence alert fires in 5 minutes, as designed.
  • Utrecht's line and 4G pulled together: the heartbeat alert fires, but the “store on 4G” alert was never set up for Utrecht. The city runbook gets a check for it.

Game day 2 (13 April) practises the major-incident roles with a pretend region outage. The 18 April restore drill counts as a game day too. Each finding becomes an action with an owner.

  1. Pick a failure you only believe you handle

    From the dependency map and the risk register.

  2. Write the hypothesis

    What exactly should happen?

  3. Keep the blast radius small

    One VM, one store, a quiet hour.

  4. Announce it and name a watcher

    With a stop button.

  5. Run one experiment at a time

    Observe, write down.

  6. Turn gaps into actions

    Owners, dates, next game day.

Vocabulary for the exam

Game day
A planned session for practising failures and responses.
Chaos experiment
Deliberately injecting a failure to test a hypothesis about resilience.
Hypothesis
What you believe will happen when the failure occurs.
Blast radius
How much could be affected if the experiment goes wrong.
Stop button
A way to end the experiment immediately.
Tabletop exercise
Talking through a scenario without breaking anything.

4The maturity ladder where are we, and what is the next rung?

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Big experiments first

    A game day becomes an outage. Fix → small blast radius, one at a time.

  • No hypothesis

    You watch, but learn nothing. Fix → write what should happen first.

  • Not announced

    Stores and support think it's real. Fix → announce it, and say it's a test.

  • Findings not followed

    The same gaps next game day. Fix → actions with owners and dates.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • You believe failures are handled but haven't seen it.
  • New on-call people need practice.
  • Before a peak or a handover.
  • After building a fallback or alert.

Skip it when…

  • When basic monitoring and runbooks don't exist yet: build those first.

8Self-check quiz

What does every experiment need?

A hypothesis, a small blast radius, a stop button, a watcher, and an announcement.

What did game day 1 find?

A wrong runbook link on core-2's alert, and no “store on 4G” alert for Utrecht.

Why one experiment at a time?

So you know what caused what, and can stop cleanly.

What is a tabletop exercise?

Talking through a scenario without breaking anything (game day 2's region outage).

What happens to findings?

They become actions with owners and dates.

what would you do?During a game day, stopping core-2 unexpectedly makes checkout errors rise to 3 %. What does the watcher do?

Press stop: start core-2 again, check errors drop, and record it. The hypothesis was wrong, which is exactly what game days are for. Find why before trying again.

try it

Write one belief about a failure you handle (“if X stops, Y happens”). Plan the smallest safe way to test it, with a watcher and a stop button. What would you need to fix if the belief were wrong?

remember!!

Turn “it should be fine” into “we've seen it”: small, announced, with a stop button, on a planned day.
