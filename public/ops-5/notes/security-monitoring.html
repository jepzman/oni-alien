Is the Alarm Real?

TL;DR

You can't respond to an attack you can't see, or investigate one whose logs were deleted. Collect the few logs that show attacks (sign-ins, admin actions, access to customer data, exports, unusual API use) in one place, keep them as long as an investigation needs (agreed with legal), and alert on a handful of signals that matter, tuned until each alert is worth reading. Like operational alerts, security alerts must be actionable.

1The situation Larder, January 2028

Eli checks Larder's security signals in January. Sign-ins are in the identity provider, kept 30 days. Cloud admin actions are in the cloud's audit log, kept 90 days. The helpdesk and the business portal have their own logs, kept 14 days. Nobody looks at any of them unless something has already happened, and two of them would already be deleted by the time anyone asked. Korenwolf's supplier questionnaire, due in March, asks exactly how long logs are kept and who watches them.

  • Attacks are found by their consequences, not their signs.
  • Investigations hit deleted logs.
  • Signals sit in four places with four retention periods.

2Real-life analogy the shop's cameras and alarm

A shop has cameras at the doors, the till and the stockroom, not everywhere. Recordings are kept for a month. And there's an alarm for only a few things: a door opened at night, the safe opened outside hours. The guard isn't watching every screen; the alarm tells him where to look, and the recordings let him find out what happened.

3How it solves it six sources, one place, a year's retention, six alerts

From February, six sources go to one log store: sign-ins, cloud admin actions, break-glass use, production access requests, views and exports of customer data in the helpdesk and portal, and network traffic at the edge. They're kept for one year, agreed with Lena, with restricted data masked in the logs themselves.

Six alerts, to on-call:

  • Impossible travel: one account signing in from two far-apart places within an hour.
  • Many failed sign-ins on one account.
  • A new admin role granted.
  • Break-glass used (a page).
  • A large customer-data export (a page).
  • A business API key used from an unexpected country.

The first week brings 40 alerts, mostly staff on VPNs and travel. Tuning (known VPN exits, travel notices) brings it to 4 a week by April, each one worth reading. On 9 March, the impossible-travel alert is the one that matters (next note).

  1. List the logs that would show an attack

    Sign-ins, admin, data access, exports, API use.

  2. Send them to one place

    With the same clock and fields.

  3. Agree retention with legal

    Long enough to investigate; mask personal data.

  4. Write a few alerts that matter

    Each with a runbook.

  5. Tune until each alert is worth reading

    Known exits, travel, service accounts.

  6. Review the alert list quarterly

    Add what incidents teach.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Collecting everything

    Expensive and unreadable. Fix → the few logs that show attacks.

  • Too short retention

    The investigation finds nothing. Fix → agree it with legal.

  • Alerts nobody can act on

    Security fatigue, like alert fatigue. Fix → few, tuned, with runbooks.

  • Personal data in logs

    Logs become a privacy risk. Fix → mask restricted data.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Nobody would notice an account takeover.
  • A client asks how long logs are kept.
  • After a security review or a near miss.
  • Before letting more people access customer data.

Skip it when…

  • Never: at least sign-ins and admin actions belong in one place.

8Self-check quiz

Which logs did Larder collect?

Sign-ins, cloud admin actions, break-glass, production access requests, customer data views and exports, edge traffic.

How long are they kept, and who agreed it?

One year, agreed with Lena; restricted data masked.

Name three of the six alerts.

Impossible travel, many failed sign-ins, new admin role, break-glass, big export, API key from an unexpected country.

Why tune alerts?

Untuned, 40 a week were mostly harmless; tuned, 4 a week are worth reading.

Why mask personal data in logs?

Otherwise the logs themselves become a privacy risk.

what would you do?An impossible-travel alert fires for Mo's account: Amsterdam at 09:00, Lisbon at 09:20. Mo is in the office. What do you do?

Treat it as possibly real: confirm with Mo by phone (not chat), and if it isn't him, follow the security incident steps: revoke sessions, reset, preserve the logs.

try it

List where your sign-ins, admin actions and customer-data exports are logged, and how long each is kept. Could you answer “who exported customer data last March?” today? Write one alert you'd want.

remember!!

See attacks by their signs, not their consequences: a few logs, kept long enough, and a few alerts worth reading.
