The Plan Nobody Has Read

TL;DR

Continuity plans rot quietly: systems change, people leave, and the plan describes a company that no longer exists. Keep them short (a page or two per scenario), owned, stamped with a last-tested date, readable when your systems are down (printed, offline, in another account), and changed after every test or real use. A plan untested for a year is a warning, not a comfort.

1The situation Larder, January 2028

Looking for Larder's disaster recovery plan after the restore, Eli finds one: 31 pages, last edited in March 2026. It describes StockPilot, Larder Core on hand-patched VMs, two stores, and an on-call number that no longer exists. It lives on a shared drive in the same cloud it's meant to help recover. Nobody on the on-call rota has read it.

  • A long plan is written once and never read.
  • A plan stored in the failed system can't be opened when it's needed.
  • Without tests, nobody notices it's wrong.

2Real-life analogy the seat-pocket safety card

An aircraft's safety card is one page, mostly pictures, updated whenever the aircraft changes, and read out before every flight. Somewhere there's also a hundred-page manual nobody opens. When something goes wrong, people use the card.

3How it solves it short plans per scenario, readable offline, changed after every test

In February Eli replaces the 31 pages with five plans of one or two pages each, one per scenario: the ordering database lost, a cloud region down, a store cut off (paper mode), a key vendor out, and the office and sign-in down (this volume). Each plan has the same parts: the trigger, who decides, the steps (linking to runbooks), how to switch back, an owner and a last tested date.

  • Readable when systems are down: in the repo, printed in the store binders, and copied every week to laptops and a second cloud account.
  • Changed after every use: every test or real invocation changes the plan within a week.
  • Tested at least yearly: a plan untested for 12 months turns red on the monthly service review.

By June every plan has been tested once: two by game days, one by the April restore drill, one by Zuid's paper drill, and one for real (the office plan, in May).

  1. Find every plan and check its date

    And what it describes.

  2. Split into one plan per scenario

    One or two pages each.

  3. Give each an owner and a last-tested date

    Visible on the plan.

  4. Make them readable when systems are down

    Printed, offline, another account.

  5. Change the plan after every test

    Within a week.

  6. Turn untested plans red

    On the monthly review.

Vocabulary for the exam

Continuity plan
The steps to keep the business running through a specific disruption.
Scenario
A specific kind of disruption the plan is for.
Plan rot
A plan drifting out of date as systems and people change.
Last tested
The date a plan was last exercised for real or in a drill.
Offline copy
A copy that can be read without the systems it's about.
Plan owner
The person who keeps a plan current and tested.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • One giant plan

    Nobody reads it. Fix → one short plan per scenario.

  • Stored only in the failing system

    Can't be opened when needed. Fix → printed and offline copies.

  • Never changed after tests

    Lessons lost. Fix → change within a week.

  • No owner

    It rots. Fix → an owner per plan.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • You find a plan nobody has read.
  • Systems have changed since the plan was written.
  • After a test or real use.
  • Before an audit or questionnaire.

Skip it when…

  • Never, if you have anything to recover.

what would you do?A game day shows the region plan's step 4 uses a command that no longer exists. Whose job is it, and by when?

The plan's owner (Eli), within a week; and the test date goes on the plan. That's the plan doing its job: finding the rot before a real outage.

try it

Find your continuity or DR plan. When was it last changed, and does it describe today's systems? Could you open it if your main systems were down? Rewrite the most important scenario on one page.

remember!!

A plan is short, owned, tested and readable offline, or it's a document about a company that no longer exists.
