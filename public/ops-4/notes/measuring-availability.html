How Available Were We, Really?

TL;DR

“Availability” can mean very different numbers depending on where and how you count. Server logs miss failures outside your servers, request counts treat a slow minute differently from a dead one, and a contract may count whole minutes. Measure with real requests and outside checks, write precise rules for partial outages and maintenance, publish the numbers side by side, and explain the gap. The honest number is the one your customer would compute.

1The situation Larder, October–November 2027

For October, Eli has three numbers for business ordering, all called “availability”. The servers' own logs say 99.95 %. The request-success SLI says 99.86 % (the month the budget ran out). The outside checks, counting down minutes the way the new contract does, say 99.79 %, just under the 99.8 % Ruben is about to sign. Ruben will read the third. The gap is made of minutes when DNS or the content network failed outside Larder's servers, and minutes when a few percent of requests failed.

  • The flattering number is the one nobody outside sees.
  • Failures outside your servers never reach your server logs.
  • Without written rules, every month's number is a negotiation.

2Real-life analogy the open shop on a closed road

A shop's own clock says the door was unlocked from 08:00 to 18:00 every day: 100 % open. But on three mornings the road outside was closed, and no customer could get in. Asked how available the shop was, the honest answer counts the road too, because the customer's trip does.

3How it solves it real requests plus outside checks, precise rules, both numbers published

From November, availability is measured two ways:

  • Real requests at the load balancer: good ÷ valid, the SLI.
  • Outside checks every 60 seconds from two locations in different countries. Each one logs in as a test office manager and places a test business order, like a real customer would.

The rules are written down. A minute is down if more than 5 % of requests fail, or if both outside checks fail. A partial outage counts by its share in the SLI, and as a down minute in the contract measure. Planned maintenance always counts internally, even where the contract excludes it, so nobody is tempted to call an outage “maintenance”.

Both numbers go side by side on the monthly review, with the gap explained. November: request SLI 99.92 %, contract minutes 99.81 %, matching Ana's measurement. October's DNS minutes lead to a second DNS provider in December.

  1. List every “availability” number you have

    And where each is counted.

  2. Add outside checks that act like a customer

    Two locations, every minute, a real flow.

  3. Write the rules

    Down minute, partial outage, maintenance.

  4. Count maintenance internally

    Always, whatever the contract says.

  5. Publish the numbers side by side

    With the gap explained.

  6. Fix what the gap shows

    Often outside your servers: DNS, CDN, vendors.

Vocabulary for the exam

Availability
The share of time (or requests) a service works for its users.
Probe / synthetic check
A robot that uses the service like a customer, on a schedule.
Down minute
A minute counted as unavailable under written rules.
Partial outage
Some requests or users fail while others work.
Agreed service time
The hours a service is promised (24/7, or 06:00–23:00).
Measurement gap
The difference between numbers counted in different places.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Reporting the flattering number

    The client computes a lower one. Fix → publish the customer's number.

  • Checks from inside the cloud

    They fail with you, or succeed while users can't reach you. Fix → outside, two locations.

  • Maintenance as an excuse

    Outages relabelled. Fix → count it internally, always.

  • A check that only pings

    The home page loads; ordering doesn't. Fix → check a real flow.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • You promise availability in a contract.
  • Different numbers circulate for the same thing.
  • Customers report outages your logs don't show.
  • Before signing an SLA.

Skip it when…

  • Internal tools without promises: the SLI is enough.

8Self-check quiz

Why did the server logs say 99.95 % when the contract measure said 99.79 %?

Failures outside the servers (DNS, content network) and minutes with partial failures never reached the server logs.

What do Larder's outside checks do?

Every 60 s from two countries, they log in as a test office manager and place a test business order.

When is a minute down?

When more than 5 % of requests fail, or both outside checks fail.

Why count maintenance internally even when the contract excludes it?

So outages can't be relabelled as maintenance, and the internal picture stays honest.

What did the October gap lead to?

A second DNS provider in December.

what would you do?A client's IT team says your availability last month was 99.6 %, but your report says 99.85 %. What do you do?

Compare how each was counted: where, how often, what counts as down. Share your rules and raw down minutes. If their measure is closer to what users felt, learn from it; if not, agree one method together.

try it

Collect every availability number you report and write where it's counted. Set up one outside check that performs a real user flow every minute. How does its number compare with the others?

remember!!

Count availability the way the customer would: their trip, not your door. Publish the numbers side by side and explain the gap.
