What Do Users Count as Working?

TL;DR

A service level indicator (SLI) measures one thing users care about, as good events divided by all events: successful checkouts over attempts, fast requests over all requests, orders assigned in time over all orders. Measure it where the user meets the service (the load balancer, the app), not on the servers. A few SLIs per service turn “how reliable are we?” into numbers the business recognises.

  • Family: making promises
  • Lives in: ops/slo/<service>.yml, computed from request logs and app events
  • Pairs with: SLOs, error budgets, measuring availability

1The situation Larder, Monday 5 July 2027

At the board prep on 5 July Femke asks: “How reliable are we, actually?” Korenwolf's renewal is in the autumn, and Ruben wants a number in the contract. Eli has server uptime (99.98 %), incident counts and the orders-affected line from his monthly page. None of them answers “did it work for the person using it?” Server uptime says nothing about a checkout failing at the payment step, a business order timing out, or a packed order waiting for a courier.

  • Machines can be up while customers fail.
  • Every team measures something different.
  • A contract number needs a precise definition first.

2Real-life analogy the train company's punctuality

A train company could count trains that left the depot, or engines that started. Passengers count one thing: did my train arrive within five minutes of the timetable? That's the number on the station wall, worked out as trains on time divided by all trains. It's the operator's job to make that number good, whatever the engines did.

3How it solves it a few good ÷ total numbers per service, where the user is

In July Eli writes five SLIs with the service owners, each as good events ÷ all events:

  • Checkout success: successful checkouts ÷ attempts. Card declines don't count, because a declined card isn't Larder failing.
  • Checkout speed: checkouts faster than 800 ms ÷ all checkouts (Ana's performance budget).
  • Business ordering: portal and API requests that succeed ÷ all requests.
  • Dispatch: packed orders assigned a courier within 90 s ÷ all packed orders.
  • Store sync: store-minutes with stock less than 2 minutes old ÷ all store-minutes in opening hours.

They're measured where the user meets the service: at the load balancer, in the apps and in the store heartbeat, not on the servers. June's values are 99.86 %, 96.1 %, 99.74 %, 98.9 % and 99.6 %. Femke's answer now fits on one line per service. The next question, how good is good enough, is the next note.

  1. Ask what users count as working

    Per service, in their words.

  2. Write it as good ÷ all

    Define both: what's an event, what's good.

  3. Exclude what isn't your failure

    Card declines; requests from tests.

  4. Measure where the user is

    Load balancer, app, device, not the server.

  5. Keep it to a few per service

    Success and speed are usually enough.

  6. Compute a baseline

    Last month's value, before setting any target.

Vocabulary for the exam

SLI (service level indicator)
A measured share of good events: what users experience, as a number.
Good event
An event that met the user's need: a success, fast enough, on time.
Valid event
An event that counts at all (tests and card declines excluded).
Latency SLI
Share of requests faster than a threshold.
Freshness SLI
Share of time data is newer than a threshold.
Measurement point
Where an SLI is counted; closer to the user is better.
ops/slo/checkout.ymlSLIs · July 2027
service: checkout                     owner: Ben           tier: critical
slis:
  success:
    good:   checkout requests with status ok (2xx)
    valid:  all checkout attempts, excluding card declines and test baskets
    where:  load balancer logs + app event checkout_completed
    june:   99.86 %
  speed:
    good:   checkout completed in < 800 ms (server side) and < 2.5 s (app side)
    valid:  all completed checkouts
    june:   96.1 %
not an SLI: server uptime (99.98 %) · CPU · number of incidents

tools: request logs at the load balancer, app events, a small job that computes good ÷ valid per minute.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Measuring the servers

    Up machines, failing customers. Fix → measure where the user is.

  • Counting what isn't yours

    Card declines make checkout look broken. Fix → define valid events.

  • Too many SLIs

    Nobody knows which one matters. Fix → success and speed, mostly.

  • Averages instead of shares

    An average speed hides the slow tail. Fix → share of requests under a threshold.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Someone asks how reliable you are.
  • A client wants a number in a contract.
  • Teams argue about whether a service is “fine”.
  • Before setting any target.

Skip it when…

  • Internal tools nobody depends on: a heartbeat is enough.

8Self-check quiz

What is an SLI?

A measured share of good events among valid events, for something users care about.

Why exclude card declines from checkout success?

A declined card isn't Larder failing; counting it would hide real failures.

Where should SLIs be measured?

Where the user meets the service: load balancer, apps, devices.

Why share-under-a-threshold rather than average speed?

Averages hide the slow tail that users feel.

Why isn't server uptime an SLI?

Servers can be up while customers fail.

what would you do?Sam says customers complain about slow checkout, but the checkout speed SLI looks fine. What do you check?

Where it's measured: the server-side number may miss slow networks and the app. Add (or look at) the app-side measure, and split by city and device.

try it

Pick your most important service and write two SLIs as good ÷ valid: one for success, one for speed. Where would you measure them? Compute last month's values.

remember!!

Measure what users count, as good ÷ all, and measure it where they meet the service.
