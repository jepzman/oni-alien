What Did We Sign?

TL;DR

A service level agreement (SLA) is a promise to a customer with consequences if it's missed. It needs a precise definition: what's covered, how it's measured, over what period, what counts as down, what's excluded, and what happens if it's missed. Keep it looser than your internal SLO, so you notice and act before you owe anything, and report it automatically from the same data you run on.

1The situation Larder, October 2027

For the Korenwolf renewal, Ruben's first draft says: “Larder guarantees 99.9 % availability of ordering.” It doesn't say what “ordering” means, how it's measured, over what period, what counts as down, whether announced maintenance counts, or what happens if it's missed. And 99.9 % is exactly Larder's own internal target, with no room between the promise and the target.

  • A vague SLA becomes an argument every time something goes wrong.
  • An SLA equal to the SLO means owing money the moment the target slips.
  • A report computed by hand each month is slow, and easy to dispute.

2Real-life analogy the parcel company's guarantee

A parcel company guarantees delivery by 12:00 the next working day, measured by the time of the delivery scan, for parcels handed in before 18:00. If it's late, the shipping fee comes back. Its own internal target is 11:00, so it has room before it owes anyone money. Every word in the guarantee is there to stop an argument later.

3How it solves it precise words, a buffer below the SLO, and an automatic report

Mo, Eli and Lena rewrite the service levels with Ruben in November. They're signed with the renewal in December:

  • Ordering availability: 99.8 % per calendar month. Covers the portal and the business API, counted per minute. A minute is down if more than 5 % of requests fail, measured by Larder's outside checks every minute plus real requests. Maintenance announced 5 working days ahead and outside 06:00–20:00 doesn't count.
  • If missed: a 5 % credit on that month's service fee; below 99.5 %, 10 %.
  • Deliveries: as in 2026, 98 % complete by 09:00, with 10 % of a missed delivery credited automatically.
  • Reported automatically every month in the portal (Grace's service levels, live since 4 October), from the same data Larder uses for its SLOs.

The internal SLO stays at 99.9 %, so the error budget policy kicks in long before a credit is owed. Ruben still wanted 99.9 %. It became a joint goal for 2028: worked on together, not promised. Ana's availability work (measured 99.81 % on the business path in November) shows the 99.8 % is safe to sign.

  1. Define what's covered

    Which services, which users.

  2. Define how it's measured

    Which data, how often, what's a down minute.

  3. Set the period and the exclusions

    Calendar month; announced maintenance.

  4. Agree the consequences

    Credits, simple and capped.

  5. Keep it below your SLO

    Room to act before you owe.

  6. Report it automatically

    From the data you run on.

Vocabulary for the exam

SLA (service level agreement)
A promise to a customer about service levels, with consequences.
Service credit
Money back (or off the next invoice) when an SLA is missed.
Exclusion
Something agreed not to count, such as announced maintenance.
Measurement period
The window an SLA is judged over: a calendar month here.
Buffer
The room between the internal SLO and the external SLA.
Joint goal
A shared aim worked on together, without contractual consequences.

4The maturity ladder where are we, and what is the next rung?

1By hand

a number in a sentence

  • Vague, disputed.
  • Equal to the internal target.

2Written down Larder now

a precise SLA with a buffer

  • Definitions, exclusions, credits.
  • Reported automatically.

3Scripted

SLAs from a catalogue

  • Standard service levels per offer.
  • Credits computed automatically.

4Automatic

SLAs rarely tested

  • SLOs and budgets keep service well above the promise.
  • Credits almost never paid.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • SLA equal to SLO

    You owe money whenever the target slips. Fix → keep the SLA looser.

  • No definition of down

    Every incident becomes a dispute. Fix → a down minute, precisely.

  • Hand-made reports

    Slow and easy to argue with. Fix → automatic, from the same data.

  • Promising the stretch goal

    Today's hope becomes tomorrow's penalty. Fix → a joint goal, not a commitment.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A client asks for guarantees.
  • A renewal or a new contract.
  • Disputes about whether something was “down”.
  • Before promising a number in a sales deck.

Skip it when…

  • Consumers who use the app without a contract: SLOs are enough.

8Self-check quiz

What must an SLA define?

What's covered, how it's measured, the period, what counts as down, exclusions, and consequences.

Why is Larder's SLA 99.8 % when the SLO is 99.9 %?

To leave room: Larder notices and acts on its error budget before owing credits.

What counts as a down minute in Korenwolf's SLA?

A minute in which more than 5 % of portal and API requests fail, by outside checks and real requests.

What happens if the SLA is missed?

A 5 % credit on the month's fee; 10 % below 99.5 %.

What happened to Ruben's 99.9 %?

It became a joint goal for 2028, not a contractual promise.

what would you do?A sales colleague wants to offer a new office client “99.95 % availability” to win the deal. What do you say?

Larder's SLO is 99.9 %, so promising more means owing credits often. Offer the standard 99.8 % with precise definitions, and a joint goal if they want ambition, or price the extra reliability honestly.

try it

Find a promise your team has made to a customer (in a contract, a sales deck or an email). Rewrite it with what's covered, how it's measured, the period, what's down, exclusions and consequences. Is it looser than your internal target?

remember!!

An SLA is a promise with consequences: define every word, report it automatically, and keep it looser than your SLO.
