When Do We Stop Shipping Features?

TL;DR

If the SLO is 99.9 %, the other 0.1 % is the error budget: the unreliability you've agreed to accept. While there's budget left, ship features and take risks. When it's spent, a policy written in advance says what happens: features pause for that service, and the team works on reliability until it's back. Burn-rate alerts warn while there's still time. It turns “features or reliability?” from an argument into a rule both sides signed.

1The situation Larder, September 2027

The business ordering SLO is 99.9 %, but the 28-day value has slid to 99.86 %: a slow API release here, a flaky portal deploy there, none of them big enough to be an incident. Grace's team has three more portal features planned for the autumn. Eli thinks reliability should come first, with Korenwolf's renewal coming up. Grace thinks the features are what Korenwolf is asking for. They have no agreed rule for who wins.

  • An SLO without consequences is just a number on a page.
  • Reliability work loses to features, until there's an incident.
  • The decision ends up with whoever argues hardest.

2Real-life analogy the eating-out envelope

A family keeps a monthly envelope for eating out. While there's money in it, they eat out when they like, no questions. When it's empty, they cook at home until next month. Nobody argues about it, because the rule was agreed before anyone was hungry.

3How it solves it a budget, a signed policy, and alerts while there's time

Eli drafts the error budget policy with Grace, and Priya and Femke sign it on Monday 13 September. It applies per service:

  • More than half the budget left: ship as normal.
  • Less than a quarter left: high-risk changes need Eli's agreement, and every release goes through a canary.
  • Budget spent: feature releases for that service pause, and the team works on reliability until the 28-day value is back above the SLO. Exceptions are made only by Femke, in writing.

Burn-rate alerts come with it. Spending 2 % of the month's budget in one hour pages on-call; 10 % in six hours makes a ticket. Most of the old threshold alerts are retired.

In October business ordering spends its budget. The portal pauses for 9 working days. Ben's team fixes the API's retries and a slow query, and the value goes back to 99.93 %. Grace ships the next portal feature afterwards, and tells Mo the pause was the reason Korenwolf's mornings got calmer. Nobody had to argue.

  1. Compute the budget from the SLO

    100 % − SLO, over the window.

  2. Write what happens at each level

    Plenty, low, spent.

  3. Have the business sign it

    Product and the CEO, before the first fight.

  4. Alert on burn rate

    Fast burn pages; slow burn makes a ticket.

  5. Apply it when it hurts

    That's when it matters.

  6. Review it with the SLOs

    Quarterly.

Vocabulary for the exam

Error budget
The amount of failure an SLO allows over its window.
Error budget policy
Written, signed rules for what happens as the budget is used up.
Burn rate
How fast the budget is being spent, relative to the window.
Fast burn / slow burn
A sudden big spend (page) vs a steady leak (ticket).
Feature freeze
Pausing feature releases for a service while reliability recovers.
Budget exception
A documented decision to ship anyway, by a named leader.

4The maturity ladder where are we, and what is the next rung?

1By hand

features vs reliability by argument

  • Whoever argues hardest wins.
  • Reliability waits for incidents.

2Written down Larder now

a signed policy and burn alerts

  • Three levels, a pause when spent.
  • Exceptions by name.

3Scripted

budget in planning

  • Each team sees its budget weekly.
  • Risky work planned when budget is high.

4Automatic

budget as permission

  • Teams spend it on purpose: experiments, migrations.
  • Rarely spent by accident.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A policy nobody signed

    Ignored the first time it costs a feature. Fix → product and the CEO sign it.

  • Budget as punishment

    Teams hide failures. Fix → it's permission to take risk, not a scorecard.

  • Exceptions by habit

    Every pause gets overruled. Fix → Femke only, in writing, visible.

  • Alerting on thresholds still

    Pages for blips that don't touch the budget. Fix → burn-rate alerts.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Features and reliability compete for the same team.
  • An SLO is being missed without consequences.
  • You want fewer, better pages.
  • Before a contract promise depends on a service.

Skip it when…

  • Services without SLOs: set those first.

8Self-check quiz

What is an error budget?

The amount of failure the SLO allows over its window (100 % − SLO).

What happens when the budget is spent?

Feature releases for that service pause; the team works on reliability until it's back above the SLO.

Who can make an exception?

Only Femke, in writing.

What are Larder's burn-rate alerts?

2 % of the budget in one hour pages; 10 % in six hours makes a ticket.

What happened in October?

Business ordering spent its budget; the portal paused 9 working days; fixes brought it back to 99.93 %.

what would you do?The checkout budget is spent with two days of the window left, and marketing has a campaign feature due tomorrow. What happens?

The policy says features pause. Marketing can ask Femke for a written exception; she decides with the budget status in front of her. Otherwise the feature waits until checkout is back above its SLO.

try it

For one service with an SLO, compute its error budget and how much of it you've used this month. Write three sentences: what happens with plenty left, with little left, and when it's spent. Ask your product owner to sign them.

remember!!

The budget is permission to take risk. When it's spent, features wait, and the rule was agreed before anyone was hungry.
