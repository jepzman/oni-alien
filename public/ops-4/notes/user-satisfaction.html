Asking the Users

TL;DR

SLOs measure whether systems work; they can't measure whether using them is any good. Batteries that die by evening, slow screens, a Wi-Fi that's “up” but weak: none of it breaks an SLO. Ask the people who use IT, regularly and briefly (three questions, in their language, on a phone), act on the top answers, and tell them what you changed. Green numbers and unhappy people means you're measuring the wrong thing.

1The situation Larder, November 2027

In November every SLO is green: checkout, Dispatch, store sync, business ordering. Then Inès, the new Antwerp store manager, says on a call that her pickers hate the handhelds. Tomás agrees: by 21:00 half of Noord's older handhelds are on their last bar of battery, and pickers queue at the chargers. None of this shows in any SLO.

  • A system can work perfectly and still be miserable to use.
  • Staff stop reporting what they think IT can't fix.
  • Annoyances cost picking speed long before they cause incidents.

2Real-life analogy the hotel's hot water

A hotel's systems all say fine: the lifts work, the Wi-Fi is up, the rooms are cleaned on time. But guests complain that the shower takes five minutes to get hot. No system measures that. The best hotels ask three questions after every stay, fix the top answer, and tell the next guests what changed.

3How it solves it three questions a quarter, act on the top answers, say what changed

From November a quarterly IT pulse goes to store staff, support agents and office managers: three questions, in Dutch, French or English, on a phone, about two minutes.

  1. How well does IT help you do your job? (1–5, per thing: handhelds, Wi-Fi, desk, portal)
  2. What's the most annoying thing this quarter?
  3. What should we fix first?

November brings 214 answers. Handhelds score 3.1 (the older model is slow, and batteries die by evening). The service desk gets 4.2, Antwerp's Wi-Fi 2.8, and office managers give the portal 4.0. The actions: spare batteries and a mid-shift swap at every store from December, the older handheld model moved up the lifecycle register for replacement in 2028, and a Wi-Fi survey in Antwerp. The results go on a “you said, we did” poster in every store, in the same format as Grace's operations feedback loop.

  1. Ask the people who use IT, not their managers

    Pickers, agents, office managers.

  2. Keep it to three questions

    Two minutes, on a phone, in their language.

  3. Ask regularly

    Quarterly is enough to see trends.

  4. Act on the top answers

    Even small fixes: batteries.

  5. Tell them what changed

    Or they stop answering.

  6. Put the scores next to the SLOs

    Experience and reliability together.

Vocabulary for the exam

User satisfaction
How people feel about using a service, asked directly.
Pulse survey
A short, regular survey with a few fixed questions.
XLA (experience level agreement)
A newer idea: targets based on user experience, not only system metrics.
Proxy metric
A number that stands in for what you really care about (SLIs stand in for happiness).
Watermelon effect
Green on the outside (metrics), red on the inside (experience).
Closing the loop
Telling people what you did with their feedback.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Asking managers instead of users

    You hear about the managers' problems. Fix → ask the pickers and agents.

  • Long surveys

    Nobody answers. Fix → three questions.

  • Asking without acting

    Answers stop after one round. Fix → act and say what you did.

  • Trusting only green metrics

    The watermelon effect. Fix → experience next to reliability.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Metrics are green and people still complain.
  • Staff stopped reporting annoyances.
  • A new site or country (Antwerp).
  • Before a big device or tool replacement.

Skip it when…

  • When you already talk to every user weekly: just write it down.

8Self-check quiz

Why did no SLO show the handheld problem?

SLOs measured whether systems worked; batteries and slowness made them miserable to use without failing.

What are the three pulse questions?

How well IT helps you (1–5 per thing), the most annoying thing, and what to fix first.

What were the lowest scores in November?

Antwerp Wi-Fi 2.8 and handhelds 3.1.

What's the watermelon effect?

Green metrics on the outside, red experience on the inside.

Why post “you said, we did”?

So people see their answers lead to changes, and keep answering.

what would you do?The pulse says the portal is annoying because orders can't be changed after 16:00. Is that IT's to fix?

Not alone: it's a product decision (Grace), maybe for good operational reasons. Pass it on with the numbers, and report back in “you said, we did” what was decided and why.

try it

Ask ten people who use your systems daily the three questions this week. What's the top annoyance? Fix the smallest version of it, and tell them.

remember!!

Uptime measures the system; asking measures the experience. Three questions, act, and say what changed.
