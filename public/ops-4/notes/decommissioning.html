Switching a System Off

TL;DR

Switching a system off is a change, and often a risky one: someone you didn't know about is still using it. Retire systems on purpose. Find users from the logs, not from memory. Announce a date. Go read-only for a while so anyone still depending on it shouts. Keep the data the law and finance need. Remove every trace (keys, rules, alerts, runbooks). Cancel the contract, delete, and update the catalogue.

1The situation Larder, July 2027

Ana's application portfolio review on 19 July marks four applications to eliminate: a survey tool, a second chat tool, an old cron server and a legacy reporting database, together about €2,300 a month. StockPilot is to be switched off on 15 September, once the warehouse move is done. Larder has never switched a system off on purpose. The last attempt, an old reporting job in 2026, was turned off on a Friday and back on on Monday, because finance's month-end report broke.

  • Nobody knows everyone who uses a system.
  • Data that must be kept can disappear with the system.
  • Credentials, rules and alerts for dead systems linger for years.

2Real-life analogy moving out of a flat

Moving out of a flat isn't just stopping the rent. You redirect the post, read the meters, keep the contract papers, cancel the internet, return the keys, and tell the people who still send letters there. Miss one, and something important arrives at an address you no longer have.

3How it solves it users from logs, notice, read-only, keep, remove, cancel, delete

Eli writes a decommissioning checklist and uses it first on StockPilot:

  1. Find users from the logs: 30 days of access logs, not just the users people know about. StockPilot's logs show a finance spreadsheet pulling stock history every Monday. Marco's team moves it to the data team's export in August.
  2. Announce with four weeks' notice and a date.
  3. Read-only for two weeks: nothing can change, everything can still be read. Anyone who needs something shouts now.
  4. Keep the data the law and finance need: three years of stock movements exported and archived, with Lena's retention date on it.
  5. Remove every trace: API keys, network rules, monitoring and alerts, runbooks, catalogue and asset-register entries.
  6. Cancel the contract, then delete.

StockPilot is switched off on 15 September, and the bridge contract ends on 30 September (about €84k a year). The four smaller systems, and three 2026 Dispatch leftovers that Ana's sweep found (a courier cron fallback, a Core database view and a nightly copy job), are gone by 30 September. Nothing has to be switched back on.

  1. Find users from logs

    30 days, every caller, not just the known.

  2. Announce a date

    Four weeks' notice is common.

  3. Go read-only first

    Dependents shout while you can still help.

  4. Keep what must be kept

    Export, archive, retention date (legal).

  5. Remove every trace

    Keys, rules, alerts, runbooks, catalogue, assets.

  6. Cancel, delete, and record it

    The catalogue says it's retired, and when.

Vocabulary for the exam

Decommissioning
Retiring a system or service on purpose.
Hidden dependency
A user or system relying on something nobody knew about.
Read-only period
A time when a system can be read but not changed, before switching off.
Retention
How long data must be kept, by law or policy.
Orphaned credential
An API key or account for a system that no longer exists.
Retired service
A service listed as gone, with its date, in the catalogue.

4The maturity ladder where are we, and what is the next rung?

1By hand

switch off and hope

  • Users found by breaking them.
  • Traces left behind.

2Written down Larder now

a decommissioning checklist

  • Logs, notice, read-only, keep, remove, cancel.
  • Catalogue updated.

3Scripted

retirement planned at launch

  • Every new service records its users and data from day one.
  • Retirement is cheap.

4Automatic

regular pruning

  • Unused services flagged automatically.
  • Retired yearly as routine.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Users from memory

    The spreadsheet nobody mentioned. Fix → read 30 days of logs.

  • Deleting data with the system

    Records the law requires are gone. Fix → export and archive with a retention date.

  • Leaving traces

    Keys and network rules for a dead system. Fix → remove every trace, then check.

  • No read-only period

    Dependents find out by breaking. Fix → read-only first.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A system is being replaced.
  • A portfolio review marks systems to eliminate.
  • A contract is ending.
  • You find a system nobody can explain.

Skip it when…

  • Truly unused test resources with no data: delete them (but check the logs).

8Self-check quiz

Why find users from logs, not memory?

Hidden users exist; StockPilot's logs found a finance spreadsheet nobody had mentioned.

What's the read-only period for?

Anyone still depending on the system finds out and shouts while it can still be read.

What data did Larder keep from StockPilot?

Three years of stock movements, archived with Lena's retention date.

What traces must be removed?

API keys, network rules, monitoring and alerts, runbooks, catalogue and asset entries.

What was saved?

About €84k a year for StockPilot and about €2.3k a month for the four smaller systems.

what would you do?During the read-only period, someone in marketing says the survey tool holds three years of customer feedback they still use. What do you do?

Pause the switch-off date if needed, export what they need to where they can use it (with Lena on personal data and retention), then continue. That's exactly what the read-only period is for.

try it

Pick one system nobody seems to use. Read 30 days of its access logs. Who still calls it? Write the checklist for switching it off, including what data must be kept and for how long.

remember!!

Switching off is a change. Find users from the logs, go read-only first, keep what the law needs, and remove every trace.
