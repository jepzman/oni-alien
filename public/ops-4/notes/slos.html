How Good Is Good Enough?

TL;DR

A service level objective (SLO) is a target for an SLI over a time window: “99.9 % of checkouts succeed over 28 days”. It's set with the business, just above what customers notice, and never 100 %, because 100 % means never changing anything and still failing. Every extra nine costs a lot more than the last, so raising a target means saying what it costs. Review SLOs every quarter.

1The situation Larder, July 2027

With SLIs in place, Eli asks what the target for checkout should be. Priya says 100 %. Femke, remembering Christmas, says 99.99 %. Ben points out that June was 99.86 % and asks what 99.99 would cost: Ana's options page from January said a second region, about €9,000 a month and two engineers. Nobody has said what customers would actually notice, or what the business would give up to get there.

  • 100 % sounds right and means no changes, ever.
  • Each extra nine costs much more than the last.
  • A target nobody chose with the business becomes an engineering wish.

2Real-life analogy the pizza place's 30 minutes

A pizza place could promise delivery in 20 minutes, but it would need twice the drivers and would still miss sometimes. It promises 30 minutes: customers are happy with that, and the shop keeps it almost every time. It checks the share every month, and if customers start leaving for a faster rival, it rethinks. Good enough is a choice, and it has a price.

3How it solves it targets just above what customers notice, chosen with the business

On Monday 2 August Eli meets Femke, Priya, Joost and Mo with one page per service: the SLI, last six months' values, what customers notice (from Sam's tickets and Grace's numbers), and what each extra nine would cost. They agree five SLOs over a rolling 28 days:

  • Checkout success 99.9 %: about 40 minutes' worth of failures a month. Customers barely notice below that, and they do notice above.
  • Checkout speed 95 % under 800 ms.
  • Business ordering 99.9 %, deliberately higher than the 99.8 % Mo will offer Ruben in the contract.
  • Dispatch 99 % assigned within 90 seconds, and store sync 99.5 % fresh.

None is 100 %. Raising a target later requires saying what it costs, in money, people and slower change. They're reviewed every quarter, the first time in November.

  1. Show the SLI's history

    Six months of values.

  2. Find what customers notice

    Tickets, complaints, churn around bad days.

  3. Price each extra nine

    Money, people, slower change.

  4. Agree the target with the business

    Owners sign it.

  5. Choose a window

    Rolling 28 days is common.

  6. Review quarterly

    And when the business changes.

Vocabulary for the exam

SLO (service level objective)
A target value for an SLI over a time window.
Nines
99.9 % is three nines; each extra nine is ten times stricter.
Rolling window
The last 28 days, recalculated every day.
Aspirational SLO
A target you aim for but don't yet promise or enforce.
Over-reliability
Being more reliable than users need, at a cost to speed of change.
Target vs promise
An SLO is internal; an SLA is a promise with consequences.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • 100 % as a target

    No change is ever safe enough. Fix → just above what customers notice.

  • Engineers choose alone

    A target the business doesn't own. Fix → agree it with the business, signed.

  • No price per nine

    Raising targets looks free. Fix → say what each nine costs.

  • Never reviewing

    The target outlives the business it served. Fix → quarterly.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • You have SLIs and someone asks “is that good?”
  • A client wants a promise.
  • Leaders ask for “four nines”.
  • Teams disagree about how much reliability work is enough.

Skip it when…

  • Services with no real users: no target needed.

8Self-check quiz

What is an SLO?

A target for an SLI over a time window, like 99.9 % of checkouts succeeding over 28 days.

Why never 100 %?

It means no changes are ever safe enough, and it still can't be met.

Why is the business ordering SLO (99.9 %) higher than the SLA (99.8 %)?

So Larder notices and acts before a contract promise is at risk.

Who signs the SLOs?

The business owners: Femke, Priya, Joost, Mo, per service.

What must accompany a request to raise a target?

What it costs: money, people and slower change.

what would you do?After a bad week, Femke asks to raise checkout to 99.99 %. What do you bring to the conversation?

What 99.99 % means (≈ 4 minutes a month), what customers noticed in the bad week, and the cost (Ana's second-region numbers or cheaper options). Then she decides with the price in front of her.

try it

For one service, put six months of its SLI on one page with what customers noticed and what one extra nine would cost. Propose a target and ask a business owner to agree or change it.

remember!!

Good enough is a choice with a price. Set it with the business, just above what customers notice, and never at 100 %.
