Operating in a Second Country

TL;DR

A new country quietly breaks assumptions built into every kit and runbook: line lead times, languages, public holidays, payment methods, local vendors, laws, even keyboard layouts. Before opening, write a “second country” sheet with the people who know the place, listing each difference and what changes. Put holidays in the change calendar, speak the users' language at the desk, and make the sheet part of the runbook for the next country.

1The situation Larder, June 2027

Antwerp's keys arrive on 1 June, and Eli looks at the store IT kit and the city runbook with Belgium in mind. Both quietly assume the Netherlands: a Dutch provider with a six-week lead time, Dutch posters, the Dutch holiday calendar, iDEAL. In Belgium the line takes eight weeks, half the staff and customers speak French, the public holidays differ, Bancontact replaces iDEAL, and the store laptop's keyboard is AZERTY. The kit was written for one country without anyone noticing.

  • Hidden assumptions surface one at a time, after opening.
  • French-speaking pickers can't use Dutch-only guides.
  • A change could land on a Belgian holiday nobody marked.

2Real-life analogy driving the family car abroad

A family moving abroad takes the same car, but a lot changes: the emergency number, the rules for winter tyres, the plug for the charger, in some countries the side of the road. They make a list before they go, with a friend who lives there, not at the first roundabout.

3How it solves it one sheet of differences, written early with people who know

On Monday 5 July Eli writes the second-country sheet with Inès (Antwerp's store manager), Cho, Lena and Sam:

  • Line: a Belgian provider, ordered 2 June (8 weeks), live 2 August; 4G backup on a Belgian SIM.
  • Language: posters, guides, the status page and desk replies in Dutch and French; Sam's team adds French-speaking agents' hours.
  • Calendar: Belgian public holidays (21 July, 15 August, 1 and 11 November, and the rest) go into the change calendar and the on-call notes.
  • Payments: Bancontact through the payment provider, Cho, live in July.
  • Devices: an AZERTY keyboard on the store laptop; handhelds in French for pickers who choose it.
  • People: Inès joins the store leads' escalation list.

Antwerp opens on Monday 18 October, later than planned because of the permit, so the IT is ready with weeks to spare. Week one has 2.4 tickets per 100 orders. The sheet becomes the “country differences” section of the city runbook, ready for the next country.

  1. Re-read every kit and runbook for the new country

    Mark every assumption.

  2. Write the sheet with people who know the place

    The local manager, legal, payments.

  3. Check lead times locally

    Lines and vendors differ by country.

  4. Serve users in their language

    Posters, guides, status page, desk.

  5. Add local holidays to the change calendar

    And to on-call notes.

  6. Fold the sheet into the runbook

    For the next country.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Assuming it's the same

    Differences found one by one after opening. Fix → re-read every kit for the new country.

  • Writing it alone

    You don't know what you don't know. Fix → with the local manager, legal, payments.

  • One language

    Half the store can't use the guides. Fix → both languages, from day one.

  • Forgetting the calendar

    A change on a national holiday. Fix → holidays in the change calendar.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Opening in a new country or region.
  • Serving users in a new language.
  • Adding a local vendor.
  • Before writing kits you'll reuse abroad.

Skip it when…

  • A new city in the same country: the city runbook covers it.

8Self-check quiz

Which Dutch assumptions broke in Belgium?

Line lead time, language, holidays, payment method (iDEAL vs Bancontact), keyboard layout.

Who wrote the sheet with Eli?

Inès, Cho, Lena and Sam.

Why add Belgian holidays to the change calendar?

Traffic and staffing change on them; a change mustn't land on one by accident.

How did the language difference change the desk?

Replies, guides, posters and the status page in Dutch and French, with French-speaking agents' hours.

What happens to the sheet after Antwerp?

It becomes the “country differences” section of the city runbook.

what would you do?Larder considers a first city in Germany. What do you do first?

Re-read the kit, city runbook and Belgian sheet for German assumptions, then write a German sheet with someone who knows the place: line lead times, language, holidays, payments, local vendors, law.

try it

Take one of your runbooks or checklists and read it as if you were opening in another country. List every assumption that would change. Who would you need in the room to complete the list?

remember!!

A kit written in one country assumes that country. Write the differences down before opening, with people who know the place.
