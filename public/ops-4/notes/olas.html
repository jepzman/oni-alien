What Do Teams Promise Each Other?

TL;DR

An external promise (an SLA) is only as strong as the internal links behind it. Operational level agreements (OLAs) are the promises teams make to each other so the external one can be kept: the platform promises failover in under two minutes, payments promises to notice a silent billing vendor within fifteen. Keep them short, measurable, owned, and reviewed alongside the SLA. Vendor contracts that support them are the outer ring: underpinning contracts.

1The situation Larder, November 2027

With the Korenwolf SLA drafted, Eli draws the chain behind the 99.8 %. The portal and API belong to Ben's team. They depend on the database and its failover (Eli and Bram), billing (a vendor, owned by Cho), single sign-on (Eli), and stores picking business orders the evening before (Joost's operations). Larder made one promise outside, and inside five teams hold the links. None of them has promised the others anything.

  • A weak link is found when the SLA is missed, not before.
  • Teams don't know what others rely on them for.
  • Vendor contracts were signed without the SLA in mind.

2Real-life analogy the wedding caterer's timings

A wedding caterer promises the couple dinner at 19:00. Behind that: the kitchen promises plates at the pass by 18:50, the bakery the cake by 15:00, the drinks supplier delivery by 14:00. If any of those slip, the caterer knows early, because each one was a promise with a time, not a hope.

3How it solves it one page per link, measurable, reviewed with the SLA

In November Eli writes six OLAs, one page each, agreed with each team lead:

  • Platform → ordering: database failover under 2 minutes, tested monthly; backups restorable within 1 hour.
  • Ordering → business: no portal or API changes in the 07:00–09:00 window.
  • Payments → business (Cho): a silent billing vendor is detected within 15 minutes; invoices are regenerated within one working day.
  • Identity (Eli): SSO for office managers at 99.9 %.
  • Operations (Joost): business orders picked the evening before, by 22:00 (ADR 0017).
  • Support (Sam): business tickets answered within 30 minutes in office hours.

Each OLA has an internal measure that appears on the monthly service review. The vendors behind them (billing SaaS, PSP, cloud, WMS B) are the outer ring, the underpinning contracts. At the next renewal of each, the contract owner checks that the vendor's terms still support the OLA. The OLAs aren't legal documents. They're how one team learns what another relies on it for.

  1. Draw the chain behind the SLA

    Every team and vendor it depends on.

  2. Write one page per link

    What, measured how, owned by whom.

  3. Agree it with the team lead

    Not imposed; negotiated.

  4. Make it measurable

    A number on the monthly review.

  5. Check vendor contracts against it

    Underpinning contracts at renewal.

  6. Review OLAs with the SLA

    When the outside promise changes, so do the inside ones.

Vocabulary for the exam

OLA (operational level agreement)
An internal agreement between teams that supports an external SLA.
Underpinning contract
A contract with a vendor that supports an SLA or OLA.
Internal SLO
A target one service sets for the services that use it.
Dependency chain
The links an outside promise rests on.
Weakest link
The link whose failure most often breaks the promise.
Service owner
Accountable for a service and its promises (vol. 1).

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • OLAs as legal documents

    Teams argue clauses instead of fixing things. Fix → one page, negotiated, practical.

  • Not measurable

    “Best effort” promises. Fix → a number on the monthly review.

  • Forgetting vendors

    The weakest link is outside the company. Fix → underpinning contracts checked at renewal.

  • Writing them once

    The SLA changes; the OLAs don't. Fix → review together.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • You've signed an SLA.
  • Several teams hold the links behind a promise.
  • A miss came from a team or vendor nobody thought about.
  • Teams don't know what others depend on them for.

Skip it when…

  • One team owns everything behind a promise.

8Self-check quiz

What is an OLA?

An internal agreement between teams that supports an external SLA.

What is an underpinning contract?

A vendor contract that supports an SLA or OLA.

Give one of Larder's six OLAs.

E.g. platform → ordering: failover under 2 minutes, tested monthly; or payments: billing silence detected within 15 minutes.

How are OLAs kept honest?

Each has a measure on the monthly service review.

Why aren't OLAs legal documents?

Their job is to make dependencies visible and fixable, not to win arguments.

what would you do?Joost's team can't always pick business orders by 22:00 on Sundays, because of staffing. What do you do with the OLA?

Talk to Joost: change the OLA (e.g. by 06:00 on Mondays with a stock reservation) and check it still supports the SLA, rather than leave a promise nobody keeps.

try it

Take one promise you make to a customer. Draw every team and vendor it depends on. For the weakest link, write a one-page OLA: what, measured how, owned by whom.
