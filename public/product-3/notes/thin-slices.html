Thin Slices of Value

TL;DR

Building a feature layer by layer (all the backend, then all the screens) means nothing works until the end, and you learn nothing until then. Cut it into thin vertical slices instead: each one a little of every layer, working end to end for some real user, small enough to ship in a week or two. Order the slices so each one teaches you something before the next, and make the first one embarrassingly small.

  • Lives in: the release lines on the story map

1The situation Larder, 23 February 2026

Ben's first plan for approvals is sensible engineering: backend first (two weeks), then the customer app, then the picker screens, then refunds. It's eight weeks before a picker and a customer can try anything together, and the riskiest question (will pickers keep going while customers decide?) is answered last. Nothing usable until week eight, and the business case's clock is ticking at €30k a month.

  • Layered plans deliver nothing usable until the last layer lands.
  • The riskiest assumptions are tested last.
  • Partial layers can't be shown to users for feedback.

2Real-life analogy the layer cake

Cut a layer cake horizontally and one guest gets sponge, another cream, another jam: nobody tastes the cake. Cut a thin vertical slice and every guest gets a little of every layer, a real taste of the whole thing. If the recipe's wrong, you find out from the first slice, not after serving the whole cake.

3How it solves it each slice end to end, each one teaches

Grace and Ben cut four slices. Slice 1: express orders, one swap per order, Noord staff accounts only: ugly-but-working, live on 30 March, three weeks in. It taught the big lesson: pickers waiting for answers slowed packing, so “answer applied at packing” moved into slice 2. Slice 2 went to all Amsterdam express on 13 April; slices 3 and 4 follow with slots, Rotterdam and the web shop.

  1. Find the end-to-end path

    From the story map: the top card under every step.

  2. Cut by case, not by layer

    Fewer order types, fewer swaps, fewer users, one store, one city.

  3. Make slice 1 tiny

    Staff only, one case, rough edges: but working end to end.

  4. Put the riskiest question first

    The slice that answers it ships first.

  5. Size each slice to a week or two

    If it's longer, cut by another case.

  6. Learn, then re-slice

    Each release changes what the next slice should be.

Vocabulary for the exam

Vertical slice
A thin piece of a feature that works through every layer.
Horizontal layer
One technical part (backend, UI) built on its own.
Splitting by case
Fewer order types, users, stores or rules per slice.
Hamburger method
Gojko Adzic's way of picking one option per layer.
Risk-first
Ordering slices to answer the riskiest question soonest.
Increment
A slice released to some real users.

Not sliced by layer: no "backend sprint", no "UI sprint".

tools: the story map's release lines; feature flags to release slices to staff or a percentage; Gojko Adzic's “hamburger method” and Richard Lawrence's story-splitting patterns as guides.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Something works in weeks, not months.
  • Risk tested first: the picker flow in week three.
  • Real feedback from real users at every slice.
  • Value starts sooner: refunds fall from slice 2.
  • Easy to stop or turn after any slice.

What we lose

  • Some rework: early slices get rebuilt.
  • Engineers may feel it's messy at first.
  • Needs feature flags and good release habits.
  • Hard to slice some technical foundations.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Slicing by layer

    “Sprint 1: the backend.” Fix → every slice crosses every layer.

  • Slice 1 too big

    A complete feature for one city. Fix → staff only, one case, rough edges.

  • Risk last

    The easy slices first. Fix → the slice that answers the riskiest question goes first.

  • Never re-slicing

    The plan from February, unchanged in May. Fix → re-slice after every release.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any feature that takes more than two weeks.
  • When the riskiest assumption isn't tested yet.
  • When stakeholders want to see progress early.
  • Paired with feature flags.

Skip it when…

  • Truly indivisible changes (a legal deadline switch).
  • One-day tasks.

8Self-check quiz

What's a vertical slice?

A thin piece of a feature that works through every layer, end to end.

How do you make a slice thinner?

Cut by case: fewer order types, swaps, users, stores or cities.

Which slice goes first?

The one that answers the riskiest question.

What did slice 1 teach Larder?

Pickers waiting slowed packing, so answers moved to packing time in slice 2.

try it

Take the next feature on your roadmap. Write down its riskiest question, then describe a first slice that works end to end for five internal users and could ship in two weeks. Show it to an engineer and ask what else could be cut.

remember!!

A slice of the cake, not a layer. The first one, tiny.
