Story Mapping

TL;DR

A flat backlog hides the journey. A story map lays out the backbone (the steps a user takes, left to right), hangs the stories under each step (most important at the top), and draws release lines across: everything above the first line is the thinnest version of the whole journey. Build it together, with the team, on a wall; then the backlog is just the map read from the top.

  • Origin: Jeff Patton, User Story Mapping (2014)
  • Lives in: a wall or a board, then the backlog
  • Pairs with: thin slices, walking skeleton, user stories

1The situation Larder, 16 February 2026

The business case for approvals was approved on 12 February. By the 16th the backlog holds 41 tickets in a flat list, ordered by when someone wrote them: “countdown timer”, “picker swap screen”, “push notification copy”, “refund logic”… Ben asks the obvious question: which of these make the Noord staff test? Nobody can tell, because the list doesn't show the journey they belong to.

  • A flat list hides which tickets belong to which step of the journey.
  • Teams build one step fully before the next exists at all.
  • “What's in the first release?” becomes an argument instead of a line.

2Real-life analogy the road-trip map

Planning a road trip, you lay the stops along the top in order (pack, drive, ferry, campsite, beach), and under each, the things to sort out, most important first. Then you draw a line: for the first, short trip, you need just the top item under every stop. A trip with perfect packing and no ferry booking goes nowhere.

3How it solves it backbone across, stories down, lines across

Grace, Noor (the new product designer) and Ben built the map on a wall in 90 minutes. The backbone follows a swap from item runs out to customer receives, including the picker's steps. The 41 tickets found their places, nine new ones appeared (nobody had written “picker packs with the answer”), and the first release line answered Ben's question: the thinnest version of all six steps, for Noord's staff.

  1. Name the user and the goal

    Here: a customer whose item ran out, and the picker who handles it.

  2. Lay out the backbone

    The steps, left to right, in the order they happen. Verbs on cards.

  3. Hang stories under each step

    Everything you might build, most important at the top.

  4. Walk it together

    With engineering and design: what's missing? Nine cards appeared.

  5. Draw release lines

    Release 1 = the thinnest version of every step; then R2, R3.

  6. Read the backlog from the map

    Top to bottom, left to right; keep the map on the wall.

Vocabulary for the exam

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • The whole journey is visible, not just tickets.
  • Missing steps show up (nine new cards).
  • Release 1 is obvious: the thinnest version of every step.
  • Shared understanding, built together on a wall.
  • Easy to re-slice when plans change.

What we lose

  • Time: a 90-minute session, sometimes two.
  • Hard to keep in sync with the tracker.
  • Big maps get unwieldy.
  • Needs the team in the room to work.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Building it alone

    Grace's map, handed over. Fix → build it with engineering and design, on a wall.

  • Screens as the backbone

    The backbone lists screens, not steps. Fix → use the user's verbs: “customer decides”.

  • A fat first release

    Release 1 completes step one perfectly. Fix → release 1 is the thinnest version of every step.

  • Map then forget

    The wall is cleaned after a week. Fix → keep it up; re-read it at each release.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • New features with a multi-step journey.
  • When a backlog has grown into a flat list.
  • Planning the first release of something new.
  • Onboarding a team to a feature.

Skip it when…

  • Single-step changes and bug fixes.
  • Work with no user journey (pure infrastructure).

8Self-check quiz

What runs across the top of a story map?

The backbone: the user's steps, in order.

What goes above the first release line?

The thinnest version of every step of the journey.

Why build it together?

Engineers and designers spot missing steps and share the picture.

How does the backlog relate to the map?

It's the map read top to bottom, left to right.

try it

Take a feature with a long backlog. On a wall, write the user's steps as verbs across the top, place every ticket under its step, and draw one line: the thinnest version of every step. Do it with one engineer and one designer, in 90 minutes.

remember!!

Stops across, details down. The first trip covers every stop.
