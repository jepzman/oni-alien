Pre-mortem

TL;DR

When a launch looks good, people stop looking for problems. A pre-mortem flips the question: imagine it's after the launch and it failed. Everyone writes down, alone, for ten minutes, why it failed. Share, cluster, pick the most likely and most damaging reasons, and turn each into an action with an owner before the launch. It makes doubt safe to say out loud, and finds the risks optimism hides.

  • Origin: Gary Klein, “Performing a Project Premortem” (Harvard Business Review, 2007)
  • Lives in: a one-hour session, then actions in the launch checklist
  • Pairs with: assumption mapping, launch checklist, risk-storming

1The situation Larder, 16 March 2026

Approvals has everything going for it: the concierge test, the prototype test, an approved business case, slice 1 in two weeks. The team is optimistic, and that's the danger. Nobody has asked how the Amsterdam launch on 13 April could fail; doubts feel like disloyalty when everyone's excited.

  • Success so far makes people stop looking for problems.
  • Junior team members don't raise doubts in an excited room.
  • Risks are discussed generally (“adoption”) instead of specifically.

2Real-life analogy the mountain guide's briefing

The evening before a climb, a good mountain guide asks the group: “Imagine it's tomorrow afternoon and we turned back. Why?” The weather turned. Someone's boots failed. We started late. Each answer becomes a check for the morning: forecast at 05:00, a boot check, leave at 04:30. Nobody is being negative; they're planning.

3How it solves it imagine failure, write alone, act

Grace ran it with Ben, Noor, Dee, Sam and Tomás: ten minutes writing alone, then round the table. Sam's reason surprised everyone: 37 % of app users have notifications switched off, so they'd never see the question. Tomás worried about pickers waiting at the Friday peak; Noor about allergens in a substitute. Four actions, each with an owner, went into the launch checklist; all four shipped before 13 April.

  1. Set the scene

    “It's [date after launch]. It failed. Why?” Make failure certain, not hypothetical.

  2. Write alone, ten minutes

    Everyone, including the quietest; no discussion yet.

  3. Share round the table

    One reason each, round and round, until all are out.

  4. Cluster and rank

    Most likely × most damaging; pick the top four or five.

  5. Turn each into an action

    A change, a check or a fallback, with an owner and a date.

  6. Put actions in the plan

    In the launch checklist, reviewed at go/no-go.

Vocabulary for the exam

Pre-mortem
Imagining a future failure to find its causes now.
Post-mortem
Analysing a failure after it happened.
Prospective hindsight
Imagining an event has happened makes causes easier to name.
Fallback
What happens when the main path fails (a default answer).
Go / no-go
The decision meeting before launch.
Optimism bias
The tendency to underestimate what can go wrong.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Hidden risks found: notifications off for 37 %.
  • Doubt becomes safe to say.
  • Specific risks, not “adoption”.
  • Actions with owners before launch.
  • Quiet people contribute by writing alone first.

What we lose

  • An hour of the team's time.
  • Can feel negative if framed badly.
  • Long lists need ruthless ranking.
  • Only as good as who's in the room.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Discussing before writing

    The loudest voice sets the list. Fix → ten minutes alone, in silence, first.

  • “If it fails…”

    Hypothetical framing; people hedge. Fix → it failed: say why.

  • A list with no actions

    23 worries, no owners. Fix → top four or five become actions with owners and dates.

  • Only the core team

    No support, no operations. Fix → invite the people who'll feel the launch.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Before any launch the team is excited about.
  • Big bets with a business case behind them.
  • Launches that touch operations or support.
  • When nobody is raising concerns.

Skip it when…

  • Tiny, easily reversed changes.
  • Experiments designed to fail cheaply.
