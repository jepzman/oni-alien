User Stories that Work

TL;DR

A user story isn't a specification; it's a reminder of a conversation. Write the card with a real person, a real need and a reason (“as a tonight-dinner parent whose item ran out, I want to say yes or no within two minutes, so that I don't find out on the doormat”). Have the conversation with design and engineering. Write the confirmation as examples that say when it's done. Check it with INVEST: independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small, testable.

1The situation Larder, 18 February 2026

With the story map on the wall, Grace reads the approvals tickets. One says “As a user, I want approvals, so that I can approve.” One says “Implement swap endpoint.” One says “Countdown timer.” None says who it's for, what they're trying to do, or how anyone will know it works. The tickets start no conversation; engineers fill the gaps with guesses.

  • “As a user” describes nobody, so nobody's needs shape the work.
  • Technical tasks pose as stories, with no value of their own.
  • Without a confirmation, “done” is whatever the engineer decides.

2Real-life analogy the order ticket

A restaurant order ticket is short: “table 4, steak medium-rare, no garlic”. It works because the waiter talked to the guest (and heard about the nut allergy), and because the chef checks the plate against it at the pass. The ticket isn't the order; it's the reminder of a conversation, and a test for the result.

3How it solves it card, conversation, confirmation

Grace rewrote the stories with Noor and Ben, using the proto-personas from Vol. 1. “As a user” became “as a tonight-dinner parent whose item ran out”; technical tasks moved under the stories they serve; every story got a link to its example map as the confirmation. The approvals backlog went from 41 tickets to 17 stories, each something a picker or customer could notice.

  1. Name a real person

    A persona or role with a situation: “a tonight-dinner parent whose item ran out”.

  2. Say what they need

    In their terms, not the system's: “say yes or no”, not “POST /answer”.

  3. Say why

    The outcome for them: “don't find out on the doormat”.

  4. Have the conversation

    Design and engineering, with the map and the evidence.

  5. Write the confirmation

    Examples that say when it's done (from example mapping).

  6. Check INVEST

    Too big? Split it. Not valuable alone? Merge it into a story.

Vocabulary for the exam

User story
A short description of a need, from a user's point of view.
3 Cs
Card, conversation, confirmation.
INVEST
Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable.
Acceptance criteria
Conditions that confirm a story is done.
Persona
A described type of user, based on research.
Task
Technical work that serves a story; not a story itself.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Real people shape the work, not “a user”.
  • Conversations happen: the card invites them.
  • “Done” is clear: the confirmation says when.
  • Fewer, more valuable items in the backlog.
  • Tasks stay under stories, serving value.

What we lose

  • Writing well takes practice.
  • Template worship: perfect sentences, no conversation.
  • Not every item fits (pure technical work).
  • Conversations take time, which is the point.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • “As a user”

    Nobody in particular. Fix → a real persona and situation.

  • Stories as specs

    Three pages of requirements in the ticket. Fix → the card is a reminder; have the conversation.

  • Tasks as stories

    “Implement swap endpoint.” Fix → put tasks under the story they serve.

  • No confirmation

    Done when the engineer says so. Fix → examples that say when it's done.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Customer-facing features.
  • When tickets don't start conversations.
  • Teams new to working from a backlog.
  • Alongside story maps and example maps.

Skip it when…

  • Pure technical work: write it as a task or enabler, honestly.
  • Bugs: describe what happens and what should.

try it
