Example Mapping

TL;DR

Most misunderstandings about a story are about its rules, and they're found too late, in testing. Example mapping finds them first, in 25 minutes, with the “three amigos” (product, engineering, testing or design). Put the story on a yellow card, each rule on a blue card, concrete examples on green cards under their rule, and every question nobody can answer on a red card. Lots of reds: not ready. Too many blues: split the story.

1The situation Larder, 25 February 2026

Halfway through slice 1, Dee tests a swap with no answer. What should happen at 2:00? Ben's code keeps the substitute. Sam assumed a refund. Grace assumed it depends on a customer setting. Three people, three rules, and nobody found out until testing. Grace realises the other stories probably hide the same kind of gap.

  • Rules live in people's heads, each slightly different.
  • Edge cases surface in testing, when changing them is expensive.
  • Long specs don't help: nobody reads them closely enough to disagree.

2Real-life analogy referee training

Referees train with video clips. The rule is on the screen; then clips that show it applied (offside, not offside); and the clips nobody in the room agrees on are written down as questions for the rules committee. By the time of the match, the hard cases have been talked through.

3How it solves it story, rules, examples, questions

Grace ran 25-minute sessions for every slice-2 story, with Ben, Noor and Dee. The decide story yielded four rules (two-minute window; no answer keeps the swap unless the customer's setting says refund; more than €2 extra must be answered; allergens never default) and two questions. One, “what if the app is closed?”, went to the pre-mortem and became the in-app banner. Stories with more than three reds waited.

  1. Pick one story

    Yellow card on the table.

  2. Write the rules you know

    One blue card each: the acceptance criteria, as rules.

  3. Add examples under each rule

    Green cards, concrete: “no answer, setting “refund me” → refund”.

  4. Write every open question

    Red cards; don't debate them now.

  5. Stop at 25 minutes

    Many reds → not ready. Many blues → split the story.

  6. Keep the result

    A photo or table linked from the story; examples become tests.

Vocabulary for the exam

Three amigos
Product, engineering and testing (or design) looking at a story together.
Rule
An acceptance criterion: a constraint on behaviour.
Example
A concrete case that illustrates a rule.
Question
Something nobody in the room can answer yet.
BDD
Behaviour-driven development: examples become automated tests.
Gherkin
Given-when-then format for examples as tests.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Rules agreed before building, not in testing.
  • Edge cases found in 25 minutes.
  • Examples become tests.
  • Readiness is visible: count the red cards.
  • Too-big stories show up: count the blue cards.

What we lose

  • 25 minutes per story for three or four people.
  • Needs discipline not to debate questions in the session.
  • Photos get lost unless linked.
  • Not for trivial stories.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Debating the reds

    The session becomes a two-hour argument. Fix → write the question, find who can answer, move on.

  • Only product in the room

    Grace writes the rules alone. Fix → product, engineering and testing or design together.

  • Abstract examples

    “Handles timeouts correctly.” Fix → concrete: “no answer, setting “refund me” → refund”.

  • Ignoring the signal

    Seven reds, built anyway. Fix → many reds means not ready.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Stories with business rules or edge cases.
  • Before a story enters a sprint.
  • When testing keeps finding “what should happen here?”.
  • Teams adopting behaviour-driven development.

Skip it when…

  • Trivial stories (a copy change).
  • Exploratory spikes.

8Self-check quiz

What do the four colours mean?

Yellow story, blue rules, green examples, red questions.

What do many red cards tell you?

The story isn't ready to build.

What do many blue cards tell you?

The story is too big; split it.

Who should be in the session?

The three amigos: product, engineering, and testing or design.

try it

Pick the next story your team will start. Get an engineer and a tester or designer for 25 minutes with four colours of sticky notes. Map rules, examples and questions. Count the reds before deciding whether it's ready.

remember!!

The rule, the clips, and the disputed ones as questions. Twenty-five minutes.
