chapter 1 · 2 min

Three notes in sixty seconds

Gist Change data capture copies every committed change out of the app database, upserts make loading safe to repeat, and a star schema shapes the result for reports. This deep dive follows one order through the pipeline that joins them, and through everything that later happens to it.

A homeware shop sells on the web and in 30 shops. Finance wants revenue by category by day, without slowing down checkout. Three notes from the data volume, put together, give the answer: copy the changes out of the app database, load them so a rerun never double-counts, and shape them as a star for reporting.

NoteIn one line
Change Data Captureread the database's own change log and stream every committed insert, update and delete
Upsertsinsert the row, or update it if the key already exists, in one atomic statement
Star Schemaa fact table of numbers at a stated grain, with flat dimension tables around it
The CDC note's going-deeper chapter covers the snapshot, ordering and the slot that fills the disk. This dive starts where it ends: at the warehouse door.

The notes describe each piece on a good day. In production the pipeline runs every 15 minutes for years, and it is rerun, backfilled, paused for a weekend and pointed at a source table that someone renamed. The questions this dive answers are the ones finance asks: why did March revenue change after we closed March, why is yesterday’s number missing three orders, and why does the warehouse say €55 when the app says €47?

The order we will follow

WhenWhat happens to order 9001
31 Mar 23:58customer 77 signs up and orders a kettle (€39) and two mugs (€8 each): €55, web store
1 Apr 00:00payment confirms 40 seconds later; the transaction commits after midnight
3 Aprone mug is returned: the line changes from 2 × €8 to 1 × €8, the order is now €47
12 Maythe kettle moves from category Kitchen to Small appliances

Each event on that list breaks a naive pipeline in a different way. By the end of the dive the same order will be counted once, in the right day, with the right amount, under the category it had when it was sold.

The real mechanism: raw, staging, marts

Gist Raw keeps every change event, untouched. Staging keeps the latest row per key, ordered by log position. Marts hold facts at a stated grain and versioned dimensions. Because raw is kept, every later layer can be thrown away and rebuilt, which is what makes fixes and backfills safe.

The CDC note ends with a stream of change events. The star schema note starts with a clean fact table. Between them sit two layers that the notes skip, and most of the hard work happens there.

What each layer promises

LayerHoldsPromiseRebuilt from
rawevery change event exactly as received, plus load metadatanever edited, never deleted (until retention)the source, by a new snapshot
stagingone row per source key: the latest version, typed and renamed, deletes flaggedmatches the source as of the last log position readraw, at any time
martsfacts at a stated grain, dimensions with surrogate keysbusiness meaning: one definition of revenuestaging, at any time
reportqueries, extracts and dashboardsreads marts onlynothing to rebuild
“Medallion” (bronze, silver, gold) is the same three layers under other names.

The key line is the last column. Because raw is kept, every later layer can be thrown away and rebuilt. That is what makes it safe to fix a bug in the revenue definition: change the SQL, rebuild the marts, and last year is corrected too. Without raw, the only copy of last year is the wrong one.

ETL or ELT

In classic ETL a separate tool transformed the data before it reached the warehouse, and only the result was stored. In ELT the raw data is loaded first and transformed inside the warehouse with SQL, usually by dbt. ELT won for one reason: warehouses got cheap and fast enough to keep raw data and rerun the transforms. This pipeline is ELT. The extract step does no business logic at all; it copies.

Staging: from events to rows

Staging turns a pile of change events into the table as it is now. For each key, keep the event with the highest log position, and flag the key if that event is a delete, so the next layer can remove it too:

stg_order_lines.sqllatest version per key
SELECT *, op = 'd' AS is_deleted
FROM (
  SELECT coalesce(after, before).*, op, lsn,
         ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY coalesce(after, before).id ORDER BY lsn DESC) AS rn
  FROM raw.order_lines_changes
) latest
WHERE rn = 1;

Order by log position, never by the time the event arrived. Events can arrive out of order after a connector restart or a retry; the log position is the order the database committed them in.

Marts: from rows to facts

The fact table’s grain is one order line, as the star schema note recommends. Each fact row looks up its dimension keys: the date from placed_at, the product version, the customer and the store. Two details matter more than they look:

  • The date is the shop’s local date. Order 9001 was placed at 23:58 in Amsterdam, which is 21:58 UTC. An order at 00:30 on 1 April Amsterdam time is 22:30 UTC on 31 March. Finance closes months in local time, so the date key is computed in local time, once, in the pipeline, not in every report.
  • Amounts are stored, not recomputed. The line total comes from the source as it was charged. Recomputing qty × current price in the warehouse gives a different answer the day prices change.

checkpoint

Why order staging by log position rather than by arrival time?

Events can arrive out of order after retries or restarts; the log position is the order the database committed them in.

Why compute the date key in local time in the pipeline?

Finance closes days and months in local time. Doing it once in the pipeline means every report agrees.

Failure drills

Gist Six ways to get a wrong number: a late commit hidden behind a timestamp watermark, a rerun that doubles a day, a renamed column that loads NULLs, a hard delete the extract cannot see, a dimension overwrite that changes last year, and a fact dropped because its customer had not arrived yet.

Each drill is a real way to get a wrong number. Guess the cause before you read it.
  • 1 · after midnight
  • 2 · counted twice
  • 3 · renamed column
  • 4 · deleted, still sold
  • 5 · last year changed
  • 6 · early fact
Drill 1

The order that committed after midnight

Timeline
The extract reads WHERE updated_at > :watermark and saves the largest updated_at it saw. Order 9001 is written at 23:58:10 but its payment step keeps the transaction open, and it commits at 00:00:40. The 00:00:30 run could not see it and saved a watermark of 23:59:58. The next run asks for rows after 23:59:58. Order 9001 is never loaded.
Tempting mistake
“Use the database clock at commit time.” There is no column for that, and now() inside a transaction returns its start time, which is exactly the problem.
Why
A timestamp is set when the row is written; the row becomes visible when the transaction commits. Any transaction that is open while the extract runs can commit later with an older timestamp. Long transactions, replicas that lag and clocks on several app servers all widen the gap.
What saves you
Either read the log (CDC), which is ordered by commit, or re-read a safety window: updated_at > :watermark − 10 minutes, and make the load idempotent (drill 2) so the overlap does no harm. Set the window longer than the longest transaction you allow, and enforce that with idle_in_transaction_session_timeout.

Rule: a timestamp watermark misses late commits. Overlap the window and make the load idempotent, or read the log.

Drill 2

March counted twice

Timeline
On 1 April the load for 31 March fails halfway. The on-call engineer reruns it. The load appends rows. Half of 31 March is now in the fact table twice, and the dashboard shows the best day of the year.
Why
Appending is not idempotent: running it twice gives a different result from running it once. Every pipeline is rerun eventually: after a failure, a fix, a backfill or a retry by the scheduler.
What saves you
Make every load safe to repeat. Either replace the whole slice (DELETE the day, then INSERT it, in one transaction, or overwrite the partition), or MERGE on the line’s key so a second run updates the same rows. Add a uniqueness test on the fact key that fails the run (chapter 7).

Rule: every load must give the same result when run twice. Replace a slice or merge on a key; never blindly append.

Drill 3

The column that was renamed on a Tuesday

Timeline
The app team renames line_total to amount_gross in a normal release. CDC carries the new field. Staging still selects line_total, which is now always NULL. Revenue is €0 for every new line. Nobody notices until the weekly finance meeting.
Why
The pipeline depends on the app’s table layout, but nobody told the app team it was a contract. The warehouse accepted NULLs without complaint because nothing said they were wrong.
What saves you
Fail loudly: a not_null test on the amount and a volume check (“revenue today is 0 % of the usual”) stop the run before the marts publish. Then fix the cause: a schema check in the app’s CI that knows which columns the warehouse reads, or, better, a published contract or outbox event instead of raw tables (the CDC note’s going-deeper chapter).

Rule: silent NULLs are worse than a failed run. Test what the report depends on, and make the source schema a contract.

Drill 4

The cancelled order still in revenue

Timeline
Fraud cancels order 9733, and a clean-up job hard-deletes its lines. The timestamp extract looks for changed rows; a deleted row has no row left to change. The warehouse keeps the €310 sale forever.
Why
Incremental extracts by timestamp only see rows that exist. Deletes leave nothing behind to select.
What saves you
CDC sees deletes as events (op = d), and staging drops the key. Without CDC: ask the app team for soft deletes on tables the warehouse reads, or run a periodic key comparison (all ids in the source against all ids in staging) and delete the missing ones. A nightly reconciliation of order counts and totals per day (chapter 8) catches what both miss.

Rule: an extract that cannot see deletes will slowly overstate. Read deletes from the log, or reconcile keys.

Drill 5

Last year’s revenue changed overnight

Timeline
On 12 May the kettle moves from Kitchen to Small appliances. The product dimension is a copy of the current product table, so it overwrites the category. The March report, closed and filed, now shows €39 less for Kitchen and €39 more for Small appliances.
Why
Overwriting a dimension (type 1) rewrites history: every old fact now joins to today’s label. Sometimes that is what you want (a typo fix). For a category used in filed reports it is not.
What saves you
Keep history in the dimension (type 2): close the old row and add a new one with a new surrogate key and valid dates. Facts keep the key they were loaded with, so March still joins to Kitchen. Decide per column: typos are type 1, categories and regions are type 2. If someone wants old sales under the new category, that is a different question with its own join (“current category”), not a silent change.

Rule: a dimension that overwrites changes the past. Version the columns that reports group by.

Drill 6

The fact that arrived before its customer

Timeline
Customer 77 signed up one minute before ordering. Orders come from the app by CDC every 15 minutes; customers come from the CRM in a nightly batch. The 00:15 fact load joins order 9001 to dim_customer, finds no row for customer 77, and the inner join drops both lines. €55 is missing until someone reloads the day by hand.
Why
Facts and dimensions come from different sources on different schedules. An inner join treats “not loaded yet” as “does not exist”.
What saves you
Never drop a fact for a missing dimension. Either point it at an unknown member (key −1, “not yet known”) and re-key it when the dimension arrives, or insert an inferred member: a placeholder row for customer 77 that the nightly load fills in. Monitor the share of facts pointing at unknown members; above a few percent, a source is late.

Rule: an inner join between sources on different schedules loses data. Facts always load; missing dimensions get a placeholder.

What the six drills add up to

HabitDrill

checkpoint

A timestamp extract misses a few orders near midnight. Likely cause and fix?

Transactions that commit after the run with an older updated_at. Overlap the window and make the load idempotent, or switch to CDC.

Why point a fact at an unknown member instead of waiting for the dimension?

An inner join drops the fact silently. A placeholder keeps the revenue and can be re-keyed when the dimension arrives.

chapter 4 · 3 min

The hard problem: rerun anything

Gist A correct pipeline gives the right answer when any part is run again for any period. Use three clocks for three jobs: event time decides the day, commit order decides the version, load time decides nothing. Load with newer-only merges on the source key, and keep hidden clocks out of the transforms.

Five of the six drills come down to one property. A correct pipeline is one where running any part of it again, for any period, gives the right answer. Reruns after failures, backfills after bug fixes, replays after a source outage and duplicate events from a connector restart are then all the same, boring operation. Everything in this chapter serves that one promise.

Three clocks

ClockOrder 9001Used for
event time: when it happened in the business31 Mar 23:58 (placed_at)which day the sale belongs to
commit order: when the database made it visiblelog position 1000, then 5200 for the returnwhich version of a row is the latest
load time: when the warehouse got it1 Apr 00:15, then 3 Apr 10:30freshness, debugging, nothing else
Mixing up event time and load time is the single most common warehouse bug. It looks fine until the first late event.

Every decision uses the right clock. The fact row’s date comes from event time. Which version wins comes from commit order. Load time is recorded but never decides anything. The return on 3 April changes a fact for 31 March: an update to a day that is already loaded. A pipeline that only processes “today’s” rows by load time will put the €8 return on 3 April’s report or not at all.

Four ways to load, compared

Load methodIdempotent?Handles late updatesCostUse for
appendnonocheapestraw layer only, where duplicates are expected and deduped later
replace a slice (delete + insert a day, or overwrite a partition)yes, per sliceif you reload the affected slicesrewrites whole slicesfacts by event date, when you know which days changed
MERGE on key, newer onlyyesyes, any dayreads the target to match keysfacts and staging fed by CDC
full rebuildyesyesrereads everythingsmall tables, dimensions, and the fix after a logic change

The pipeline uses three of them: append into raw, MERGE from raw into staging and facts, and full rebuilds for small dimensions and after logic changes.

The merge, written properly

fact_sales_merge.sqlidempotent and order-safe
MERGE INTO mart.fact_sales AS t
USING (
  SELECT * FROM stg.order_lines_enriched
  WHERE lsn > :last_merged_lsn          -- only what changed since the last run
) AS s
ON t.order_line_id = s.order_line_id
WHEN MATCHED AND s.lsn > t.source_lsn AND s.is_deleted THEN DELETE
WHEN MATCHED AND s.lsn > t.source_lsn THEN   -- newer only: replays and old events do nothing
  UPDATE SET qty = s.qty, amount = s.amount, source_lsn = s.lsn,
             product_key = s.product_key, customer_key = s.customer_key
WHEN NOT MATCHED AND NOT s.is_deleted THEN
  INSERT (order_line_id, date_key, product_key, customer_key, store_key, qty, amount, source_lsn)
  VALUES (s.order_line_id, s.date_key, s.product_key, s.customer_key, s.store_key, s.qty, s.amount, s.lsn);

Three details make it safe. The key is the source’s line id, so a second run matches the same rows. The guard s.lsn > t.source_lsn means an older event arriving late, or the same event twice, changes nothing: it is the upsert note’s “skip if older” clause. And :last_merged_lsn is only advanced after the merge commits, so a crash halfway means the next run repeats the batch, which the first two details make harmless.

No hidden clocks in the transforms

A transform that calls now() or current_date gives a different answer on every run, so it cannot be rerun for last Tuesday. Pass the period being processed in as a parameter (the scheduler’s logical date) and use only that. Then a backfill is simply the normal job, run for old dates:

backfill.shthe same job, for a past range
# rebuild March after fixing the revenue definition
dbt build --select fact_sales+ --vars '{"from": "2026-03-01", "to": "2026-04-01"}'

If the backfill needs a different script from the daily run, the daily run is not idempotent yet.

checkpoint

The same change event is merged twice. What stops it doing harm?

The merge matches on the source key and only updates when the event’s log position is newer than the stored one.

Why is now() in a transform a problem?

The transform gives a different answer each run, so it cannot be rerun or backfilled for a past period.

Full copy, timestamp or CDC

Gist A nightly full copy is simple and correct but slow and heavy. Incremental by timestamp is light but misses late commits and deletes. CDC into raw with frequent merges is fresh and sees everything, at the cost of a connector and a slot to run. Choose per table; keep the layer contract the same.

There are three common ways to get order lines out of the app database. Here is each one, with the drills from chapter 3 run against it.

Variant A is underrated. For tables under a few million rows it is correct by construction, and easy to explain.

Variant B is the one most teams start with and the one with the most silent failures: a bulk UPDATE from a script that does not set updated_at, a hard delete, a long transaction. Every one of them produces a number that looks plausible. Variant C costs more to run, but its failures are loud: the connector stops, lag grows, an alert fires.

Choosing per table

TableRowsChangesPick
stores30a few a yearA: full copy, type 2 dimension built in the warehouse
products40,000hundreds a dayA nightly, or C if prices must be fresh
customers (from the CRM)2 millionthousands a day, no log accessB with an overlap window, plus a weekly key reconciliation
orders, order_lines60 million lines100,000 a day, updates and deletesC

Mixing variants is normal and is not a compromise. What must be consistent is the layer contract from chapter 2: whichever way data arrives, staging holds the latest row per key and marts are rebuilt from staging. The facts and dimensions do not care how the rows got there.

Where the transform runs

All three variants load into raw and transform in the warehouse (ELT). The older alternative, a tool that transforms in flight and loads only the result, still makes sense in one case: data that must be masked or dropped before it may be stored at all, such as card numbers or health data. Then the extract step removes those fields and nothing else, so it stays a copy with redactions, not a second place where business logic lives.

checkpoint

Which variant is blind to hard deletes?

Incremental by timestamp: a deleted row leaves nothing to select.

When is a nightly full copy the right answer?

For small tables that change rarely, such as stores or products: it is correct by construction.

chapter 6 · 3 min

The pipeline in numbers

Gist 100,000 new lines and 8,000 updates a day: 25 GB for a full copy, tens of megabytes incrementally. CDC with a 15-minute merge gives a report about 10 minutes old on average. A merge without a partition filter scans the whole fact table each run. A year’s backfill takes a day serially, three hours in parallel.

The shop takes about 40,000 orders a day at 2.5 lines each: 100,000 new lines a day, plus about 8,000 updates from returns and status changes. Five years of history is 60 million lines.

How much moves

VariantData moved per daySource workWorked out
A · full copy of order linesabout 25 GBa 60-million-row scan nightly60 M rows × ~400 bytes
B · incrementalabout 45 MBone index range scan per run108,000 rows × ~400 bytes
C · CDCabout 90 MBreading the log, which is written anyway108,000 events × ~800 bytes (before and after)
CDC moves more bytes than B, because each event carries before and after. It still does far less work on the source.

How fresh

With CDC the connector delivers events within seconds. The merge runs every 15 minutes and takes about 3. The worst case for a sale to appear in the report is one full interval plus the run: about 18 minutes, and about 10 on average. Halving the interval halves the average but doubles the number of merges, each of which reads part of the fact table.

What a merge costs

A MERGE has to find the matching target rows. If the fact table is partitioned by event date, and the batch only touches recent days, the warehouse reads only those partitions. Returns make that harder: they update facts weeks old.

Batch touchesPartitions readData scanned per merge
only today1~40 MB
the last 30 days (returns)30~1.2 GB
no date filter in the mergeall 1,800~70 GB, 96 times a day

The last row is a real bill: a merge without a partition filter can cost more than the rest of the warehouse together. Add the affected dates to the merge condition (AND t.date_key IN (dates in this batch)) so the warehouse can prune.

The slot on the source

The source writes about 20 GB of write-ahead log a day. If the connector stops, PostgreSQL keeps all of it for the slot: a weekend is 60 GB, the disk from the CDC note’s drill. Cap it with max_slot_wal_keep_size (say 100 GB) and alert when lag passes 30 minutes. If the cap is hit, the slot is lost and the connector needs a new snapshot, which is slow but recoverable, because raw and the merge are idempotent.

A backfill, timed

After a fix to the revenue definition, a year of facts must be rebuilt. One day of facts rebuilds in about 4 minutes. Serially, 365 days take about 24 hours; eight days in parallel take about 3. Plan backfills against the warehouse’s concurrency limit and run them outside the merge schedule, or the daily merges queue behind them and freshness drops for the day.

checkpoint

Why does CDC move more bytes than a timestamp extract?

Each change event carries the row before and after the change.

How do you stop a merge from scanning the whole fact table?

Add the event dates touched by the batch to the merge condition so the warehouse reads only those partitions.

Testing data, not just code

Gist Unit test the transforms with the drills as fixtures, test that a rerun changes nothing, check keys, volumes and ranges on every load, and reconcile counts and sums per day against the source every night. Build into a hidden schema and publish only when the tests pass.

Pipeline bugs rarely crash. They produce a number that is wrong by a few percent, and the first person to notice is in finance. Tests therefore check data as well as code, and they run on every load, not only in CI.

TestChecksRunsCatches
unit tests on transformsfixed input rows give the expected output rowsCIlogic bugs: the return, the time zone, the unknown customer

If you can only afford one test, make it the reconciliation. It checks the outcome, so it catches causes you never thought of.

A unit test for order 9001

Write the drills as fixtures. The return on 3 April is a perfect case: it must change the fact for 31 March, not add one for 3 April.

test_fact_sales.ymldbt unit test
unit_tests:
  - name: return_updates_the_original_day
    model: fact_sales
    given:
      - input: ref('stg_order_lines_enriched')
        rows:
          - {order_line_id: 51, date_key: 20260331, qty: 2, amount: 16, lsn: 1000}
          - {order_line_id: 51, date_key: 20260331, qty: 1, amount: 8,  lsn: 5200}
    expect:
      rows:
        - {order_line_id: 51, date_key: 20260331, qty: 1, amount: 8}

Reconciliation, the test that checks the outcome

Every night, compute the same small summary on both sides and compare: per event date, the number of order lines and the sum of their amounts. Run it on the source’s read replica, for the last 60 days, so late returns are included.

reconcile.sqlsame query on both sides, then compare
SELECT date(placed_at AT TIME ZONE 'Europe/Amsterdam') AS day,
       count(*)              AS lines,
       sum(line_total)       AS revenue
FROM order_lines JOIN orders USING (order_id)
WHERE placed_at >= now() - interval '60 days'
GROUP BY 1;

Expect small, explainable differences for the last hour (in flight) and none for older days. A difference on an old day is a bug every time: a missed late commit, a delete that never arrived, a duplicated reload.

Write, audit, publish

Data tests are only useful if a failing one stops bad data reaching the report. Build each run’s marts in a hidden schema, run the tests there, and swap them in only when they pass. If they fail, finance sees yesterday’s correct numbers with a “data as of” note, rather than today’s wrong ones.

checkpoint

Which single test catches the most causes, and why?

The nightly reconciliation of counts and sums per day: it checks the outcome, whatever the cause.

What does write–audit–publish protect finance from?

Seeing a wrong number: a failed test keeps yesterday’s correct marts in place.

Running it

Gist Watch freshness measured on the data, the reconciliation difference per day and the share of facts with unknown members. Keep a runbook for restarting the connector, replaying a day from raw, re-snapshotting and backfilling. Agree with finance how a closed month handles late changes.

A pipeline that runs every 15 minutes needs three numbers on a dashboard, each with an alert, and a short runbook for the five things that go wrong.

MetricWhat it meansAlert when
freshness: now minus the newest event time in the martshow old the report isover 45 minutes in opening hours
completeness: the reconciliation difference per daywhether the numbers are rightany day older than one hour differs
unknown-member share: facts with a −1 keywhether a source is lateover 2 % of yesterday’s facts
Freshness is measured on the data, not on the job. A job that succeeds while loading nothing is the classic false green.

Behind those three, the engineering view: connector lag and slot size on the source, run duration and rows per run, and tests failed per run. Show “data as of 10:42” on the finance dashboard itself, from the freshness metric, so users can see when it is stale without asking.

Closing a month

Finance closes March on 5 April, and late events keep coming: a return in May still updates a March fact. Agree a rule with finance and write it down. The usual one: the warehouse is always current, and the filed March report is a snapshot taken at close (a copy of the month’s summary, stored with the date it was taken). Later changes appear as adjustments in the month they were made, not as edits to a filed month. That is a finance decision, not a technical one, but the pipeline has to support both views.

checkpoint

Why measure freshness on the data rather than the job?

A job can succeed while loading nothing; only the newest event time shows how old the report really is.

A return in May changes a March sale. What does the filed March report show?

Usually the snapshot taken at close; the change appears as an adjustment in May. Agree the rule with finance.

Tools

Gist Debezium or a managed connector reads the log, Kafka or storage lands raw data, dbt builds staging and marts with incremental merges, snapshots and tests, a scheduler handles dependencies and backfills, and a columnar warehouse stores it. The grain, the keys and idempotency stay your job.

Every piece of this pipeline exists as a product. Each does one box or arrow well and leaves the same few decisions to you.

chapter 10 · 3 min

A lab and the checklist

Gist The lab adds gift cards: card sales fit the existing fact table, but outstanding balances need a periodic snapshot fact rebuilt from raw, with late till uploads handled by event time. The checklist turns the deep dive into review questions for any pipeline.

The lab. The shop starts selling gift cards. A card is bought (an order line, as now), then spent across several later orders, and may expire unspent after two years. Card balances live in a new gift_cards table that the app updates on every purchase and spend. Finance wants two reports: gift card sales by day, and the total outstanding balance at the end of each day. Design the pipeline before you open the answers.

  1. What is the grain of each report, and is it a new fact table?
  2. Which extract variant for gift_cards, and why?
  3. How do you get the balance at the end of 31 March, if you load on 2 April?
  4. A card is spent in a shop whose till uploads its sales a day late. What breaks, and what saves you?
  5. How do you prove the balance report is right?

model answer

1 · The grain

Card sales are order lines and already land in fact_sales with a product of type gift card. The balance is different: one row per card per day, a periodic snapshot fact (fact_gift_card_daily). Do not try to read balances out of transaction facts at query time; store the end-of-day state.

2 · Extract

CDC (variant C). Balances change on every spend, and you need every version to rebuild a past day’s balance. A nightly copy would only give the balance at copy time.

3 · Balance at the end of 31 March

From raw: for each card, the latest change event whose event time is on or before 31 March 23:59:59 local time. Because raw keeps every version, the snapshot for any day can be built, or rebuilt, at any time, which also makes the job idempotent.

4 · The late till

The spend has an event time of yesterday but arrives today, so yesterday’s balance snapshot was wrong when built. Rebuild snapshots for the days touched by each batch (the event dates in the batch), not just today. Same rule as chapter 4: event time decides the day.

5 · Proof

Reconcile every night: the sum of balances in the app for “now” equals the latest snapshot plus today’s changes, and for each past day, sales minus spends minus expiries equals the change in total balance. A difference names the day to replay.

The review checklist

Layers

  • Raw keeps every change, append only, with a stated retention
  • Staging holds the latest row per key, ordered by log position
  • Marts rebuild from staging; reports read marts only
  • No business logic in the extract

Extract

  • Each table has a chosen variant and a reason
  • Timestamp extracts overlap their window
  • Deletes are read from the log or found by reconciling keys
  • The slot has a size cap and a lag alert

Loading

  • Every load is idempotent: replace a slice or merge on a key
  • Merges are newer-only by log position
  • Merges filter on the partitions they touch
  • No now() in transforms; the period is a parameter

Modelling

  • The grain of each fact is written down
  • Date keys use the business’s time zone
  • Columns reports group by are type 2
  • Missing dimensions get unknown or inferred members

Trust

  • Keys tested unique and not null on every load
  • Volume checks stop a bad run before publishing
  • Nightly reconciliation of counts and sums per day
  • The source schema is checked in the app’s CI

Operations

  • Freshness measured on data and shown on the dashboard
  • Runbook for replay, backfill and re-snapshot
  • Month-close rule agreed with finance
