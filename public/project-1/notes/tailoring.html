How Much Process Do We Need?

TL;DR

Tailoring means fitting the way of working to the project: choosing which practices, documents and meetings to use, and how big to make each. The guide's forty processes are a menu, not a checklist. Too little process and things are forgotten (Zuid). Too much and the team serves the paperwork. Decide on purpose, write down what you left out and why, and review the fit once the project is moving.

1The situation Larder, 18 February 2026

Sanne's old company had forty templates and a 60-page plan for every project, large or small. Larder has a chat channel and Joost's spreadsheet. For five weeks she has been improvising between the two. The team has started asking: is there a risk list? do we need to fill in something for changes? which meeting is the real one? Nobody decided how Rotterdam would be run. It just accumulated.

  • Heavy process on a fifteen-week launch eats the fifteen weeks.
  • No process is how Zuid opened with 340 unpriced products.
  • Process that grows by accident fits nobody.

2Real-life analogy the tailor

A suit off the rack fits nobody well. A suit from nothing takes months. A tailor starts from a standard pattern, measures the person, takes it in here, lets it out there, and then asks them to walk around in it and adjusts again. The standard does most of the work. The fit makes it wearable.

3How it solves it keep, shrink or leave out, on purpose

Sanne lists the usual practices and decides each one with the leads: keep, shrink, or leave out. A charter: yes, one page. A plan: one page and a milestone chart. A risk register: the top fifteen, looked at weekly. Status: one page, seven lines, every Friday. Change control: the three triggers, no form. Meetings: one, thirty minutes, Thursdays at 09:30. Left out, with reasons written down: a communications plan, a procurement plan, earned value. A date goes in the diary to review the fit: 20 March.

  1. List the practices you might use

    From the standard, and from your organisation.

  2. Measure the project

    Size, risk, novelty, parties, rules.

  3. For each practice: keep, shrink or leave out

    With the team.

  4. Write down what you left out, and why

    So it's a decision, not a gap.

  5. Check with the sponsor and any required rules

    Some things aren't optional.

  6. Review the fit after a month

    What was missing? What was waste?

Vocabulary for the exam

Tailoring
Deliberately adapting the approach, governance and processes to suit the project.
Artefact
A document or other output of project management: a plan, a register, a report.
Process
A set of activities that turns inputs into outputs.
Ceremony
A regular meeting with a fixed purpose.
Just enough
The least process that still controls the risk.
Retrospective
A look back at how the work is going, to improve the way of working.

＋Going deeper how to tailor, step by step

“Use judgment” is true and not much help. This chapter gives the judgment something to hold: the four steps of tailoring, five dials that tell you how heavy to go, the things you may not tailor away, and what a review of the fit looks like.

1The four steps

  1. Choose the approach.

    Predictive, adaptive or hybrid, per workstream (the note before last). This sets the shape of everything else.

  2. Fit to the organisation.

    What does your organisation require (finance approvals, security review, a PMO's templates)? What is it used to? Start from there; don't fight habits you don't need to.

  3. Fit to the project.

    Now size each practice for this project: its length, risk, people and rules. This is the tailoring table.

  4. Keep improving.

    Review the fit while the project runs. Add what was missed, drop what nobody used.

2Five dials that set the weight

Five things decide how much process a project needs. Mark your project on each:

  • Size and length. Fifteen weeks needs less than three years.
  • What a mistake costs. Food safety and a signed lease cost more than a slow web page.
  • How new it is. The tenth store needs less than the first city.
  • How many parties. Each extra team or vendor adds joins to manage.
  • How strict the rules are. Contracts, regulators and auditors require evidence.

The dials don't move together, and that is the useful part. Rotterdam is light on size but heavier on the cost of a mistake. So Sanne keeps documents short and spends her process on the places where a mistake is expensive: the quality checks, the long-lead orders, the go / no-go.

3What you may not tailor away

Tailoring changes how, never whether, for a few things:

  • The principles. You can drop a template. You can't drop telling the truth about status, or thinking about value.
  • The law and contracts. Food-safety registration, the lease, labour rules.
  • Your organisation's hard rules. At Larder: Marco approves spending; Lena reviews contracts.
  • The seven questions. Every domain still needs an answer, even if the answer is one line.

A useful test: if you leave a practice out, can you say which risk it would have covered and what covers it now? “No communications plan, because the weekly status reaches everyone who needs it” passes. “No risk list, because we're agile” doesn't.

4Reviewing the fit

On 20 March, a month in, Sanne asks the leads two questions: what did we miss that a bit more process would have caught? and what did we do that nobody used?

The answers go straight into the table. A way of working that has never been changed is a sign that nobody has looked at it. Later notes show what that review found. One thing to know now: the table had no line for store IT and internet, because it sat between the fit-out and the platform. Tailoring can't see what the whole-project map misses.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small, familiar project

  • A conversation: “how do we want to run this?”
  • Three or four practices.
  • Adjust as you go.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • A one-page tailoring table.
  • Left-outs written down with reasons.
  • A review after a month.

Heavy

large, regulated, many parties

  • Tailoring agreed with a PMO.
  • Formal approval of deviations.
  • Reviewed at each phase gate.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Tailoring as “skip it”

    Leaving things out to save effort now. Fix → name the risk and what covers it.

  • Tailoring alone

    The team gets a process handed down. Fix → decide with the leads.

  • Never reviewed

    The fit drifts. Fix → a date in the diary.

  • Every template, shrunk

    Forty one-page documents. Fix → leave some out completely.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • At the start of every project.
  • When the team asks “do we have to?”
  • When process feels heavy or things keep being missed.
  • At each new phase.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip the decision. The table itself can be five lines.

8Self-check quiz

What is tailoring?

Deliberately fitting the approach, processes and documents to the project.

Are the forty processes compulsory?

No. They are a menu.

Name the four steps.

Choose the approach, fit to the organisation, fit to the project, keep improving.

Ben says “we're only fifteen weeks, let's drop the risk list”. What does Sanne ask?

Which risks it covers and what would cover them instead. Nothing would, so it stays, at fifteen lines.

What can't be tailored away?

The principles, the law and contracts, the organisation's hard rules, and an answer for each of the seven domains.

try it

List every document and meeting your project uses. Mark each keep, shrink or drop, and for each drop write what covers that risk now. Put a date in your diary one month from today: “review the fit”.
