Who Helps the Project Managers?

TL;DR

A project management office (PMO) is whatever helps an organisation run its projects in a consistent way: shared templates and lessons, a view across all projects, sometimes rules, sometimes the project managers themselves. It can be supportive (helps), controlling (requires and checks) or directive (runs the projects). The right size is the lightest one that stops each project from starting at zero.

  • Family: ways of working
  • Lives in: a shared folder and two hours a week, at first
  • Pairs with: tailoring, lessons learned, the portfolio

1The situation Larder, 25 February 2026

Femke counts on her fingers: Rotterdam, then Utrecht, The Hague, Eindhoven and Antwerp, and Mo arrives next week for Larder for Business. Six projects in two years, and one project manager, six weeks in. A board member has told her that serious companies have a PMO. “Do we need one?” Ben, overhearing: “please, no.”

  • Every launch so far started from a blank page.
  • Nobody sees all the projects on one sheet.
  • A heavy PMO would cost more than the problem.

2Real-life analogy the team behind the surgeons

In a hospital each surgeon runs their own operations. Behind them, a small team keeps the checklists current, schedules the theatres, and collects what went wrong so the next surgeon doesn't repeat it. That team never holds the knife. When it starts telling surgeons where to cut, the surgeons stop listening, and they are right to.

3How it solves it the lightest office that stops each project starting from zero

Sanne explains the three kinds. A supportive PMO offers templates, lessons and advice. A controlling one requires a method and checks it. A directive one runs the projects. For a company of Larder's size, with six similar launches ahead, she proposes the lightest: a “launch desk”. One plan template and one status format (Rotterdam's). One risk and lessons page across all cities. The launch playbook, updated after each city. And once a quarter, one sheet of all projects for Femke. Cost: two hours of her week. Femke agrees to try it until Utrecht, and to look again then.

  1. List the projects coming

    And who will lead them.

  2. Ask what each one would otherwise reinvent

    Templates, lessons, decisions.

  3. Choose the lightest form that covers it

    Supportive first.

  4. Start with what one project already made

    Rotterdam's pages become the templates.

  5. Give leadership one view across projects

    Quarterly is enough to begin.

  6. Review when the number of projects changes

    Grow it only when it's missed.

Vocabulary for the exam

PMO
Project management office: a function that supports or standardises how projects are run.
Supportive PMO
Offers templates, training and lessons. Low control.
Controlling PMO
Requires certain methods and checks compliance. Medium control.
Directive PMO
Manages the projects directly. High control.
Portfolio view
All projects on one sheet, for those who choose between them.
Centre of excellence
A group that keeps and spreads good practice.

# The launch desk · a supportive PMO, as small as possible

WHY     six projects in two years; each one so far started from a blank page
WHO     Sanne, 2 hours a week (no new people)

WHAT IT KEEPS
  templates   plan on a page · weekly status (7 lines) · role card · tailoring table
  lessons     one page across all cities, one line a week, sorted after each launch
  playbook    "how to open a city", updated after Rotterdam, used for Utrecht
  one sheet   every project: sponsor, benefit, next milestone, colour → Femke, quarterly

WHAT IT DOESN'T DO
  run other people's projects · approve their plans · add meetings

REVIEW  at the start of Utrecht: was anything reinvented? was anything unused?

tools: a shared folder, the documents one project already made, and one sheet for leadership.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a few projects, one or two project managers

  • A shared folder of templates and lessons.
  • One sheet across projects.
  • A couple of hours a week.

Standard

a dozen projects a year

  • A small supportive team.
  • Common status and risk formats.
  • Coaching for new project managers.

Heavy

many large or regulated projects

  • A controlling or directive PMO.
  • A required method and tools.
  • Portfolio reporting and assurance.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A PMO because “serious companies have one”

    Size before need. Fix → start from what gets reinvented.

  • The template police

    Checking forms, not helping projects. Fix → supportive first; measure by what teams reuse.

  • A PMO that owns the projects by accident

    Leads stop feeling responsible. Fix → say what it doesn't do.

  • Lessons nobody reads

    A graveyard folder. Fix → one page, sorted, used at every kick-off.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Several similar projects are coming.
  • Leadership can't see all projects together.
  • Each project reinvents the basics.
  • New project managers are joining.

Skip it when…

  • One project at a time and no repeats in sight.

8Self-check quiz

Name the three kinds of PMO.

Supportive, controlling, directive.

Which did Sanne propose, and why?

Supportive: the lightest that stops each launch starting from zero.

What does the launch desk not do?

Run other people's projects, approve their plans, or add meetings.

At Utrecht's kick-off the new lead ignores the templates and writes her own. What does Sanne do?

Ask why. If hers is better, the template changes. A supportive desk is judged by what teams choose to reuse.

When would Larder need a heavier PMO?

When there are many more projects, new project managers, or rules that require proof of how projects are run.

try it

Count the projects your organisation will run in the next year. Name three things each one will reinvent. What is the smallest thing (a folder, a page, an hour a month) that would stop that?

remember!!

Keep the checklists. Don't hold the knife.
