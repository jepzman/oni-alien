What Do I Do When?

TL;DR

Project work needs five kinds of attention. Initiating: agree it should exist. Planning: work out how. Executing: do it. Monitoring and controlling: compare with the plan and adjust. Closing: hand over and learn. They are not phases in a row: they overlap, they repeat for every phase and sprint, and monitoring runs all the time. Use them as a checklist: which kind of attention is this work missing?

1The situation Larder, 11 February 2026

Joost has read a little about project management, and Sanne's week confuses him. Monday she was planning Rotterdam. Tuesday she closed the helpdesk-tool decision. Wednesday she started something: the courier hiring drive got a sponsor. “I thought planning was the first phase and closing the last. Which phase are we in?”

  • The five words are read as five phases in a row.
  • So “planning” is declared finished, and never revisited.
  • And closing is left for the very end, when everyone has gone.

2Real-life analogy cooking a dinner of several dishes

A cook making dinner decides on a dish, plans it, cooks it, tastes and adjusts, and plates it. But not for all dishes at once, one step at a time. While the soup simmers, the roast is being tasted and the dessert is still being planned. And the cook tastes all the way through, not once at the end.

3How it solves it five kinds of attention, in a loop

Sanne draws it for Joost as a loop, not a line. Initiating, planning and executing follow each other, and executing feeds back into planning whenever something is learned. Monitoring and controlling sits under all of them. Closing happens every time something ends: a decision, a phase, a sprint, the project. The loop runs once for Rotterdam as a whole and again inside each phase. So the answer to “which phase are we in?” is: Rotterdam is mostly planning this week, and something is always being checked.

  1. Initiating

    Agree it should exist, why, and who backs it.

  2. Planning

    What, when, how much, who, what could stop us.

  3. Executing

    Do the work; lead the people.

  4. Monitoring and controlling

    Compare with the plan; decide what to change.

  5. Closing

    Hand over, settle up, keep the lessons.

  6. Repeat for every phase, and ask which one is missing

    Usually initiating or closing.

＋Going deeper how the focus areas, the domains and the 40 processes fit together

The eighth edition arranges its forty processes in a grid: seven domains down, five focus areas across. Once you can see that grid, the guide stops being a list of forty things to memorise. This chapter draws it, walks one real week through it, and shows why “closing” deserves more attention than it gets.

1The grid behind the guide

Every process in the guide answers two questions: which domain is it about (scope, schedule, risk…), and which kind of attention is it (planning it, doing it, checking it…)? So each process sits in one cell of a grid.

The pattern is regular, which is what makes it learnable. Almost every domain has a process for planning how it will be managed and one for monitoring and controlling it. Some add executing processes (leading the team, carrying out risk responses). Only Governance stretches across all five columns, because starting and closing a project are governance questions.

Read a row and you get a domain from start to finish: that is how Vols. 2 to 5 are organised. Read a column and you get everything that needs your attention at one moment: that is how a project manager's week is organised.

2One week, read across the grid

In the week of 9 February Sanne touches all five columns, on four different pieces of work. That is normal. What the grid adds is a check: for each piece of work, is any column empty that shouldn't be?

Her Monday check finds one. The Zuid cold room is fitted and tested, and everyone has moved on. Nobody has closed it: no sign-off from Tomás, no warranty papers filed, no note that the supplier delivered a week late (useful for Rotterdam's order). Ten minutes on Monday saves an argument in June.

3Why initiating and closing get skipped

Planning, executing and monitoring feel like work, so they happen. The two ends don't.

  • Initiating is skipped when work “just starts”: no sponsor, no stated reason, no agreed finish. Rotterdam itself had run for months this way. The cost appears later, as arguments about what the project was for.
  • Closing is skipped because the team is already needed elsewhere. The cost: results nobody formally accepted, bills and contracts left open, and lessons that are learned again, at full price, in the next city.

Each phase has both ends too. Before the fit-out started, someone had to say “yes, go” (initiating the phase). On 3 April someone has to accept the finished store (closing it). Vol. 2 turns those two moments into phase gates.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • Ask: who wants it? what's the plan? is it done and handed over?
  • No documents.
  • A lesson in one line.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • A Monday check across projects.
  • Each phase started and closed on purpose.
  • Weekly monitoring.

Heavy

large, regulated or contracted work

  • Formal gates with sign-off.
  • A documented process for each cell that applies.
  • Closure reports and audits.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Five phases in a row

    “We're past planning.” Fix → they overlap and repeat.

  • No initiating

    Work starts with no sponsor or reason. Fix → a sponsor and one line of why.

  • No closing

    Things drift to an end. Fix → close every phase: accept, file, learn.

  • Monitoring as a report

    Numbers collected, nothing decided. Fix → it ends in a decision, or it isn't controlling.

8Self-check quiz

Name the five focus areas.

Initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, closing.

Are they phases?

No. They overlap and repeat in every phase.

Which one runs all the time?

Monitoring and controlling.

The fit-out phase ends on 3 April. Which two focus areas apply on that day?

Closing (the store is accepted from the builder) and initiating (the stocking phase is given the go-ahead).

In March the plan is clearly wrong. Joost says “planning is over, just execute”. What does Sanne answer?

Planning is a kind of attention, not a finished phase. Executing has taught us something; we re-plan, and take it to the sponsor if a trigger is hit.

try it

List the pieces of work you look after. For each, mark which of the five kinds of attention it has had. Find one with no proper start or no proper close, and fix that this week.

remember!!

Five kinds of attention, not five phases. Taste all the way through.
