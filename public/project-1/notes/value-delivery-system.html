What Is It All For?

TL;DR

A project is one link in a chain. Strategy picks goals; a portfolio is everything the company invests in to reach them; a programme is a group of related projects; a project builds something new; operations uses it. Value appears only at the end: an output (a store) leads to an outcome (people order) and then a benefit (revenue, proof). Know your link, and what comes after it.

1The situation Larder, 16 January 2026

Joost hands Sanne his launch spreadsheet, Rotterdam v7: 140 rows, 31 without an owner. She reads all of it and asks one question: “when all 140 are ticked, what is better?” Joost says “we're open in Rotterdam.” Femke, later that day, says “we've shown we can do a city in weeks, not months.” Those are two different projects.

  • A task list can be 100 % done and the goal missed.
  • People on the same project aim at different results.
  • Nobody is asked to check the benefit after the launch party.

2Real-life analogy the kitchen rebuild

A family wants to eat better and spend less on takeaways. They rebuild the kitchen. The builder's job ends with a new kitchen: the output. What the family wanted is cooking at home four nights a week: the outcome. And behind that, better health and lower bills: the benefit. A beautiful kitchen nobody cooks in is a finished project and a failed investment.

3How it solves it find your link in the chain

Sanne draws the chain on one page. Larder's strategy: six cities by the end of 2027. The portfolio: Femke's board list (the cities, Larder for Business, the platform). The programme: the six-city expansion. Her project: Rotterdam. Then operations: the store, every day, from 27 April. Under it she writes three words: the outputs she will hand over, the outcome they should cause, and the benefit Larder is paying for. Joost and Femke both sign the page: one project again.

  1. Write the strategy goal in one line

    The one your project serves.

  2. Name the programme or portfolio above you

    Who decides between projects?

  3. List your outputs

    Things you can hand over.

  4. Write the outcome

    What people do differently because of them.

  5. Write the benefit, with a number and a date

    What the business gets.

  6. Name who owns the benefit after you close

    It is rarely the project manager.

Vocabulary for the exam

Portfolio
Everything an organisation invests in to reach its goals, managed together.
Programme
Related projects managed together because they share a goal.
Output
What the project produces and hands over.
Outcome
The change in what people do once they have the output.
Benefit
The measurable gain for the business from that change.
Value
The worth of the benefits, compared with what they cost.

＋Going deeper benefits: who owns them, and when they are checked

The note stops at one page. This chapter goes one step further: how to trace each output to a benefit, why the benefit needs an owner who is not the project manager, and how to tell when a project should stop.

1Draw the benefits map

A benefits map has three columns. Plan it from right to left: start with the benefits the business wants, ask what would have to change for them to happen (outcomes), then ask what must exist for that change (outputs). Deliver it from left to right.

The map earns its place when you find an output with no arrow going right. Sanne found 22 such rows in Joost's spreadsheet, among them branded tote bags and a launch-day animation in the app. They may be nice. They are not what Rotterdam is for, so they go to the bottom.

It also shows a benefit with no arrow coming in. “A new city in 8 weeks or less” had no output behind it until Sanne added one: a written launch playbook.

2Give every benefit an owner who stays

The project team hands over in May. The benefits arrive in May, in the summer, and when Utrecht starts. If the project manager is the only person who cares about them, nobody will be looking when they arrive, or when they don't.

So each benefit gets an owner in the business and dates to check it. For Rotterdam: Joost owns orders and on-time delivery, Femke owns the eight-week promise. Sanne's job is to hand over outputs that make those benefits possible, and to say early if they won't.

3Know when to stop

Value works in both directions. If the benefit can no longer be reached, or costs more than it is worth, the right decision may be to change or stop the project, even when the plan is on schedule. A project manager who thinks in outputs reports “on track”. One who thinks in value asks, at every review, is this still worth finishing?

For Rotterdam the answer is tied to the lease: stopping costs more than opening. That is a fact about value, and Sanne writes it down, so nobody has to argue about it in March.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a two-week internal change

  • One sentence: output → outcome → benefit.
  • Said at the start.
  • Checked once.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • A one-page value chain.
  • A benefits map.
  • Owners and check dates.

Heavy

a multi-year programme

  • A full business case.
  • A benefits realisation plan.
  • Reviews at every phase gate.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Output = success

    “We opened” is celebrated; nobody checks the orders. Fix → write the benefit and the date to check it.

  • Benefits with no owner

    The project closes and they belong to nobody. Fix → name an owner who stays.

  • A vague benefit

    “Grow in Rotterdam.” Fix → a number and a date.

  • Never asking “still worth it?”

    The plan is followed off a cliff. Fix → ask at every review.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • At the start of every project.
  • When people describe the goal differently.
  • When a task list has no “why”.
  • Before cutting scope.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip it. Shrink it to one sentence for tiny work.
