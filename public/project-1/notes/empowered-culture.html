principle 6 of 6 · a.k.a. “build an empowered culture”

Let the Team Decide

TL;DR

The people doing the work know most about it. An empowered culture gives them a clear frame (the goal, the limits, the date), the facts, and the authority to decide inside that frame, and makes it safe to say “this won't work”. The project manager's part is the frame and the fit between pieces, not every decision. Plans made by the team are better plans, and they get kept.

1The situation Larder, 3–4 February 2026

Sanne has promised a plan by 20 February. She does what worked at her old job: writes it herself. 96 tasks, each with a date and a name. On 3 February she shows Ben. He reads three lines: the app estimate is half what it needs, two tasks are the wrong way round, and nobody asked him. He isn't angry. He just won't be working to that plan.

  • The person planning doesn't have the facts.
  • People don't commit to dates they didn't give.
  • Every question now comes back to Sanne's desk.

2Real-life analogy the jazz band

A band leader names the tune, the key and the tempo, and counts the band in. Then each player decides what to play, listening to the others. Nobody hands the drummer every note. A leader who tried would get a worse band, and would be too busy writing notes to hear that the song was going wrong.

3How it solves it bring the frame, let the team plan, write who decides what

Sanne throws away the 96 tasks and books two hours on 4 February with Ben, Dee, Eli, Tomás and Joost's operations lead. She brings the frame: the benefit, the date, the budget, the limits from her scan. They bring the work: they break it down, estimate it and order it. She asks questions and writes down what waits for what. Then they agree who decides what: the team decides how and in what order; Sanne decides how the plan fits together; three things go to the sponsor. The plan has fewer lines than hers, and everyone in the room has put their name to a date.

  1. Give the frame

    Goal, date, money, limits. Short.

  2. Share the facts

    Everything you know, including bad news.

  3. Let the people who do the work plan it

    Their breakdown, their estimates.

  4. Write who decides what

    Three levels is enough.

  5. Make it safe to say “this won't work”

    Thank the first person who does.

  6. Stay out of the how

    Watch the fit between pieces.

Vocabulary for the exam

Empowerment
Giving people the authority, facts and trust to decide in their own area.
Self-organising team
A team that plans and divides its own work.
Psychological safety
Feeling safe to speak up, disagree and admit mistakes.
Decision level
Who decides: the team, the project manager or the sponsor.
Guardrails
The limits inside which a team is free to choose.
Commitment
A date someone gave themselves, not one they were given.

# Rotterdam · who decides what

THE FRAME (from Sanne)
  benefit   500 orders a day by end May · 90 % on time by end of summer · next city ≤ 8 weeks
  fixed     opening 27 April (lease) · budget €380k · max 4 new hires · StockPilot stays

THE TEAM DECIDES            how the work is done, how big it is, in what order
  apps Ben, Dee · platform Eli · stores Tomás · fit-out and people Joost's ops lead

SANNE DECIDES               how the pieces fit: dependencies, shared milestones, the weekly status

THE SPONSOR (JOOST) DECIDES when the date is at risk by more than 3 days
                            when a change costs more than €5,000
                            when what customers get at launch would change

RULES   say "this won't work" as soon as you think it · estimates belong to those who do the work

tools: a room, two hours, sticky notes, and the discipline not to pick up the pen.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small experienced team

  • State the goal and the limit.
  • The team plans.
  • Decision levels by word of mouth.

Standard

Rotterdam: five leads

  • A planning session with the leads.
  • A written “who decides what”.
  • Weekly check on the fit.

Heavy

many teams, new people, vendors

  • Team charters and working agreements.
  • A responsibility matrix.
  • Coaching for leads; escalation paths in writing.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Freedom without a frame

    “Just plan it.” Fix → goal, date, money and limits first.

  • A frame that is really a plan

    Every step prescribed. Fix → say what and why, not how.

  • Punishing bad news

    People stop bringing it. Fix → thank them, then act.

  • Taking the decision back

    At the first wobble. Fix → ask a question; don't take the pen.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Always, with the frame sized to the team.
  • When estimates keep being wrong.
  • When every question lands on one desk.
  • When a new plan is needed.

Skip it when…

  • A real emergency: decide, then explain. Go back to the frame afterwards.

8Self-check quiz

What does the project manager bring to planning?

The frame: goal, date, money, limits, and the facts.

Who estimates the app work?

Ben's and Dee's teams: the people who do it.

Name the three decision levels.

The team, the project manager, the sponsor.

In week six Eli says the platform set-up will be four days late. What does Sanne do first?

Thank him for saying it early. Four days is over the three-day trigger, so she takes options to Joost; she doesn't redo Eli's plan for him.

Why was the team's plan better than Sanne's 96 tasks?

It used their facts, and they committed to dates they gave themselves.

try it

Before your next planning meeting, write the frame on half a page: goal, date, money, limits. In the meeting, don't write a single task yourself. Afterwards, write down who decides what, in three levels.

remember!!

Give the tune, the key and the tempo. Let them play.
