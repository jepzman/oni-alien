Disagree and Commit

TL;DR

When a decision isn't yours to make and you disagree, say so once, clearly: in the decision meeting, ideally in writing, with your reasons and evidence. Once it's decided, commit fully: plan it as your own, brief your team without distancing yourself, and do your best work on it. Protect yourself and the company by agreeing how you'll know whether it worked and when you'll look again. Half-hearted compliance is worse than either option.

  • Family: hard moments
  • Origin: Intel (Andy Grove); popularised by Amazon's leadership principles
  • Lives in: a written case before the decision, and agreed success measures after
  • Pairs with: decision rights, bets not promises, OKRs

1The situation Larder, 1 March 2027

Femke wants Larder Plus, a subscription with free delivery for €7.99 a month, launched before Antwerp. Grace thinks Antwerp in French and the remaining swap work matter more this spring. The decision is Femke's. Grace could argue forever, comply half-heartedly, or say it once and then make Plus work.

  • Endless arguing stalls everyone.
  • Half-hearted delivery makes any decision fail.
  • Silence loses the information the decider needed.

2Real-life analogy the rowing crew

In a rowing boat the cox decides the line. A rower who thinks it's wrong says so once, clearly, with reasons. Then they row as hard as the rest. A rower who holds back half a stroke makes the boat slower than either line would have.

3How it solves it say it once, then commit, with a way to know

On 22 February Grace made her case once, in writing, with numbers, at the decision meeting. On 1 March Femke decided for Plus. Grace committed: she planned Plus as her own bet, briefed the team without “it was Femke's idea”, and agreed with Femke how they'd know: Plus members order 30 % more often than similar customers, and 5,000 members by September, with a check-in on 1 September. Antwerp's French work moved to May, agreed with Femke.

  1. Know who decides

    Decision rights first.

  2. Make your case once

    In the decision meeting, in writing, with evidence.

  3. Accept the decision

    Out loud, once it's made.

  4. Commit fully

    Plan it as your own; brief the team without distance.

  5. Agree how you'll know

    Success measures and a check-in date.

  6. Revisit on evidence

    At the check-in, not in the corridor.

Vocabulary for the exam

Disagree and commit
Stating disagreement, then fully supporting the decision.
Decision owner
The person who makes the final call.
Malicious compliance
Doing what was decided in a way designed to fail.
Success measure
A number that shows whether a decision worked.
Check-in
A date to look at the evidence again.
Escalation
Taking a disagreement higher (rarely right after a decision).

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Decisions move, not stall.
  • The decider hears the counter-case.
  • Full effort on what's chosen.
  • A fair test: measures agreed in advance.
  • Trust both ways.

What we lose

  • Working hard on something you doubted.
  • Your view may be right, later.
  • Needs genuine commitment, not acting.
  • Not for ethical issues: those need escalation.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Arguing after the decision

    Reopening it in every meeting. Fix → say it once; revisit at the check-in.

  • Silent disagreement

    Nodding, then doubting. Fix → say it in the meeting, in writing.

  • Distancing

    “Femke wants this.” Fix → brief it as our bet.

  • No measures

    Never knowing who was right. Fix → agree how you'll know.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Decisions owned by someone else that you doubt.
  • Strategic choices between good options.
  • When debate has stopped adding information.
  • Teams that keep relitigating decisions.

Skip it when…

  • Ethical, legal or safety concerns: escalate instead.

8Self-check quiz

When do you state your disagreement?

Once, in the decision meeting, ideally in writing.

What does committing fully mean?

Planning it as your own, briefing without distance, doing your best work.

How did Grace and Femke agree to judge Plus?

Order frequency +30 % and 5,000 members by September, checked on 1 September.

When should you not disagree and commit?

On ethical, legal or safety concerns: escalate instead.

try it

Think of a decision you disagreed with. Write your case in five lines as you would have before it was made. Then write how you'd know if the decision worked and when you'd look again, and share that, not the case.
