The One-Page PRD

TL;DR

A one-page PRD (product requirements document) says, on a single page: the problem and for whom, the outcome that shows it's solved, the users, what's in scope and, just as important, what's out, the open questions with owners, and links to everything else. It's the page everyone can read before a meeting and agree on; detail lives in stories, designs and ADRs.

  • Family: communicating
  • Origin: product practice; Amazon's PR/FAQ and “one-pager” traditions
  • Lives in: the product wiki, linked from the roadmap and the epic
  • Pairs with: problem statements, story mapping, working with the architect

1The situation Larder, 12 October 2026

Korenwolf's three-office contract starts in January. Ruben has sent Mo a 14-page requirements document in procurement language; ADR 0014 says scheduled orders live in Ordering. Ben's team asks Grace: what are we actually building for January? Nobody has said what problem it solves, or what's out.

  • Long documents get skimmed and read differently.
  • Without “out of scope”, everything is in.
  • The problem gets lost under the features.

2Real-life analogy the film's one-page pitch

Before a film is made, it's pitched on one page: who it's about, what they want, what stands in the way, what kind of film it is and what it isn't. Everyone on set, from the director to the caterer, knows what they're making. The script, the storyboards and the budget come after, and point back to it.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the product
Who it's aboutOffice managers, Mo, pickers, van drivers
What they wantOrder the pantry without phoning Mo
What kind of film it isn'tOut of scope: editing, API, custom invoices
The script and storyboardsStories, designs and ADR 0014
Everyone on setBen's and Dee's teams, Mo, Joost, Cho

3How it solves it one page, with what's out

Grace wrote one page: problem (office managers order by phoning Mo, who re-types it), outcome (three offices ordering without phoning, 98 % complete by 09:00), users, in for 4 January (weekly standing orders per office, cost centre on the invoice, skip a week), out (self-service editing, an API, custom invoice layouts), open questions with owners, and links to ADR 0014 and the contract. Ruben's 14 pages became an appendix. Ben: “first time I knew what not to build.”

  1. State the problem

    Who, what's hard, from their side.

  2. Name the outcome

    A number that shows it's solved.

  3. List the users

    Everyone it touches, including operations.

  4. Write in and out

    Out matters as much as in.

  5. List open questions

    Each with an owner and a date.

  6. Link the rest

    Stories, designs, ADRs, contracts.

Vocabulary for the exam

PRD
Product requirements document.
Scope
What the work includes.
Out of scope
What it explicitly doesn't.
Open question
Something not yet known, with an owner.
Appendix
Detail kept, but not in the way.
Single source of truth
The one page everything points to.

tools: the wiki or docs, one template; links to the backlog, designs and ADRs.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • One page everyone reads.
  • “Out” made explicit.
  • The problem stays visible.
  • Open questions owned.
  • Faster agreement before work starts.

What we lose

  • Hard to write short.
  • Clients may want their long document honoured.
  • Needs updating as answers arrive.
  • Not enough on its own for complex details.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • No “out”

    Everything implicitly in. Fix → write what's out, explicitly.

  • Features as the problem

    “Build standing orders.” Fix → who struggles with what.

  • Too long again

    Three pages creep in. Fix → link detail; keep one page.

  • Stale

    Answers never added. Fix → update it as questions close.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any piece of work bigger than a few stories.
  • Client work with long requirement documents.
  • Before kick-offs and refinement.
  • When teams ask “what are we building?”

Skip it when…

  • Tiny changes: the story is enough.

8Self-check quiz

What sections does a one-page PRD have?

Problem, outcome, users, in, out, open questions, links.

Why write what's out?

Otherwise everything is assumed to be in.

What happened to Ruben's 14 pages?

They became an appendix, linked from the page.

What was out for January?

Self-service editing, the API and a custom invoice layout.

try it

Take the biggest thing on your roadmap and write its one page: problem, outcome, users, in, out, open questions with owners, links. Show it to one engineer and one stakeholder and ask what's missing.

remember!!

Pitch the film on one page, including what it isn't.
