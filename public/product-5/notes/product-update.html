Product Update that Gets Read

TL;DR

A product update people read is short (one screen), regular (same day, same time), and always in the same order, with what matters most first: outcomes (the numbers you're trying to move), then shipped, learned, next and asks, each ask with a name and a date. A list of tickets tells people what the team did; a good update tells them what changed and what you need.

  • Family: communicating
  • Origin: product practice; “weekly snippets” and status-report habits
  • Lives in: a Friday message in chat and email
  • Pairs with: managing up, OKRs, dashboards that get used

1The situation Larder, 5 October 2026

Grace's weekly update is a two-page email listing every ticket shipped. It goes to 31 people; 6 open it, nobody clicks a link, and Priya still asks in meetings what's going on with live updates. Sam finds out about changes from customers.

  • Lists of tickets don't say what changed for customers.
  • Long updates are skipped, so the important line is missed.
  • Asks buried at the end never get answered.

2Real-life analogy the captain's log

A ship's captain writes a daily log: position, weather, what happened, what's next, what's needed from the owners. Short, the same order every day, the important line first. The owners read it in a minute and know whether to worry.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the product
PositionOutcomes: swap refunds, perfect orders
What happenedShipped, at most three lines
Weather aheadNext: what's coming
What's needed from the ownersAsks, each with a name and a date
Every day, same orderFridays at 16:00, same five sections

3How it solves it five sections, outcomes first

From 5 October Grace's update fits on one screen, Fridays at 16:00, in chat and email, always in the same order: outcomes (two numbers with their trend), shipped (at most three lines), learned (one thing), next, and asks, each with a name and a date. After six weeks 24 of 31 people open it, and every ask is answered by Monday. Priya stopped asking in meetings.

  1. Pick a fixed slot

    Same day and time every week.

  2. Lead with outcomes

    The numbers you're moving, with the trend.

  3. Keep shipped short

    Three lines, in customers' words.

  4. Share one lesson

    Including what didn't work.

  5. Make asks specific

    Who, what, by when.

  6. Watch who reads it

    Opens and replies; cut what nobody uses.

Vocabulary for the exam

Product update
A regular, short message about the product's progress.
Outcome
A change in customer behaviour or a business number.
Output
Something the team shipped.
Ask
A specific request: who, what, by when.
Cadence
The fixed rhythm of a message or meeting.
BLUF
Bottom line up front.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Read: 24 of 31 instead of 6.
  • Outcomes in view every week.
  • Asks answered quickly.
  • Fewer status meetings.
  • Support and ops hear first.

What we lose

  • A weekly habit to keep.
  • Hard choices about what to leave out.
  • Bad weeks are visible too.
  • Not for discussion: decisions need a conversation.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A ticket list

    Forty lines of Jira titles. Fix → outcomes first, three lines shipped.

  • Vague asks

    “Any help welcome.” Fix → a name and a date.

  • Only good news

    Bad weeks skipped. Fix → the same format every week.

  • Different every week

    Readers can't scan it. Fix → same five sections, same order.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any product with stakeholders outside the team.
  • When people keep asking “what's the status?”
  • Replacing status meetings.
  • Alongside quarterly reviews.

Skip it when…

  • Very small teams where everyone's in the room daily.
