Delivering Bad News

TL;DR

Bad news gets worse with time. Deliver it early (as soon as the forecast is clear, not the final number), in person, to your manager first and with them to whoever must act on it. Say it in four parts: the fact, the cause, the options (with their costs), and what you recommend. Leaders forgive misses; they don't forgive surprises.

1The situation Larder, 7 December 2026

Grace's forecast shows swap refund tickets will end December at about 2.4 %. In April the board was told 2 % by December. The board meets on 14 December. The temptation: wait for the final number, hope for a good last fortnight, and let the slide say it.

  • Waiting turns a miss into a surprise.
  • Bad news without a cause or a plan sounds like failure.
  • Your manager learning it in the board meeting costs trust.

2Real-life analogy the good doctor

A good doctor with a difficult diagnosis tells you in person, early and plainly: what they know and don't, what caused it, the options, and what happens next. You leave worried, but with a plan, and you trust them more, not less.

3How it solves it early, in person: fact, cause, options, recommendation

Grace told Priya in person the same day, and on 8 December they told Femke together, a week before the board. Four parts: the fact (about 2.4 % against 2.0 %); the cause (Rotterdam's slot orders, with longer windows and notifications off, are 63 % of remaining refunds); the options (an in-app banner and SMS fallback in Rotterdam, aiming for 2.0 % by March; or forcing no-swap defaults, which cuts refunds but loses sales); and her recommendation. Femke took a range and the plan to the board. December ended at 2.4 %; March reached 2.0 %.

  1. Tell as soon as it's likely

    On the forecast, not the final number.

  2. Manager first, in person

    Then together to whoever must act.

  3. State the fact plainly

    No softening adjectives.

  4. Explain the cause

    With data, without blame.

  5. Bring options with costs

    At least two, honestly.

  6. Recommend and follow up

    What you'll do, and when you'll report.

Vocabulary for the exam

Forecast
A projection of where a number will end up.
Miss
A result short of a goal.
Root cause
The underlying reason for a problem.
Range
Telling a number as a span, with confidence.
Escalation
Bringing a problem to people who can act.
No surprises
Leaders hear important news early, from you.

FOR THE BOARD   "2.3–2.5 % in December; 2.0 % by March, with plan A"

tools: the forecast on the health page, a conversation, a short written follow-up with the same four parts.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Trust kept: no surprise at the board.
  • Time to act a week earlier.
  • Options chosen by the right people.
  • A plan instead of an excuse.
  • Leaders defend you when they're prepared.

What we lose

  • An uncomfortable conversation.
  • The forecast might have been wrong.
  • Some leaders react badly at first.
  • Takes preparation: cause and options.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Waiting for certainty

    “Let's see the final number.” Fix → tell on the forecast.

  • Email at night

    Read alone, without context. Fix → in person, then a note.

  • No cause, no plan

    Just the miss. Fix → fact, cause, options, recommendation.

  • Skipping your manager

    Straight to the CEO. Fix → manager first, then together.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Missed targets, slipping dates, failed tests.
  • Incidents that affect customers or clients.
  • When a promise made by someone else won't hold.
  • Before any meeting where the news would surprise.

Skip it when…

  • Never; only how early you can tell changes.
