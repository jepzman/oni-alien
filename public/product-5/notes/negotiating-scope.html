Negotiating Scope

TL;DR

Scope negotiations stall when both sides trade lists (positions). Ask instead why each item matters (interests): often a smaller or different solution meets the same need. Then split by time (now, later, not planned) rather than yes or no, keep the account manager or client sponsor in the room, and write the agreement down the same week. Everyone gets what they actually need, if not everything they asked for.

1The situation Larder, 23 November 2026

Ruben sends fourteen must-haves for Korenwolf's go-live on 4 January, including self-service editing, a custom invoice layout and real-time stock in the portal. Grace's team can do five well in the time. Mo wants to say yes to everything; Grace's instinct is to say no to nine. Two lists, two positions, no deal.

  • Lists hide why each item is wanted.
  • Yes-to-all fails on the date; no-to-nine fails the relationship.
  • Agreements made in a meeting are remembered differently.

2Real-life analogy the orange and the peel

Two sisters argue over one orange. Their mother cuts it in half. One sister eats her half of the fruit and throws away the peel; the other grates her half of the peel into a cake and throws away the fruit. If anyone had asked why each wanted it, both would have had everything they needed.

3How it solves it interests first, then split by time

Grace and Mo met Hanna and Ruben together. First the interests: Hanna wants no complaints from staff or the board; Ruben wants costs booked per department and a service level he can measure. That reshaped the list. For 4 January: standing orders per office, cost centre on every invoice line, skip a week, a monthly delivery report, changes by email to Mo before 08:00. In spring: self-service changes in a portal. Not planned: the custom invoice layout (the cost centres met Ruben's need). Signed by both sides that week.

  1. Meet the people with the interests

    The sponsor and the buyer, with the account manager.

  2. Ask why for each item

    What problem, what happens without it.

  3. Find smaller ways to meet needs

    The peel, not the orange.

  4. Split by time

    Now, later, not planned.

  5. Say what “not planned” means

    With the reason.

  6. Write it down, same week

    Signed by both sides.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Needs met, not lists.
  • A date the team can hit.
  • The relationship kept.
  • Smaller solutions found.
  • Written agreement ends re-arguing.

What we lose

  • Takes a meeting, not an email.
  • Some asks wait or never come.
  • Clients may push back on “not planned”.
  • Needs the right people in the room.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Trading lists

    Seven yes, seven no. Fix → ask why for each.

  • Yes to everything

    A promise the date can't keep. Fix → split by time.

  • Without the account manager

    Mo surprised later. Fix → negotiate together.

  • Nothing written

    Remembered differently in January. Fix → a signed page, same week.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Client deadlines with long wish lists.
  • Internal stakeholders with competing asks.
  • Renewals and contract changes.
  • Any “all of it, by Friday” moment.

Skip it when…

  • Truly non-negotiable items (law, safety): explain, don't trade.

8Self-check quiz

What's the difference between a position and an interest?

What someone asks for vs why they want it.

What were Ruben's interests?

Costs per department and a measurable service level.

How was the scope split?

Five things for January, the portal in spring, the custom layout not planned.

Why write it down the same week?

So nobody remembers it differently on the date.

try it

Take the longest wish list you've been handed. For each item, ask (or guess, then check) why it's wanted, find a smaller way to meet that need, and sort everything into now, later and not planned.

remember!!

Ask why before you cut the orange: one wants the fruit, one the peel.
