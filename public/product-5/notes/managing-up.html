Managing Up

TL;DR

Managing up means making it easy for your manager to back you: no surprises, a regular, short channel where they see the decisions you need, the risks and what you're doing about them, the decisions you've made, and the wins worth passing on. Learn how they like information (numbers or stories, written or spoken) and what they must defend to their own boss. It isn't flattery; it's keeping the person who carries your work upstairs well informed.

  • Lives in: a shared one-to-one document and a weekly slot

1The situation Larder, 19 October 2026

Priya has been surprised twice in a month: she heard about the live-updates guardrail trip from Femke, and about the slot waitlist being switched off from Sam. Grace thought both were too small to bother her with. Priya felt blindsided in front of her own boss, and started asking for daily status messages.

  • What's small to you can be big for your manager.
  • Surprises cost trust, and trust costs autonomy.
  • Without a channel, information arrives by accident.

2Real-life analogy the co-pilot

A co-pilot doesn't fly the plane for the captain, and doesn't stay silent either. They call out what the captain needs to know (fuel, weather, a warning light) early, in agreed words. The captain never learns about the warning light from air traffic control.

3How it solves it a shared doc, one rule, her style

Grace set up a shared one-to-one document, updated the day before their weekly 30 minutes: decisions I need (by when), risks (and what I'm doing), decisions I made (so you hear it from me), wins worth passing up. One rule: no surprises, bad news within a day, in person. She learned Priya's style: numbers before stories, and a written pre-read for anything Priya must defend to Femke. After a month Priya dropped the daily status request.

  1. Ask what they need

    What they must answer for, to whom.

  2. Set a regular channel

    A weekly slot and a shared document.

  3. Use four headings

    Decisions needed, risks, decisions made, wins.

  4. Agree “no surprises”

    Bad news within a day, in person.

  5. Learn their style

    Numbers or stories; written or spoken.

  6. Make them look good

    Give them what they need to defend your work.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • No more surprises for your manager.
  • Decisions faster: asked clearly.
  • More autonomy, earned by trust.
  • Better air cover when work is challenged.
  • Your wins reach leadership.

What we lose

  • A weekly habit.
  • Judgment about what's worth mentioning.
  • Bad news said out loud, early.
  • Different managers need different styles.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Only big news

    Small decisions surprise her later. Fix → list decisions made, every week.

  • Problems without plans

    “We have an issue.” Fix → the risk and what you're doing.

  • Bad news by email

    Discovered at night. Fix → in person or a call, within a day.

  • Your style, not theirs

    Stories for a numbers person. Fix → learn how they like it.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • With any manager, from the first week.
  • When your manager seems surprised or anxious.
  • Before asking for more autonomy.
  • When your work is politically exposed.

Skip it when…

  • Never; only the format changes.

Your manager hears important news from you first.

So your manager hears them from you, not from others.

try it

Ask your manager what they have to answer for to their boss. Then start a shared doc with the four headings, update it before your next one-to-one, and agree a “no surprises” rule.
