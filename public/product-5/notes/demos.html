Demos that Land

TL;DR

A demo that lands tells a customer's story (someone, something they need, what happens now), uses real devices and realistic data, happens where the work happens if possible, and invites the people it changes, not just the team. Keep it short, then ask a real question (“what would go wrong here?”) and listen. A demo is for learning, not applause.

1The situation Larder, 2 November 2026

At the sprint review Ben shares his screen and clicks through tickets: “and this one adds a field…”. Four people attend, all from the team. The pickers and support agents whose work will change aren't there, and nobody asks anything. Two weeks before, a picker-app change reached Noord as a surprise.

  • Tickets show what was built, not what changes for anyone.
  • The right audience isn't invited.
  • Nobody is asked anything, so nothing is learned.

2Real-life analogy the house viewing

A good estate agent doesn't read out a list of features. They walk you through the house as if you lived there: morning coffee in the kitchen, the kids' room, the route to the station, with the people who'd live in it. That's when the family notices the stairs are too steep.

3How it solves it a story, real devices, the right people, a question

Grace ran the standing-orders demo at Noord at 06:00: Korenwolf's office manager sets a weekly order on Thursday; on Monday the picker's handheld shows it; the van leaves at 07:10. Real devices, realistic data. She invited Tomás and two pickers, Sam, Mo and Eli. Twenty minutes, then: “What would go wrong here?” Tomás saw it at once: the van's loading list was in picking order, not delivery order. Fixed before go-live.

  1. Pick a customer's story

    Someone, a need, what happens now.

  2. Use real devices and data

    The phone, the handheld, realistic orders.

  3. Go where the work happens

    The store, the support desk, a call with the client.

  4. Invite the people it changes

    Operations, support, sales, a customer.

  5. Keep it short

    Twenty minutes; one story beats ten features.

  6. Ask, then listen

    “What would go wrong here?” and write it down.

# Demo · business standing orders · Mon 2 Nov 2026, 06:00, Noord · 20 min

Story      Korenwolf Amsterdam's office manager sets a weekly pantry order (Thu)
           → Monday 06:00 the order appears on the picker's handheld → packed in crates
           → van loading list → van leaves 07:10 → delivered by 09:00
Devices    office manager: laptop (admin tool for now) · pickers: two real handhelds · Eli: van app
Data       3 test offices, 48 lines each, realistic products and cost centres
Invited    Tomás + 2 pickers, Sam, Mo, Eli (Dispatch), Ben, Noor
Question   "What would go wrong here?"
Heard      Tomás: loading list in picking order, not delivery order → fixed 9 Nov
           Sam: who do office managers call at 07:30 if something's missing? → Mo's number on the note

tools: real devices, a staging environment with realistic data, a short script; record the session for those who can't come.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Problems found before go-live.
  • The right people see it early.
  • Operations and support prepared.
  • Shared understanding of the change.
  • Shorter than a ticket tour.

What we lose

  • More preparation.
  • Early mornings in the store.
  • Uncomfortable feedback in front of others.
  • Realistic data takes work.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Ticket tours

    “This one adds a field.” Fix → a customer's story.

  • Team-only audience

    Nobody it affects. Fix → invite operations, support, sales.

  • Fake data

    “Test product 1”. Fix → realistic orders.

  • No question

    Applause, then nothing. Fix → ask what would go wrong.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every sprint review.
  • Before changing someone's daily work.
  • Before launches and client go-lives.
  • Showing progress to leaders.

Skip it when…

  • Purely technical changes nobody outside the team will notice.

8Self-check quiz

What makes a demo land?

A customer's story, real devices, the right audience, and a question.

Who should be invited?

The people whose work or experience the change affects.

What did Tomás find?

The van's loading list was in picking order, not delivery order.

What's the point of a demo?

Learning, not applause.

try it

For your next sprint review, pick one customer's story, run it on a real device, and invite two people outside the team whose work it changes. End with “what would go wrong here?” and note every answer.

remember!!

Give a house viewing, not a feature list.
