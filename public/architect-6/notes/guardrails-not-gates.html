Guardrails Not Gates

TL;DR

Gates stop every change for a human check; with many teams they become queues people wait at or skip. Guardrails are automated checks and good defaults that keep every change within safe limits while teams move at their own speed: fitness functions, policy as code, templates, alerts. Keep human review only for one-way doors (new data stores, vendors, trust boundaries, public contracts), and make that review fast.

  • Family: governance
  • Origin: DevOps and platform engineering practice; “guardrails, not gates” (e.g. Thoughtworks Technology Radar)
  • Lives in: CI checks, policy as code, templates and a short list of review triggers
  • Pairs with: architecture principles, fitness functions, golden paths, lightweight review

1The situation Larder, 17 April 2028

Since 2026 every significant change goes through Ana's architecture review board, every other Thursday. With five teams the queue is two weeks long; teams wait, or quietly skip it. Most reviewed changes are reversible and get waved through; the board catches almost nothing a check couldn't.

  • Gates slow everyone to catch a few.
  • Long queues teach people to go around them.
  • Humans checking routine rules miss things machines don't.

2Real-life analogy the mountain road

A mountain road doesn't put a checkpoint every kilometre where a guard inspects each car. It has guardrails along the edge and a few signs; drivers go at their own speed, and the guardrails stop the ones who'd go over. There's a checkpoint only at the border.

3How it solves it automate the checks, review the one-way doors

Ana and Eli turned the board's routine questions into guardrails on every change: the nine fitness functions, policy as code on infrastructure (EU regions only, encryption on, tags present), the golden-path templates, and cost alerts. Human review stays only for one-way doors: a new data store, a new vendor, a new trust boundary, a change to published events. The board was replaced by a lightweight review on request. Waiting: two weeks to none for most changes; three to five days for one-way doors.

  1. List what the gate checks

    Every question the board asks.

  2. Automate the routine ones

    Fitness functions, policy as code, templates.

  3. Define the one-way doors

    The few changes that still need people.

  4. Make that review fast

    Written-first, days not weeks.

  5. Remove the gate

    Announce what replaced it.

  6. Watch what slips through

    Add a guardrail when something does.

Vocabulary for the exam

Gate
A required human approval before a change proceeds.
Guardrail
An automatic limit or default that keeps changes safe.
Policy as code
Rules written as code and checked automatically.
Review trigger
A kind of change that needs human review.
Lead time
Time from idea or commit to production.
Paved road
The supported, easy way to do something.

# How architecture is governed at Larder · from 17 Apr 2028

GUARDRAILS (every change, automatic)
  Fitness functions FF-01 … FF-12 in CI and production
  Policy as code (infrastructure): EU regions only · encryption at rest · cost tags · no public buckets
  Golden-path templates: logging, metrics, SLOs, data class, deploy pipeline built in
  Alerts: cost per 1,000 orders, new external domains called from production

REVIEW TRIGGERS (one-way doors → lightweight review, 3–5 days)
  a new data store · a new external vendor or SaaS · a new trust boundary ·
  a change to a published event or public API · leaving a principle (exception)

Everything else: decide in the team, record an ADR if it's significant.

tools: CI fitness functions, policy as code (Open Policy Agent, cloud policies), service templates, cost and dependency alerts, a request form for reviews.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • No queue for most changes.
  • Checks every change, not just reviewed ones.
  • People's time on the hard decisions.
  • Nobody goes around the process.
  • Teams own their decisions.

What we lose

  • Building guardrails takes effort.
  • Some things slip until a guardrail exists.
  • Policy code to maintain.
  • Less visibility for the architect.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Removing the gate first

    Nothing replaces it. Fix → guardrails before removing the gate.

  • Reviewing everything

    One-way doors undefined. Fix → a short list of triggers.

  • Slow reviews

    One-way doors wait weeks. Fix → written-first, days.

  • Flaky guardrails

    Checks ignored when red. Fix → keep them reliable, or remove them.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Growing organisations with review queues.
  • When teams go around architecture processes.
  • Platforms with many teams shipping daily.
  • After writing principles.

Skip it when…

  • Highly regulated changes that legally need human sign-off (keep those gates, make them fast).

8Self-check quiz

What's the difference between a gate and a guardrail?

A gate stops changes for human approval; a guardrail automatically keeps changes within safe limits.

What still gets human review at Larder?

One-way doors: new data stores, vendors, trust boundaries, published events.

What did waiting time become?

None for most changes; three to five days for one-way doors.

What happens when something slips through?

A new guardrail is added.
