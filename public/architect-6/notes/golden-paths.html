Golden Paths

TL;DR

A golden path (or paved road) is a supported, ready-made way to do a common job, like starting a new service, that already includes everything the organisation expects: pipeline, observability, security, data classes, cost tags, guardrails. Make it the easiest option rather than a rule: optional, documented, maintained by a platform team. When the right way is the easy way, governance mostly takes care of itself.

  • Family: governance
  • Origin: Spotify's golden paths; Netflix's paved road; platform engineering
  • Lives in: a service template and a command, owned by the platform team
  • Pairs with: guardrails not gates, platform thinking, architecture principles

1The situation Larder, 8 May 2028

Starting a new service at Larder takes about three weeks: copy an old repository, fix its pipeline, set up logging, metrics and alerts, ask Eli for a database, find out about data classes and cost tags. Each new service ends up slightly different, and some skip the parts the guardrails now require. The right way is the hard way.

  • Rules without help get skipped.
  • Copying old services copies old mistakes.
  • Every team re-solves the same setup.

2Real-life analogy paving the paths people walk

In a park where everyone cut across the grass, the gardeners stopped putting up signs. They paved the paths people actually walked and lit them at night. Now the easy way is also the right way, and the grass grows back.

3How it solves it one command, everything built in, optional

Eli's platform team built the golden path for a new service: one command creates a repository with the pipeline, logs, metrics and traces, health checks, a default SLO and dashboard, a data-class declaration, cost tags, and a managed database if asked. It passes every guardrail on day one. It's optional: teams can leave the path, but then they own what it gave them. New service time: three weeks to one day. Four of the next five services used it.

  1. Pick the most common job

    Usually a new service or a new data pipeline.

  2. Include everything expected

    Pipeline, observability, security, guardrails.

  3. Make it one step

    A command or a template.

  4. Keep it optional

    Teams may leave, and then own the rest.

  5. Give it an owner

    A platform team keeps it current.

  6. Measure adoption and time

    Days to first deploy; share of services on it.

# Golden path: a new service at Larder                       owner: platform team (Eli)

    larder new-service <name> [--db postgres] [--events consume|publish]

You get, passing every guardrail on day one:
  - repository + pipeline (build once, deploy many; canary to production)
  - logs, metrics, traces; health checks; default SLO (99.9 %, p95 500 ms) + dashboard
  - data-class declaration (FF-09) · cost tags · EU region only (policy as code)
  - managed Postgres (optional) with backups in DR tier 2 by default
  - event contracts registered (FF-08) if --events

Optional. Leaving the path is fine; you then own what it would have given you.
Measured: new service to first production deploy 3 weeks → 1 day; 4 of 5 new services on it.

tools: a service template and CLI (cookiecutter, Backstage software templates), the platform's managed databases, CI templates, the internal developer portal.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Three weeks to one day.
  • Guardrails passed from day one.
  • Consistent services, easier to run.
  • Teams choose it; no policing.
  • Improvements reach everyone.

What we lose

  • A platform team to fund.
  • Templates age without care.
  • Old services aren't on it.
  • One size won't fit every case.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Mandatory path

    Forced, then resented. Fix → optional and better.

  • No owner

    The template rots. Fix → a platform team owns it.

  • Too clever

    Magic nobody understands. Fix → plain, documented defaults.

  • Only for new services

    Old ones drift. Fix → a migration path for existing services.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Organisations creating new services or pipelines regularly.
  • After introducing guardrails.
  • When setup takes weeks.
  • When services drift apart.

Skip it when…

  • Very small systems with one or two services.
