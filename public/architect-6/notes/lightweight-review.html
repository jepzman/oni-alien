Lightweight Architecture Review

TL;DR

A lightweight architecture review is for decisions that are hard to undo. It's written first (a draft ADR with options), read by two reviewers (the architect and a peer from another team) who comment in writing within days, followed by a short call only if needed, ending in one of three outcomes: go, go with changes, or not yet (with what's missing). Days, not weeks; recorded, not hallway chat.

1The situation Larder, 15 May 2028

The first one-way door under the new rules: the business team wants a new search database for the catalogue. The old board would have taken two weeks and thirty slides. Without a format, the new review risks becoming a hallway chat that nobody records.

  • Heavy reviews get avoided.
  • Informal reviews leave no record and no clear outcome.
  • The architect alone becomes the bottleneck again.

2Real-life analogy peer review

A paper's peer review starts with the author writing it down. Reviewers read it before meeting, comment in the margins, and the editor gives one of three outcomes: accept, revise, reject. Short, written, with a clear answer, and the reasons kept with the paper.

3How it solves it written first, two reviewers, three outcomes

The team writes a draft ADR with options and the principles it touches. Two reviewers (Ana and a tech lead from another team) comment in writing within three days; a 45-minute call only if needed; then one of three outcomes. The search database got go with changes: the managed search service in the EU region, fed by catalogue events, added to the DR tiers as tier 2. Four working days from draft to decision. Every tech lead now reviews twice a quarter.

  1. Trigger only on one-way doors

    The review list, nothing else.

  2. Write first

    A draft ADR: context, options, principles touched.

  3. Two reviewers

    The architect and a peer from another team.

  4. Comment in writing, fast

    Within three working days.

  5. Meet only if needed

    Forty-five minutes, to resolve open points.

  6. Give one of three outcomes

    Go; go with changes; not yet, with what's missing.

Vocabulary for the exam

Lightweight review
A short, written review for important decisions.

Reviewer rotation

Approved if specific changes are made.

Not approved until named gaps are filled.
Async-first
Written comments before any meeting.

Status: Accepted (go with changes) · 2028-05-19 · Team: business ordering (Jonas, Dee)
Reviewers: Ana, Ben · written comments 15–17 May · no call needed

Options      A Postgres full-text (today) · B managed search service · C self-hosted search

Time         draft to decision: 4 working days

tools: ADRs in pull requests, a review-trigger list, a reviewer rotation, a 45-minute calendar slot when needed.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Days, not weeks.
  • Written record of reasons.
  • Clear outcomes.
  • Peers learn by reviewing.
  • The architect isn't alone.

What we lose

  • Writing first takes effort.
  • Reviewers' time.
  • Written comments can feel cold.
  • Needs discipline about deadlines.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Reviewing everything

    Reversible changes queued. Fix → only one-way doors.

  • Meeting first

    Nobody read anything. Fix → written first.

  • Vague outcomes

    “Looks fine, but…” Fix → go, go with changes, not yet.

  • One reviewer

    Always the architect. Fix → a peer too, on rotation.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • New data stores, vendors, trust boundaries, public contracts.
  • Exceptions to principles.
  • Big migrations' key decisions.
  • When teams want a second opinion.

Skip it when…

  • Two-way doors: decide in the team.
