Architecture Principles

TL;DR

Architecture principles are a handful (five to ten) of short rules that capture how the organisation builds, each with a statement, the reason, and the implications (what it means you do and don't do). They let teams make consistent decisions without asking the architect, and they make reviews faster because the reasoning is shared. Write them with the people who'll use them, from decisions you've already made, and retire the ones nobody needs.

  • Family: governance
  • Origin: TOGAF's principle format (statement, rationale, implications)
  • Lives in: one page in the architecture docs, linked from templates and reviews
  • Pairs with: guardrails not gates, ADRs, golden paths

1The situation Larder, 3 April 2028

Larder now has five product teams and thirty engineers, eight hired this year. They keep asking Ana the same questions: can we read another module's table? which database? sync call or event? Ana is the bottleneck, and her answers live only in her head and in 26 ADRs nobody new has read.

  • One architect can't answer for thirty engineers.
  • ADRs record decisions, not the rules behind them.
  • New people repeat old mistakes.

2Real-life analogy the house rules on the fridge

A family's house rules hang on the fridge: shoes off at the door, whoever cooks doesn't wash up, screens off at dinner. A babysitter can follow them without phoning the parents, and the reasons are obvious enough that nobody argues.

3How it solves it eight rules, each with a reason and implications

Ana drafted eight principles from 26 ADRs and agreed them with the tech leads: a city is configuration; events for facts, APIs for commands; one owner per kind of data; protect the critical windows; boring technology by default; cost per order is a feature; one-way doors in writing; privacy by default. Each has a reason and implications, and links to its ADRs. The design-note and review templates now ask which principles apply. Questions to Ana fell from about fifteen a week to four.

  1. Mine your decisions

    ADRs and incidents show the real rules.

  2. Write five to ten

    Short statements people can remember.

  3. Add reason and implications

    Why, and what you do and don't do.

  4. Agree with the people who'll use them

    Tech leads, product, operations.

  5. Wire them into templates

    Design notes, reviews, golden paths.

  6. Review yearly

    Retire, rewrite or add.

Vocabulary for the exam

Principle
A rule with a reason that guides decisions.
Rationale
Why the principle exists.
Implication
What following it means in practice.
Boring technology
Proven tools the team knows well.
Governance
How decisions are guided and checked.
Exception
A recorded, reasoned case of not following a principle.

# Larder architecture principles (8) · agreed 3 Apr 2028 · reviewed yearly · owner: Ana

3. One owner per kind of data
   Statement     Every kind of data has one owning module and one master copy.
   Rationale     Shared tables tied seven modules to Core's schema (2025–26); 212 products
                 disagreed across systems before Antwerp (ADR 0024).
   Implications  - Never read or write another module's tables (FF-11 checks it).
                 - Get data from the owner's events or API; keep a local copy if needed.
                 - New data: name the owner in the design note before building.
   ADRs          0002, 0023, 0024, 0027
   Exceptions    recorded in an ADR; reviewed at the yearly principles review

tools: one page in the docs, the TOGAF statement/rationale/implications format, links from design-note and review templates, fitness functions for the checkable ones.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Consistent decisions without asking.
  • Faster onboarding.
  • Shorter reviews: shared reasoning.
  • The architect isn't the bottleneck.
  • Some principles become checks.

What we lose

  • Vague principles are useless.
  • Rules can harden into dogma.
  • Exceptions need a process.
  • A yearly review to keep them true.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Platitudes

    “We value quality.” Fix → a rule you could break, with implications.

  • Too many

    Thirty principles. Fix → five to ten.

  • No reasons

    Rules without why. Fix → a rationale from real decisions.

  • Carved in stone

    Never revisited. Fix → yearly review, recorded exceptions.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Growing teams where the architect becomes a bottleneck.
  • Onboarding many new engineers.
  • Before replacing reviews with guardrails.
  • When decisions are inconsistent across teams.

Skip it when…

  • One small team that decides together daily.
