Evolutionary Architecture

TL;DR

An evolutionary architecture is designed to change: it moves in small, reversible steps, it protects the qualities that matter with fitness functions (automated checks in CI and production), and it keeps room to turn by recording decisions with the conditions that would change them. The aim isn't to predict the future but to make the next change safe, so the system never needs a big rewrite.

1The situation Larder, 22 May 2028

Larder's architecture has changed every year: a modular monolith, then Dispatch as a service, then events, then a new warehouse system, and now Ordering's own database. Each change risked breaking qualities nobody was watching. Femke asks: will we have to rebuild everything at 60,000 orders a day?

  • Architectures that can't change get rewritten, expensively.
  • Changes break qualities that aren't checked.
  • Big-bang changes are hard to undo.

2Real-life analogy the old city

An old city changes for centuries without being demolished: streets are added, buildings replaced, a tram line threaded through. What holds it together are a few rules everyone keeps: building lines, heights, the canals stay open. Change everything else, one building at a time.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the project
One building at a timeSmall, reversible releases
Building lines and heightsFitness functions: boundaries, p95, cost
The canals stay openNo direct reads of another module's tables

Never demolishedNo big rewrite

3How it solves it small steps, fitness functions, room to turn

Ana wrote down how Larder evolves: small steps, each released on its own and reversible; nine fitness functions in CI and production guarding module boundaries, checkout p95 at load, cost per 1,000 orders, declared data classes and no reads of another module's tables; and decisions recorded with triggers, so there's room to turn. Her answer to Femke: no rebuild. The Ordering split is the next step of the same evolution, city by city, guarded by the same checks.

  1. Name the qualities that matter

    From the utility tree.

  2. Guard each with a fitness function

    Automated, in CI or production.

  3. Change in small steps

    Each releasable and reversible.

  4. Record decisions with triggers

    So you know when to turn.

  5. Review the fitness functions

    Add, tighten or retire as priorities change.

  6. Avoid big bangs

    Strangler and expand/contract instead.

Vocabulary for the exam

Evolutionary architecture
Architecture that supports guided, incremental change.
Fitness function
An automated check that a quality holds.
Incremental change
Changing in small, safe steps.
Reversibility
How easily a change can be undone.

The smallest part that can be deployed on its own.
Big bang
Changing everything at once.

FF-07  WMS B client only inside the Stock adapter                CI        since 2027
FF-08  event schema changes compatible (registry)                CI        since 2027
FF-09  new tables and topics declare a data class                CI        since 2027
FF-10  cloud cost per 1,000 orders ≤ €70 (alert)                 daily     since 2028
FF-11  no reads of another module's tables (DB grants + scan)    CI + prod since May 2028
FF-12  bundle ≤ 300 KB, home ≤ 2.5 s (performance budgets)       CI        since 2027

tools: import-linter or ArchUnit, load tests with thresholds, cost alerts, schema registry checks, database grants; ADRs with triggers.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • No rewrites: each change is a step.
  • Qualities guarded automatically.
  • Reversible releases.
  • Confidence for big changes.
  • Decisions that can turn on evidence.

What we lose

  • Fitness functions to build and keep.
  • Small steps can feel slow.
  • Temporary states to manage.
  • Discipline across teams.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Predicting the future

    Designing for every possible need. Fix → make the next change safe.

  • Unguarded qualities

    Boundaries erode quietly. Fix → a fitness function per quality.

  • Big bangs

    Everything switched in one night. Fix → small, reversible steps.

  • Too many checks

    Fifty flaky checks. Fix → a few that matter, kept green.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Systems that will keep growing for years.
  • Before and during big migrations.
  • When qualities have broken unnoticed.
  • When leaders fear a rewrite.

Skip it when…

  • Short-lived systems and prototypes.
