Negotiating with Stakeholders

TL;DR

When architecture needs time, money or a change of plan, it's a negotiation, not a lecture. Look for the interests behind each stakeholder's position (why that date? why that budget?), put several options on the table that serve all of them, use fair criteria (numbers everyone accepts), and write the agreement down the same day. Most deadlocks between leaders are positions colliding while their interests don't.

  • Origin: Roger Fisher and William Ury, Getting to Yes (1981)
  • Lives in: one meeting with options prepared, and a written agreement
  • Pairs with: influencing without authority, trade-off sliders, negotiating scope (Grace)

1The situation Larder, 17 July 2028

Femke wants Haarlem open on 4 September for the board's eight cities. Marco wants the cloud bill flat. Ana needs Ordering's switch finished before a new city starts on the old database. Three reasonable positions, colliding on one week in September.

  • Positions sound fixed; interests rarely are.
  • Architects arguing “technical reasons” lose to dates.
  • Unwritten agreements get remembered differently.

2Real-life analogy three neighbours and a driveway

Three neighbours share a driveway that needs repaving: one wants it done before her daughter's wedding, one wants to spend as little as possible, one wants the drain fixed first. Talking about why, they find a plan: drain this week, paving the next, the cheapest contractor who can do both. Nobody gives anything up.

3How it solves it interests, options, fair criteria, in writing

Ana asked why before the meeting. Femke needed eight cities by the board on 28 September, not the 4th specifically. Marco needed cost per order not to rise. Ana needed Haarlem born on the new database. She brought options: Haarlem on 11 September, born on the new database; the old database's last replica switched off the same week, so no extra cloud spend; her commitment on cost per order. Agreed in one meeting, written the same day. Haarlem opened on 11 September.

  1. Ask why, before the meeting

    Each position's real interest.

  2. Find fair criteria

    Board dates, cost per order: numbers all accept.

  3. Prepare several options

    Each serving every interest somewhat.

  4. Talk interests in the room

    Not technical reasons.

  5. Agree and write it down

    The same day, to everyone.

  6. Keep your side

    And report when you have.

tools: one-to-one conversations before the meeting, options on one page, numbers everyone trusts, a written agreement.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Agreements instead of deadlocks.
  • Everyone's real need met.
  • Architecture needs heard in business terms.
  • One meeting, prepared.
  • Trust for the next negotiation.

What we lose

  • Preparation before the meeting.
  • Some positions really are fixed.
  • Compromises cost something.
  • Needs credibility with leaders.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Technical arguments

    “The database isn't ready.” Fix → their interests, in their terms.

  • One option

    Take it or leave it. Fix → several prepared.

  • Meeting cold

    Interests discovered in the room. Fix → ask why beforehand.

  • Nothing written

    Remembered differently. Fix → write it the same day.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When architecture needs time or money.
  • Conflicting dates between business and platform work.
  • Budget and capacity discussions.
  • Vendor and partner agreements.

Skip it when…

  • Decisions inside your own authority.

try it

Think of a conflict between your architecture needs and someone's date or budget. Write each side's position and the interest behind it, then three options that serve every interest. Take them to one meeting.
