Feedback Loops with Operations

TL;DR

The people who operate your product every day (pickers, couriers, store staff) see problems long before dashboards do. Build a feedback loop: an easy way in (a button on the device they already use), a regular visit where they work, and answers going back (“you said, we did”). Without the way back, people stop telling you; with it, they become your best early-warning system.

  • Origin: lean “go and see” (genchi genbutsu); service design practice
  • Lives in: the handheld app, a fortnightly store visit and a notice board

1The situation Larder, 20 July 2026

With answers at 88 %, the next question is why customers accept only 71 % of substitutes. Pickers at Noord know: the handheld lists suggestions by price, so the first one is often a strange choice (oat milk for whole milk, the cheapest bread for sourdough). They've grumbled about it for weeks, but only to each other. Nobody asked, and there was no easy way to tell the team.

  • Operations staff see problems first and have the least time to report them.
  • Feedback without answers dries up.
  • Occasional visits catch what's visible that day, not patterns.

2Real-life analogy the backstage noticebook

Backstage, a theatre keeps a noticebook: crew write anything that slows the show (a sticking door, a dim light). The stage manager reads it daily and writes back what's fixed and when. The crew keep writing because things get fixed; a noticebook nobody answers is empty within a month.

3How it solves it easy in, regular visits, answers back

Dee added an idea button to the handheld: one tap, a sentence or a photo. Every other week the trio spends twenty minutes in the store with the manager and two pickers. Answers go on the notice board: “you said, we did”. In three weeks: 14 items. The top one, sort suggestions by similarity, shipped on 3 August, and customers accepting substitutes rose from 71 % to 78 %. Tomás: “first time anyone wrote back.”

  1. Make it easy

    A button on the device they use; one tap, a sentence or photo.

  2. Go to where the work is

    Fortnightly, short, in the store, at a busy time.

  3. Triage like any request

    Joins an opportunity, quick fix, parked, declined.

  4. Write back, visibly

    “You said, we did” where they'll see it.

  5. Measure the effect

    Pick the metric the fix should move.

  6. Thank by name

    Credit the picker whose idea shipped.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Problems found early, by those who see them.
  • Real fixes: 71 → 78 % accepted.
  • Trust with operations.
  • Cheap: a button and twenty minutes.
  • Better designs from people doing the work.

What we lose

  • A habit to keep.
  • Raised expectations if answers stop.
  • Noise mixed with gold.
  • Store time during busy hours.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • No way back

    Ideas vanish; people stop sending them. Fix → “you said, we did”, visibly, every time.

  • Hard way in

    A form on a laptop pickers never use. Fix → a button on the device they hold.

  • Visits at quiet times

    Nothing to see at 11:00. Fix → go at peak.

  • Only the manager

    Pickers' view filtered. Fix → talk to pickers directly.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Products used by operations staff.
  • When metrics drop and nobody knows why.
  • Any feature that changes someone's daily work.
  • Building trust with operations.

Skip it when…

  • Products with no operational users.

8Self-check quiz

What are the three parts of the loop?

An easy way in, regular visits where the work is, answers going back.

Why does the way back matter?

Without answers, people stop giving feedback.

What did the top item change?

Suggestions sorted by similarity; acceptance rose from 71 % to 78 %.

When should you visit?

At busy times, when the work is happening.

try it

Add the simplest possible feedback route for the people who operate your product, and visit them for twenty minutes at their busiest time. Answer every item within two weeks in a place they'll see, and track one metric for the first fix.

remember!!

Keep a noticebook backstage, and always write back.
