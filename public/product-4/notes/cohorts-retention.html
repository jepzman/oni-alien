Cohorts & Retention Curves

TL;DR

Totals mix everyone together: new customers can hide lost ones. A cohort is a group that shares a starting point (joined in March, had a refund ticket, had an approved swap). A retention curve shows what share of a cohort is still active over time. Compare cohorts to see whether something changes behaviour: does a good experience bring customers back? Use them for questions about keeping customers, which totals can't answer.

  • Family: instrumenting
  • Origin: cohort analysis in epidemiology and marketing; SaaS retention practice
  • Lives in: the analytics tool or a warehouse query, reviewed monthly
  • Pairs with: funnels, north star metric, post-launch review

1The situation Larder, 6 July 2026

Femke asks the question behind the business case: do approvals actually keep customers? The monthly active customers line is going up, but it mixes in thousands of new Rotterdam customers. Vol. 1 found that customers with two refund tickets order 30 % less; nobody knows yet whether a good swap undoes the damage. One total line can't say.

  • Growth from new customers hides whether existing ones stay.
  • Averages over time blur the effect of a change.
  • “Keeps customers” needs comparing similar groups over time.

2Real-life analogy the gym's members

A gym tracks members by the month they joined: of January's joiners, how many still come in February, March, April? Then it compares with March's joiners, who had the new induction class. If March's line stays higher, the induction works. The total number of visitors could never show that; new joiners would hide it.

3How it solves it compare similar groups over time

Grace grouped spring's customers by their first swap: ended in a refund ticket, an approved swap, or no swap at all. After eight weeks, 58 % of the refund-ticket cohort were still ordering, against 71 % for approved swaps and 69 % for no swap. A good swap doesn't just avoid harm: customers come back like those who never needed one. Femke took the chart to the board.

  1. Define the starting event

    What puts someone in the cohort: first order, first swap, joined month.

  2. Define “still active”

    Ordered again in the week? In the month?

  3. Build the curve

    Share active at each period since the start.

  4. Compare cohorts

    Before and after a change; with and without an experience.

  5. Check they're comparable

    Similar customers? Same season? Watch for selection effects.

  6. Review monthly

    New cohorts show whether a change is working.

Vocabulary for the exam

Cohort
A group sharing a starting event or period.
Retention
The share of a cohort still active after some time.
Retention curve
Retention plotted over time since the start.
Churn
Customers who stop being active.
Selection effect
Cohorts differing for reasons other than the thing studied.
Monthly active customers
A total that mixes all cohorts.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Answers “do they come back?” honestly.
  • Growth can't hide losses.
  • Effects of changes show in new cohorts.
  • Strong evidence for leadership.
  • Links product to revenue over time.

What we lose

  • Takes weeks to see a curve.
  • Cohorts can differ for other reasons.
  • Easy to over-read small cohorts.
  • Not causal on its own (A/B tests are).

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Reading totals

    Monthly actives up, all is well. Fix → compare cohorts.

  • Unfair comparisons

    Cohorts from different seasons or stores. Fix → check composition; compare within segments.

  • Small cohorts

    Conclusions from 40 customers. Fix → wait for size, or show the uncertainty.

  • Treating it as proof

    Correlation read as cause. Fix → use A/B tests for causal claims.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Questions about keeping customers.
  • Judging whether a change improved habits.
  • Subscription and repeat-purchase products.
  • Explaining long-term value to leadership.

Skip it when…

  • Questions about one session or one step: use funnels.
  • Causal claims: run an experiment.

8Self-check quiz

What's a cohort?

A group sharing a starting event or period.

Why can't total active customers answer “do they come back?”

New customers hide whether existing ones stay.

What did Larder's curves show?

After eight weeks: refund ticket 58 %, approved swap 71 %, no swap 69 %.

What's a selection effect?

Cohorts differing for reasons other than the thing you're studying.

try it

Pick a good and a bad experience in your product. Group customers by which they had first, define “active”, and plot eight weeks of retention for both groups. Check one way they might differ besides the experience.

remember!!

Count members by the month they joined. Totals hide who left.
