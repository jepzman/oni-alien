Killing a Feature

TL;DR

Features are easy to add and nobody's job to remove, so products fill up with things few people use and everyone maintains. Killing a feature on purpose: make the case with data (usage, outcomes, upkeep, confusion), talk to the people who use it and offer an alternative, announce it in advance, switch it off, then delete the code and data, and tell the team what it freed up.

1The situation Larder, 21 September 2026

The slot waitlist was a Should for Rotterdam's launch and shipped in June. By September, 1.1 % of slot customers have joined a waitlist, and 9 % of those ever ordered from one. It has caused two bugs, costs about two engineer-days a month, and produces a trickle of confused tickets. Nobody wants to be the one to remove it: someone asked for it, and someone built it.

  • Features accumulate; removal has no owner.
  • Every feature costs upkeep, support and attention.
  • Removing feels like admitting a mistake.

2Real-life analogy taking a dish off the menu

A restaurant takes a dish off the menu when few people order it, it slows the kitchen and takes fridge space. A good chef tells the regulars who liked it, suggests an alternative, and frees the kitchen for the dishes people love. A menu that only grows gets worse, not better.

3How it solves it case, users, announce, off, delete

Grace put the case to Priya and Sam with numbers, then called the twelve customers who used the waitlist most and offered slot alerts by email; most were happy. An in-app notice ran two weeks; Rotterdam's store manager heard first. The waitlist was switched off on 5 October, and the code and data were deleted the same week. Tickets about it since: zero; two engineer-days a month went back to the roadmap.

  1. Review usage regularly

    Every feature's usage, outcomes and upkeep, once or twice a year.

  2. Make the case with data

    Usage, effect on outcomes, bugs, upkeep, support load.

  3. Talk to the users

    The heaviest users first; offer an alternative.

  4. Announce in advance

    In the product, two weeks or more; staff told first.

  5. Switch off, then delete

    Remove the code and data, not just the button.

  6. Say what it freed

    Time, attention and simplicity, out loud.

Vocabulary for the exam

Upkeep
The ongoing cost of keeping a feature working.
Long tail

Deprecation notice
Telling users a feature will go.
Sunk cost
The effort already spent; not a reason to keep it.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • A simpler product for everyone.
  • Time freed for what matters.
  • Fewer bugs and tickets.
  • Honest about bets that didn't work.
  • Users treated well on the way out.

What we lose

  • A few users lose something.
  • Uncomfortable for whoever asked for it.
  • Effort to remove code and data.
  • Occasional backlash.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Switching off but not deleting

    Dead code and data linger. Fix → delete code and data the same week.

  • Surprising users

    It vanishes one morning. Fix → announce in advance; talk to heavy users.

  • Sunk-cost loyalty

    “We spent six weeks on it.” Fix → decide on value from here.

  • Never reviewing

    Features only accumulate. Fix → a usage review once or twice a year.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Features with low usage and real upkeep.
  • After a post-launch review shows a bet didn't pay.
  • Before a big redesign: fewer things to carry.
  • When support keeps explaining a confusing feature.

Skip it when…

  • Features with low usage but high importance (legal, safety, accessibility).
  • Too early: give a new feature time and a fair test.

8Self-check quiz

Why do features rarely get removed?

Removal is nobody's job, and it feels like admitting a mistake.

What goes into the case?

Usage, effect on outcomes, bugs, upkeep and support load.

How should users be treated?

Talked to first, offered an alternative, warned in advance.

What's the last step?

Delete the code and data, and say what it freed.

try it

List the five least-used features in your product with their upkeep and ticket load. Pick one, write the case in five lines, call three of its heaviest users, and propose a sunset date to your manager.
