Reading Results Honestly

TL;DR

Most bad experiment decisions come from reading results, not running tests. Don't peek and stop when it looks good; read once, at the planned end. Report the primary metric as a range (a confidence interval), not a single point. Check guardrails. Treat segment findings as new hypotheses, not results. And decide by the rule you wrote before the test, not by how you feel about the numbers.

1The situation Larder, 7 July 2026

On day three of the window test, three minutes is ahead by 9 points and the dashboard says “significant”; Femke wants to stop and ship. On day five, an analyst finds that Android users gained 11 points and suggests a special rule for Android. Both are tempting. Both are traps: early leads shrink, and if you look at enough segments, one always looks exciting.

  • Checking daily and stopping on a good day inflates false wins.
  • Slicing results many ways finds patterns that are just noise.
  • Single numbers hide how uncertain the result is.

2Real-life analogy the bet on the match

You bet on the full ninety minutes. Settling at half-time because your team is ahead isn't the result; neither is a friend who, after losing, points out his team won the second half. Both are true statements about part of the match, and both are wrong about the bet.

3How it solves it read once, as a range, by the rule

Grace held the line: the test ran its two weeks. The day-three lead of 9 points ended at +6 (interval +3.8 to +8.2), still a clear win, but smaller. Guardrails held. The Android gain went on the opportunity tree as a hypothesis for its own test, not a rule. The decision followed the card: ship three minutes. Femke later said the shrinking lead was what convinced her to wait next time.

  1. Pre-register

    Metric, guardrails, duration, rule: written before day one.

  2. Don't peek and stop

    Look only for safety (guardrails); read the result once, at the end.

  3. Report a range

    The difference with its confidence interval.

  4. Check guardrails

    Any tripped guardrail overrides the win.

  5. Treat segments as hypotheses

    Interesting splits become new tests.

  6. Decide by the rule

    And write down any disagreement with it.

Vocabulary for the exam

Peeking
Checking results repeatedly and stopping when they look good.
Pre-registration
Writing the plan and rules before the test.
Confidence interval
A range likely to contain the true effect.
Multiple comparisons
Testing many slices; some look significant by chance.
False positive
A “win” that isn't real.
Regression to the mean
Extreme early results drifting back toward normal.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Fewer false wins shipped.
  • Honest uncertainty in every result.
  • Segments become a test backlog, not rules.
  • Decisions match the plan, so nobody moves goalposts.
  • Leaders learn to wait.

What we lose

  • Patience: no early celebrations.
  • Ranges disappoint people who want one number.
  • Some real segment effects wait for their own test.
  • Discipline under pressure.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Stopping on a good day

    Day three looked great. Fix → read once, at the planned end.

  • A single number

    “+6 points.” Fix → the range: +3.8 to +8.2.

  • Segment rules

    A special Android rule from one slice. Fix → segments become new hypotheses.

  • Rewriting the rule

    “+6 is basically +4, let's also…” Fix → decide by the rule; record any disagreement.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every experiment's results.
  • When leaders want early answers.
  • When someone finds an exciting segment.
  • Reviews of past experiments.

Skip it when…

  • Safety stops: if a guardrail is badly broken, stop early (that's what guardrails are for).

8Self-check quiz

Why not stop on day three?

Early results are noisy; stopping on a good day inflates false wins.

How should the primary result be reported?

As a difference with a confidence interval.

What do you do with an exciting segment?

Turn it into a hypothesis for its own test.

When is it fine to stop early?

When a guardrail is badly broken: a safety stop.

try it

Take your last experiment. Rewrite its result as a difference with a confidence interval, list every segment someone highlighted, and check whether the decision matched a rule written in advance. Write the rule for the next one now.
