A/B Tests

TL;DR

To know whether a change caused a difference, compare two groups that differ only in that change: split users at random into A (as now) and B (the change), run both at the same time, and compare on a primary metric chosen in advance, with guardrails that mustn't get worse. Write the metric, guardrails, duration and decision rule before the test starts. Before/after comparisons mix in everything else that changed.

  • Origin: randomised controlled trials; online experiments (Ronny Kohavi et al.)

1The situation Larder, 22 June 2026

Should customers get two or three minutes to answer a swap? Sam says three: customers complain the timer is stressful. Tomás says two: pickers can't wait longer. Both have anecdotes. Changing it for everyone and comparing June with July would mix in the summer holidays, the weather and Rotterdam's growth; whatever happened, nobody could say why.

  • Anecdotes favour whoever has the most vivid story.
  • Before/after comparisons can't separate the change from everything else.
  • Without a metric chosen first, both sides will claim the result.

2Real-life analogy the fair taste test

A fair taste test of two recipes assigns tasters at random to A or B; they don't know which they got; the plate, temperature and time of day are the same. The only difference is the recipe, so any difference in scores is the recipe's. Serving A on Monday and B on Friday would also be testing Mondays and Fridays.

3How it solves it random split, one change, decided in advance

Grace wrote the test card first: primary metric share of swaps answered by the customer, guardrail picking time per swap order ≤ +15 s, two full weeks, and the rule “ship B if it wins on the primary metric and the guardrail holds”. Result: 82 % vs 88 %, a difference of +6 points (95 % interval +3.8 to +8.2); picking +7 s vs +11 s. Three minutes from 13 July. Sam and Tomás both read the same card.

  1. Write the hypothesis

    “Three minutes will raise the share of swaps customers answer.”

  2. Choose one primary metric

    And two or three guardrails, with limits.

  3. Work out sample size and duration

    Before starting; whole weeks (see the next note).

  4. Split at random

    By customer, not by session; stable for the test.

  5. Don't change anything else

    No other releases to the same flow during the test.

  6. Decide by the rule you wrote

    Ship, don't ship, or re-test: as the card says.

Vocabulary for the exam

A/B test
A randomised comparison of a control (A) and a variant (B).
Control
The current version.
Variant
The changed version.
Primary metric
The one number the test is designed to move.
Randomisation
Assigning users to groups by chance.
Confidence interval
The range the true difference likely lies in.

# Test: approval window, 2 vs 3 minutes                      owner: Grace · flag: approvals.window

Hypothesis   3 minutes raises the share of swaps answered by the customer.
Population   Amsterdam express customers with a swap; 50/50 by customer id
Primary      swaps answered by the customer (not defaulted)        baseline 82 %
Guardrails   picking time per swap order ≤ +15 s · refund tickets per 100 swaps not up
Size         ≈ 1,300 swaps per arm to detect +4 pts (see sample-size note)
Duration     22 Jun → 6 Jul (two full weeks, two Friday peaks)
Rule         Ship B if primary up and both guardrails hold; else keep A.

Result       A 82 %  B 88 %  · +6 pts (95 % CI +3.8 to +8.2) · picking +7 s vs +11 s
             refund tickets per 100 swaps: 2.9 vs 2.8 · ≈ 5,200 swaps per arm
Decision     B: three minutes for all Amsterdam express customers, 13 Jul. Rotterdam: 20 Jul.

tools: a feature flag with percentage and sticky assignment (Statsig, GrowthBook, LaunchDarkly, Optimizely); analytics for results; a test-card template.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Cause, not correlation.
  • Debates settled by agreed numbers.
  • Guardrails protect operations.
  • Decisions written in advance, so nobody moves the goalposts.
  • Confidence intervals show how sure we are.

What we lose

  • Needs traffic: small products wait long.
  • Two versions to run for weeks.
  • Only measures what you chose to measure.
  • Easy to misread without discipline.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Before/after

    June vs July, holidays and all. Fix → run both at the same time, randomised.

  • Metric chosen afterwards

    Whichever number looks best. Fix → write the primary metric on the card first.

  • Splitting by session

    Customers see both versions. Fix → assign by customer, sticky.

  • Other changes mid-test

    A new release to the same flow. Fix → freeze the flow during the test.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Changes where the effect is uncertain and traffic is enough.
  • Debates that anecdotes can't settle.
  • Changes that might hurt something else (with guardrails).
  • Tuning settings like timers, wording, ordering.

Skip it when…

  • Low traffic: use qualitative tests or a longer, simpler comparison.
  • Obvious fixes (a bug) and legal changes.

8Self-check quiz

Why randomise?

So the groups differ only in the change, not in who they are or when.

What must be written before the test?

Hypothesis, primary metric, guardrails, duration and decision rule.

Why split by customer, not session?

So each customer sees one version consistently.

What was the window test's result?

82 % vs 88 % answered (+6 points), guardrail held; three minutes shipped.

try it

Pick a setting your team argues about. Write a test card: hypothesis, one primary metric, two guardrails with limits, duration in whole weeks, and the decision rule. Get both sides of the argument to sign it before the test starts.

remember!!
