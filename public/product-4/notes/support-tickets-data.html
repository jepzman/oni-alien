Support Tickets as Data

TL;DR

Support tickets are the largest, cheapest stream of customer pain you have, if you read them as data, not anecdotes. Agree consistent reason tags with support, check them, and review the top reasons per 1,000 orders (normalised, so growth doesn't fool you) every week, with the trend. Use it to pick problems, set targets and close requests, and keep reading a few actual tickets for the “why”.

1The situation Larder, 15 June 2026

Sam's team handles about 900 tickets a week. Grace hears about them as anecdotes: an angry email forwarded by Femke, a story over lunch. The loudest complaint gets attention; the most common one doesn't. Sam's auto-refund button (parked in March) is up for review, and nobody has the numbers to decide it.

  • Anecdotes favour vivid stories over frequent ones.
  • Raw counts rise with orders, hiding whether things improve.
  • Support's knowledge stays in support.

2Real-life analogy the doctor's appointment book

A family doctor writes a short, consistent reason next to each appointment. Read weekly, the book shows what no single visit can: a rise in ear infections after the swimming pool reopened. The doctor still listens to every patient; the book shows where to look.

3How it solves it tags, per 1,000, weekly, with the trend

Sam's team already used the tag rule from the baseline work in Vol. 1. Grace and Sam added a weekly review: the top five reasons per 1,000 orders, with the trend, fifteen minutes on Mondays. It paid off at once: swap refunds had nearly halved (6.2 → 3.4), so the auto-refund button was closed with Sam's agreement; and “where is my order?” at 11.2 per 1,000 became the target for live updates.

  1. Agree reason tags

    Ten to fifteen, mutually exclusive, with examples; one per ticket.

  2. Check the tagging

    Sam's team re-checks a sample monthly (the baseline found 38 % wrong once).

  3. Normalise

    Per 1,000 orders, so growth doesn't hide improvement.

  4. Review weekly

    Top five reasons, trend, fifteen minutes, with the support lead.

  5. Read real tickets too

    Ten a week from the top reason, for the why.

  6. Act and close the loop

    Decisions go back to support: “you said, we did”.

Vocabulary for the exam

Ticket reason
The tag saying why the customer contacted support.
Normalised rate
Tickets per 1,000 orders, comparable over time.
Voice of the customer
Customer feedback gathered and used systematically.
Contact rate
Tickets per order: how often customers need help.
Tag drift
Tags used inconsistently over time.
Closing the loop
Telling support what changed because of their data.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Frequent pain over loud pain.
  • Cheap, large, continuous customer signal.
  • Targets set from real numbers.
  • Requests closed with evidence.
  • Support's knowledge reaches the product.

What we lose

  • Tags need discipline.
  • Only customers who complain are counted.
  • Numbers lack the why without reading tickets.
  • A weekly habit to keep.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Raw counts

    Tickets up 20 %: panic. Fix → per 1,000 orders.

  • Messy tags

    Twenty overlapping reasons. Fix → few, exclusive, with examples; check a sample monthly.

  • Numbers only

    Nobody reads a ticket. Fix → ten real tickets a week from the top reason.

  • One-way flow

    Support never hears what changed. Fix → “you said, we did” back to support.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any product with a support team.
  • Choosing which problem to tackle next.
  • Checking whether a launch reduced contacts.
  • Closing or confirming feature requests from support.

Skip it when…

  • Very low ticket volumes: read them all instead.

8Self-check quiz

Why normalise tickets per 1,000 orders?

So growth in orders doesn't hide whether things are improving.

What decision did the first review drive?

Closing the auto-refund button: swap refunds had nearly halved.

What's still needed beyond the numbers?

Reading a sample of real tickets for the why.

What was the top reason?

“Where is my order?”, 11.2 per 1,000 orders.

try it

Ask your support lead for last month's tickets by reason. Turn them into rates per 1,000 orders and a four-week trend. Read ten tickets from the top reason, then decide one thing to do about it and tell support.
