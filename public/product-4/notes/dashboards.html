Dashboards That Get Used

TL;DR

Dashboards multiply and stop being read. Build one page people actually use: the north star at the top, the few input metrics that drive it, the guardrails that mustn't get worse, each with this period, last period and the target. Give it a fixed review (fifteen minutes, weekly, with the people who act on it), and archive the rest. Detail belongs in analyses, not on the health page.

1The situation Larder, 13 July 2026

The analytics tool now holds fourteen product dashboards, built by different people for different questions. Most haven't been opened in a month. When Priya asks “how is ordering doing?”, Grace still builds a slide by hand every Monday, copying numbers from four of them.

  • Every question gets a dashboard; nobody removes old ones.
  • No page shows the few numbers that matter together.
  • Without a review rhythm, nobody looks until something breaks.

2Real-life analogy the car dashboard

A car's dashboard shows speed, fuel and a few warning lights, always in the same places, read at a glance while driving. Not forty gauges. The detailed diagnostics live in the garage's computer, used when something needs investigating. Nobody studies the dashboard; they glance and drive.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the product
SpeedThe north star: perfect orders per week
FuelInput metrics: complete, on time, no refund, answered
Warning lightsGuardrails: conversion, picking time, crashes

3How it solves it one page, a weekly glance

Grace replaced the slide with an ordering health page: the north star on top, four inputs underneath, three guardrails below, each with this week, last week and the target. Every Monday, fifteen minutes with Priya and the trio. Twelve dashboards were archived (two kept for deep dives). The first review caught on-time slipping in Rotterdam a week before anyone complained.

  1. Start from the north star

    One number at the top, with its target.

  2. Add the inputs that drive it

    Three to five; the levers the team can pull.

  3. Add guardrails

    Two or three things that mustn't get worse.

  4. Show now, before, target

    For each number; colour only against the target.

  5. Set a review rhythm

    Weekly, fifteen minutes, with the people who act.

  6. Archive the rest

    Keep analyses for deep dives; remove unused dashboards.

Vocabulary for the exam

Health page
One page with the few numbers that show whether the product is OK.
North star
The one number for the value customers get.
Input metric
A number that drives the north star and the team can move.
Guardrail
A number that must not get worse.
Vanity metric
A number that looks good but doesn't guide action.
Review rhythm
A fixed time when the page is read and acted on.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • One page everyone reads.
  • Problems caught weekly, not monthly.
  • No manual slides.
  • Shared numbers for product and leadership.
  • Fewer dashboards to maintain.

What we lose

  • Choosing what's off the page is hard.
  • Metrics need to be right first.
  • A weekly habit to keep.
  • Deep questions still need analyses.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Fifty numbers

    A health page nobody can read at a glance. Fix → north star, a few inputs, a few guardrails.

  • No targets

    Numbers without a sense of good or bad. Fix → show the target next to each.

  • No rhythm

    Opened only when something breaks. Fix → a fixed weekly review.

  • Keeping everything

    Old dashboards linger. Fix → archive what nobody opened in a month.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any product with a north star and a team that acts on numbers.
  • When leadership keeps asking for slides.
  • When dashboards have multiplied.
  • After a launch, to watch steady state.

Skip it when…

  • One-off analyses: keep them as analyses.
  • Incident monitoring: that's operations dashboards and alerts.

What goes at the top of the health page?

The north star, with its target.

What are input metrics?

The few numbers that drive the north star and the team can move.

What makes a dashboard get used?

Few numbers, targets beside them, and a fixed weekly review.

What happened to the other dashboards?

Twelve archived; two kept for deep dives.

try it

Count your product's dashboards and when each was last opened. Design one health page on paper: your north star, up to five inputs and three guardrails, each with now, before and target. Book fifteen minutes a week to review it, and archive anything unopened for a month.
