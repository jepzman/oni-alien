Vanity vs Actionable Metrics

TL;DR

Vanity metrics look impressive and change nothing: totals that only go up (downloads, sign-ups, “swaps approved since April”). Actionable metrics can go both ways and would change a decision if they moved: rates per customer or per order, shares, retention. Test every number on a slide with one question: if this changed, would we do something different? If not, replace it.

  • Lives in: every slide, report and health page
  • Pairs with: OKRs, dashboards that get used, north star metric

1The situation Larder, 22 June 2026

Femke's draft board slide for June leads with two big numbers: 120,000 swaps approved since April! and 180,000 app downloads! Both are true and both are growing. Neither would make anyone do anything differently, and neither says whether customers trust their orders more, which is what the business case was about.

  • Cumulative totals only rise, so they always look good.
  • Big numbers impress without informing.
  • The numbers that would show a problem aren't on the slide.

2Real-life analogy the gym's two numbers

A gym can report total sign-ups ever, which only rises, or the share of new members still training after three months, which can fall. Only the second tells the manager whether the new induction is working, and what to change if it isn't. The first looks great on a poster.

3How it solves it one question per number

Grace asked the same question of each number: if this changed, would we do something different? The totals failed. She offered three that pass: swap refund tickets per 100 orders (6.2 → 3.4), the share of swaps answered by the customer (82 %), and customers still ordering eight weeks after a swap (71 %). Femke used them; the board's questions were sharper (“what would take refunds to 2?”).

  1. List every number on the slide

    Or the report, or the dashboard.

  2. Ask the question

    If this changed, would we do something different?

  3. Prefer rates and shares

    Per order, per customer, per week: comparable over time.

  4. Check it can go both ways

    Totals that only rise are almost always vanity.

  5. Pair with a target

    So the reader knows good from bad.

  6. Keep vanity numbers out of decisions

    Fine for a celebration; not for steering.

Vocabulary for the exam

Vanity metric
A number that looks good but doesn't guide action.
Actionable metric
A number whose change would change a decision.
Cumulative total
A running sum: only goes up.
Rate
A number per order, customer or time unit.
Ratio
One number divided by another, comparable over time.
Leading indicator
An early, actionable signal (see Vol. 1).

Test for every number: "If this changed, would we do something different?"

tools: any reporting tool; the habit of asking the question in reviews; rates and ratios instead of totals.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Numbers that steer, not decorate.
  • Problems stay visible.
  • Sharper questions from leaders.
  • Comparable over time as rates.
  • Honest reporting builds trust.

What we lose

  • Less impressive slides.
  • Rates can be harder to compute.
  • Some vanity numbers motivate teams (keep them separate).
  • Needs targets to read well.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Running totals

    “1 million orders!” Fix → rates per order or customer.

  • No target

    82 %: good or bad? Fix → show the target or the comparison.

  • Dropping the bad numbers

    Only rising metrics on the slide. Fix → include the ones that could show trouble.

  • Banning celebration

    No totals ever. Fix → fine for celebrating; not for steering.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Board slides, reports, health pages.
  • Choosing key results.
  • Reviewing any dashboard.
  • When numbers look great but nothing feels better.

Skip it when…

  • Team celebrations (just don't steer by them).

8Self-check quiz

What's the test for a vanity metric?

If it changed, would we do something different? If not, it's vanity.

Why are cumulative totals usually vanity?

They only go up, so they always look good and never signal a problem.

What replaced “swaps approved since April”?

Swap refund tickets per 100 orders, swaps answered, and repeat orders after a swap.

Are vanity metrics ever fine?

For celebrating, yes; not for steering.

try it

Take your last report or slide. Ask the question of every number, and replace each one that fails with a rate or share that could go down, with a target next to it.

remember!!

Not total sign-ups: who's still training after three months?
