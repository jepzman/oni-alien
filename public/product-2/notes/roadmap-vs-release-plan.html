Roadmap vs Release Plan

TL;DR

A roadmap says why and in what order: outcomes and bets, with no firm dates beyond now. A release plan says which increments ship when, with dates and a confidence level for each. They have different owners (the product owner; the delivery manager with the PO), different rhythms (monthly; weekly) and different audiences. Keep them as two documents, link them, and when people ask “when?”, send them to the release plan.

1The situation Larder, 9 March 2026

Sanne, the delivery manager for the Rotterdam launch, reads Grace's new roadmap. “Approvals” is in Now, so Sanne assumes it's done by 31 March, ready for Rotterdam on 27 April, and tells Joost so. Joost tells his store managers. Grace never said that: one document was doing two jobs, and a promise appeared that nobody had made.

  • A roadmap without dates gets dates read into it.
  • A release plan without a why gets reshuffled by whoever asks loudest.
  • Confidence levels are missing, so every date sounds certain.
meanwhile, in Ana’s notebook
  • Trade-off Sliders (Ana, vol. 2): on 31 March these sliders moved approvals in Rotterdam (R3) from launch day to June in Grace and Sanne's release plan.

2Real-life analogy the menu and tonight's service

A restaurant's menu says what it serves and why: seasonal, local, the chef's style. It changes a few times a year. The kitchen's service plan for tonight says table 4 gets mains at 20:15, table 7 is waiting on the fish. It changes every hour. Nobody hands the guests the service plan, or runs the kitchen from the menu.

3How it solves it two documents, linked

Grace and Sanne split it the same week. The roadmap stayed as it was. Sanne's release plan lists increments with dates and how sure they are: R1, a staff test at Noord on 30 March (90 %); R2, all of Amsterdam behind a flag on 13 April (80 %); R3, Rotterdam. Joost got a corrected message the same day. On 31 March, the trade-off sliders moved R3 to June, a one-line change in one document, explained by the other.

  1. Separate the documents

    Roadmap: outcomes, bets, order. Release plan: increments, dates, confidence.

  2. Name the owners

    Roadmap: the product owner. Release plan: the delivery manager, with the PO.

  3. Give every date a confidence

    90 % for this sprint, 70 % for next month; say what would change it.

  4. Link them both ways

    Each release names its roadmap item; each roadmap item links its releases.

  5. Set two rhythms

    Roadmap monthly; release plan weekly.

  6. Send “when?” to the release plan

    And “why?” to the roadmap.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • No accidental promises from a misread roadmap.
  • Dates with confidence: honest and useful.
  • Clear owners for why and for when.
  • Changes are explained: the roadmap says why a date moved.
  • Each audience gets the right document.

What we lose

  • Two documents to keep in sync.
  • Confidence levels are judgement, not science.
  • Some people read only one.
  • Weekly updates take Sanne's time.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Dates on the roadmap

    “Now = by 31 March.” Fix → dates live only in the release plan.

  • Dates without confidence

    “13 April” sounds certain. Fix → add a percentage and what it depends on.

  • Plans that don't link

    Releases with no outcome; roadmap items with no release. Fix → link both ways.

  • Big-bang releases

    One release, one date, all or nothing. Fix → plan small increments: staff test, one city, then more.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any team with a delivery manager or a fixed launch date.
  • When roadmap items are read as promises.
  • Coordinating releases across teams or cities.
  • Reporting to operations who need dates.

Skip it when…

  • Tiny teams shipping continuously with no external dates.
  • When there's one release and one date: a release plan alone is enough.

8Self-check quiz

What does a roadmap answer, and a release plan?

Roadmap: why and in what order. Release plan: what ships when, with confidence.

Who owns each?

The roadmap: the product owner. The release plan: the delivery manager, with the PO.

Why add confidence levels to dates?

So people know how sure the date is and what would change it.

Approvals in Rotterdam moved to June. Where is that recorded?

In the release plan's change log, with the reason (the trade-off sliders).

try it

Find the dates people quote from your roadmap. Move them into a separate release plan, one line per increment, with a confidence percentage and what each depends on. Ask your delivery manager (or yourself) to own it weekly, and link the two documents both ways.

The menu says why. Tonight's service plan says when, and how sure.
