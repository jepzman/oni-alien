Saying No Without Burning Bridges

TL;DR

A product owner says no far more often than yes. A good no is clear (no “maybe later” that means never), explained with the outcome and the evidence, offers what you're doing instead, often includes a small yes (a cheap test, a manual workaround), and sets when you'll look again. Say it in writing, to the person, before they hear it from someone else. A quiet “parked” comes back louder.

1The situation Larder, 24 February 2026

In November Grace parked Femke's recipe ideas on her opportunity tree. She mentioned it in a stand-up; she never told Femke clearly. At the February board meeting, an investor asks where the recipe ideas are. Femke, surprised, turns to Grace in front of the board. The no was right; the way it was said wasn't, and it cost Femke a moment of looking uninformed.

  • Quiet parking feels kinder; it just postpones the conversation, often to a worse moment.
  • “Maybe later” without a date sounds like yes to the asker.
  • A no without reasons feels like a judgement on the person, not the idea.

2Real-life analogy the good waiter

A good waiter doesn't pretend the fish is coming. “Not tonight”, clearly. “It didn't come in fresh”, a reason. “May I suggest the lamb?”, something instead. “It's back on Friday”, a date. The guest doesn't get the fish and still leaves happy, and comes back on Friday.

3How it solves it clear, explained, and with a door left open

Grace wrote to Femke the next morning, in five short paragraphs, and offered to brief the investor herself. The small yes mattered: marketing put three recipe links in the weekly newsletter for four weeks. By late March the numbers were in: 2.1 % clicked, 0.3 % added an item. Femke shared them with the investor herself. Recipes stayed parked, and nobody was surprised.

  1. Say it soon, to the person

    Before a meeting, in writing, not via the grapevine.

  2. Restate the idea fairly

    Show you understood it, in the asker's terms.

  3. Give the reason with evidence

    The outcome it doesn't serve, the data, the score.

  4. Say what you're doing instead

    The work that won, and what it'll achieve.

  5. Offer a small yes

    A cheap test, a workaround, an owner elsewhere, or a link to their goal.

  6. Set a date

    When and how you'll look again; put it in the parked list.

Vocabulary for the exam

Parked
Not now, with a reason and a review date.

Anyone who can affect, or is affected by, the product.

tools: a short written message, and a public “parked” list with reasons and review dates (a page in the product wiki, or a column on the board).

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • No surprises for senior stakeholders.
  • Focus protected without damaging relationships.
  • Ideas get a fair test through the small yes.
  • Fewer repeat requests: the reason and date are on record.
  • Trust grows: people learn your no is reasoned.

What we lose

  • Uncomfortable: writing a no to the CEO is hard.
  • Small yeses cost something: marketing's time.
  • Not every no lands well, even done right.
  • Takes time: 30 minutes per message.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • The quiet park

    Nobody tells the asker; it comes back at the board. Fix → say it to the person, in writing, soon.

  • “Maybe later”

    No date, so the asker hears yes. Fix → a clear no now, and a dated review.

  • No reason

    “It's not a priority.” Fix → the outcome and the evidence, in one or two sentences.

  • All or nothing

    Either the full feature or nothing. Fix → look for a small yes that tests the idea cheaply.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Ideas from senior stakeholders that don't fit the outcome.
  • Requests that have been parked quietly too long.
  • Before a meeting where the idea may come up.
  • Any no that will be repeated to others.

Skip it when…

  • Tiny requests: a one-line answer in triage is enough.
  • When you're actually unsure: say so and test it.

8Self-check quiz

Name the five parts of a good no.

Restate the idea, the reason with evidence, what instead, a small yes, a date to look again.

Why is quiet parking risky?

The asker doesn't know; it comes back at a worse moment, often in public.

What's a small yes?

A cheap way to serve or test part of the request, like newsletter links for four weeks.

Why put it in writing?

It's clear, can be forwarded, and the reason and date are on record.

try it

Find one idea you've parked without telling the asker clearly. Write them five short paragraphs: restate, reason, instead, a small yes, a review date. Send it before your next meeting with them.
