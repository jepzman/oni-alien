MoSCoW and Its Traps

TL;DR

MoSCoW sorts scope into Must, Should, Could and Won't (this time). It's useful for a fixed date, and it's easy to break. Make it work with three rules: a Must is only something without which the release fails or is illegal; Musts take at most about 60 % of the capacity, so there's room to absorb surprises; and the Won'ts are written down, so they stop coming back. Agree the categories with the people who'll later ask “where's my feature?”.

1The situation Larder, 3 March 2026

Sanne asks Grace for the Rotterdam app scope. Grace collects requests from Joost, Femke, Priya, Sam and the team: 19 items. Everyone marks their own as “Must”. Result: 14 Musts, about twice what eight weeks of the team's time can deliver. When things slip, nobody will know what to drop, so the team will drop whatever's last, which may be order tracking.

  • Everyone's own item is a Must; “must” stops meaning anything.
  • No slack: one surprise and the date is at risk.
  • Won'ts aren't written down, so they come back every week.
meanwhile, in Ana’s notebook
  • Decision Log (Ana, vol. 2): two weeks later Sanne asks Ana the same kind of question about the open decisions for Rotterdam.

2Real-life analogy the 10-kilo cabin bag

Packing a 10-kilo cabin bag: the passport and medicine are musts (you can't travel without them), the charger is a should, the third pair of shoes is a could, and the hairdryer is a won't, because the hotel has one. Pack the musts first, leave some space, and if the bag won't close, the shoes come out, not the passport.

3How it solves it a strict Must, room to spare, Won'ts on paper

Grace and Sanne asked one question per item: does the Rotterdam launch fail, or break the law, without it? That left six Musts, about 55 % of the capacity. Approvals in Rotterdam became a Should, which mattered later: when the trade-off sliders were agreed on 31 March, it moved to June without a fight. The four Won'ts went into the plan with one line each explaining why.

  1. List everything

    Every request for the release, with who asked for it.

  2. Apply the strict Must test

    Does the release fail, or break the law, without it? If not, it's not a Must.

  3. Check capacity

    Musts ≤ ~60 % of what the team can do. Too many? Find a smaller version of each Must.

  4. Sort the rest

    Should: painful to miss. Could: nice. Won't: not this time, with a reason.

  5. Agree with the askers

    In one session, with the people who'd escalate later.

  6. Use it when things slip

    Drop Coulds, then Shoulds; Musts are protected by the slack.

Vocabulary for the exam

Must
Without it the release fails or is illegal.
Should
Important and painful to leave out, but there's a workaround.
Could
Nice to have; first to go.
Won't (this time)
Agreed not to do now, written down with a reason.
Contingency
The slack Musts leave for surprises.
Timebox
A fixed date or period; scope flexes to fit it.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • A protected core for a fixed date.
  • Clear drop order when things slip.
  • Won'ts stop recurring once written down.
  • Simple language stakeholders already know.
  • Slack for surprises with the 60 % rule.

What we lose

  • Inflation: everything drifts back to Must.
  • Coarse: no order inside a category.
  • Political: “your item is a Could” hurts.
  • Ignores value per effort: pair with RICE or CD3.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Must inflation

    14 of 19 items are Musts. Fix → the strict test: fails or illegal without it.

  • No slack

    Musts fill 100 % of the capacity. Fix → keep Musts at about 60 %.

  • Won't = never

    People fight Won'ts as if forever. Fix → say “won't, this time”, with a reason and a review date.

  • Sorted alone

    Grace labels everything; Joost escalates in April. Fix → agree the labels with the askers in one session.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A release with a fixed date.
  • Many stakeholders, each sure their item is essential.
  • Planning what to drop when things slip.
  • Short timeboxes where scope must flex.

Skip it when…

  • Ongoing prioritisation of a backlog: use RICE or cost of delay.
  • When there's no fixed date: order by value instead.

8Self-check quiz

What's the strict test for a Must?

The release fails, or breaks the law, without it.

Why keep Musts at about 60 % of capacity?

To leave slack for surprises so the Musts are safe.

What does “Won't” mean?

Won't this time: agreed and written down with a reason, not “never”.

When things slip, what goes first?

Coulds, then Shoulds; Musts stay.

try it

Take your next fixed-date release. Apply the strict Must test to every item and count how many survive. Check the Musts against 60 % of your capacity, write four Won'ts with a one-line reason each, and agree the list with the two loudest askers.

remember!!

Passport first, space left, hairdryer stays home. Write the Won'ts down.
