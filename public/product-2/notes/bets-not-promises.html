Bets, Not Promises

TL;DR

A product team controls what it builds, not how customers respond. So present plans as bets, not promises: what you're doing, the expected effect as a range, your confidence and why, the signal that says double down, and the one that says stop or rethink. Report the number every month. A missed promise costs trust; a bet that's updated honestly earns it, even when it loses.

1The situation Larder, 14 April 2026

Approvals went live in Amsterdam on 13 April. Femke's draft slide for Thursday's board says “Refunds: 2 % by June”, the goal Grace wrote in November. Grace is worried: Larder controls the app, but not how fast customers answer, or how often items run out, and Rotterdam won't have approvals until June. If June shows 3.5 %, the board will remember a missed promise, not a halving of refunds.

  • Promises on outcomes you don't control are likely to be missed.
  • A missed promise damages trust more than an honest range ever would.
  • Without signals, nobody knows early whether the plan is working.

2Real-life analogy the weather forecast

One forecaster says “it will rain tomorrow”. Another says “70 % chance of rain”, and updates it every six hours. When tomorrow stays dry, the first is simply wrong. The second was right to say 70 %, and people keep trusting her, because she told them how sure she was and kept them posted.

3How it solves it expected effect, confidence, signals

Grace and Femke rewrote the slide together: three bets, each with an expected effect, a confidence level, and a signal. The first signal was already in: 79 % of Amsterdam customers answered within two minutes in the first three days. The board heard “3–4 % by June, 2 % by December if bets 2 and 3 work, and you'll get the number every month”, and an investor said it was the first product slide he'd believed.

  1. Separate what you control

    What you'll build and ship (control) from what customers do (influence).

  2. Write each bet

    The work, the outcome it targets, the expected effect as a range.

  3. State confidence and why

    High, medium, low, with the evidence behind it.

  4. Set two signals per bet

    Double down if…; stop or rethink if… Both measurable, both early.

  5. Give leadership a range and a date

    Not a point promise: “3–4 % by June”.

  6. Report monthly

    The number, the signals, and what you changed.

Vocabulary for the exam

Bet
Work you believe will move an outcome, with stated confidence.
Promise
A commitment to a result, as if you controlled it.
Confidence
How sure you are, and on what evidence.
Signal
An early, measurable sign a bet is working or not.
Range
An honest spread for the expected effect.
Double down
Invest more because the signal says it works.

# Substitution refunds: our bets for 2026          reported monthly to the board

Today: 6.2 % of orders end in a swap refund ticket (≈ €45k a month).
We control what we ship; we influence how customers respond.

BET 1  Approvals in the app (Amsterdam 13 Apr, Rotterdam June)    confidence HIGH
       Expect   6.2 % → ~3.5 % by end June
       Why      concierge 1.1 % vs 6.8 %; prototype with 6 customers
       Double down if ≥ 70 % answer within 2 min   (first 3 days: 79 %)
       Rethink if     < 50 %, or picking slows > 15 s per swap order
BET 2  "No swaps" flag per item (live 23 Feb)                       confidence MEDIUM
       Expect   ≈ −0.5 point · signal: used on ≥ 8 % of orders
BET 3  Live order updates (Q3)                                       confidence LOW
       Expect   fewer "where is my order?" tickets · a test first

Our message: 3–4 % by June; 2 % by December if bets 2 and 3 work.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Trust survives a miss, because the range was honest.
  • Early signals show if the plan is working.
  • Leaders understand uncertainty instead of being surprised by it.
  • Bets can change without anyone being wrong.
  • Better decisions about where to double down.

What we lose

  • Feels less decisive to some leaders.
  • Ranges can be gamed (always low, always beat it).
  • Monthly reporting takes time.
  • Investors sometimes want a single number.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Promising an outcome

    “2 % by June.” Fix → a range, a confidence level and a monthly number.

  • Sandbagging

    Always promise low and beat it. Fix → state your real best estimate; show the evidence.

  • No signals

    Wait until June to find out. Fix → two early signals per bet: double down, or rethink.

  • Silent updates

    The range changes and nobody is told. Fix → report every month, including bad news.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Reporting outcome goals to leadership, boards or investors.
  • When a past target now looks unlikely.
  • Any plan where customers' behaviour decides the result.
  • Portfolios of several initiatives.

Skip it when…

  • Pure delivery commitments you fully control (a contract date).
  • Legal or safety obligations: those are promises.

8Self-check quiz

Why not promise “2 % by June”?

Larder doesn't control how customers respond; a missed promise costs trust.

What does each bet need?

The work, the expected effect as a range, confidence and why, and signals to double down or rethink.

What was the first signal for approvals?

79 % answered within two minutes in the first three days.

How often should bets be reported?

Monthly: the number, the signals, what changed.

try it

Find one target your team has promised that depends on customers' behaviour. Rewrite it as a bet: expected range, confidence with evidence, and two signals. Share it with the person who'll be asked about it, and offer a monthly one-paragraph update.

remember!!

Say “70 % chance”, not “it will rain”. And update the forecast.
