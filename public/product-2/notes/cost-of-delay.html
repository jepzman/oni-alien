Cost of Delay

TL;DR

Every week an idea isn't live has a price: lost revenue, refunds, risk, a missed date. That's its cost of delay, per week or month. Divide it by how long the work takes and you get CD3 (cost of delay divided by duration): do the highest CD3 first, so short, valuable jobs don't wait behind long ones. Also look at the urgency profile: some items lose value steadily, and some have a fixed date after which they're worth nothing; protect those first.

1The situation Larder, 9 February 2026

RICE put approvals first, so the team planned it first: eight weeks, then the “no swaps” flag, two weeks. It looks obvious: do the most valuable thing first. But the flag would then wait eight weeks, and in those weeks allergy households (4 of 12 interviews) keep getting swaps they can't eat. Meanwhile Rotterdam's app work (zones, prices, opening hours) has to be ready for 27 April, whatever any score says.

  • Big-value-first makes small, urgent items wait behind long ones.
  • Value scores ignore time: when something ships matters as much as what.
  • Fixed dates hide in the backlog until they're suddenly late.

2Real-life analogy the supermarket queue

At the checkout, the shopper with a full trolley lets the person with one carton of milk go first. The trolley loses a minute; the milk saves fifteen. Nobody thinks the milk is more important than the week's shop; it's just that value per minute of waiting decides the order. And the person whose train leaves in five minutes? Let them through too.

3How it solves it cost of delay ÷ duration

Grace priced each item's delay roughly, from the refund numbers: approvals ≈ €30,000 a month of avoidable refunds, the flag ≈ €12,000 (refunds plus allergy customers leaving). Flag first costs about €15,000 of delayed approvals; approvals first, about €24,000 of delayed flag. The flag went live on 23 February. Rotterdam's app work was protected separately: a fixed-date item doesn't compete on CD3, it gets scheduled back from its date.

  1. Price a week or month of waiting

    Refunds, revenue, churn, cost, risk. A rough euro figure beats “high”.

  2. Name the urgency profile

    Standard (value lost steadily), fixed date (worthless if late), expedite (costs now), intangible (cheap now, costly later).

  3. Estimate the duration

    Weeks of the team's time, roughly.

  4. Compute CD3

    Cost of delay ÷ duration. Highest first.

  5. Protect fixed dates

    Schedule them back from the date with a buffer; they don't compete on CD3.

  6. Revisit when numbers change

    New evidence, a new date, a shorter design: recompute.

Vocabulary for the exam

Cost of delay
What it costs per week or month that something isn't live.
CD3
Cost of delay divided by duration: the order to do things in.
Urgency profile
How value changes with time: standard, fixed date, expedite, intangible.
Fixed date
A deadline after which the work loses most of its value.
WSJF
Weighted shortest job first: SAFe's version of CD3.
Queue cost
The total value lost by everything waiting.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Short, valuable work ships sooner.
  • Time has a price, so waiting stops looking free.
  • Fixed dates are visible and protected early.
  • Euro figures make sense to finance and leadership.
  • Less total value lost across the whole queue.

What we lose

  • Rough numbers: cost of delay is an estimate.
  • Hard for intangible items (tech debt, learning).
  • Can churn the order if recomputed too often.
  • Needs finance-style thinking some teams aren't used to.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Value first, time ignored

    The eight-week item goes first because it's worth more. Fix → divide by duration; short jobs often win.

  • Fake precision

    €29,412 a month. Fix → round numbers; the order matters, not the decimals.

  • Fixed dates scored like the rest

    Rotterdam's app work competes on CD3 and slips. Fix → schedule fixed-date items back from the date.

  • Reordering every day

    The plan changes with every new estimate. Fix → recompute at planning, or when evidence really changes.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Choosing the order of work, not just the top idea.
  • When big items keep small, urgent ones waiting.
  • Talking about priorities with finance and leadership.
  • Anything with a deadline in the mix.

Skip it when…

  • When every item is about the same size: RICE is enough.
  • Legal must-dos: they're fixed dates, schedule them.

8Self-check quiz

What is CD3?

Cost of delay divided by duration; do the highest first.

Why did the flag go before approvals?

Its CD3 was higher (24 vs 15): two weeks of work that stopped €12k a month of losses.

How do you handle a fixed-date item?

Schedule it back from the date with a buffer; don't let it compete on CD3.

Name two urgency profiles.

Standard (value lost steadily) and fixed date (worthless if late); also expedite and intangible.

try it

Take your next five backlog items. For each, write a rough euro cost per month of it not being live, and its duration in weeks. Compute CD3 and compare the order with your current plan. If anything has a fixed date, schedule it back from that date first.
