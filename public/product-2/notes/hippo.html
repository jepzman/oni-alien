Handling the HiPPO

TL;DR

The HiPPO is the Highest Paid Person's Opinion: in many rooms it wins by default. Don't fight the person; change the question from “who's right?” to “how would we know?”. Restate the idea as a hypothesis, add a guardrail metric (what mustn't get worse), run the smallest test that answers it, and read the result together. Senior people usually like evidence; what they dislike is being blocked.

  • Family: saying no
  • Origin: “HiPPO” coined by Avinash Kaushik (2007); experimentation practice at Microsoft (Ronny Kohavi)
  • Lives in: a hypothesis card and a one-week test
  • Pairs with: testable hypotheses, saying no, A/B tests

1The situation Larder, 10 March 2026

Femke comes back from a follow-up meeting with Korenwolf full of energy: Larder for Business should be a big banner at the top of the app's home screen, “so everyone sees it”. Nobody in the room objects. Dee starts building it that afternoon. Grace isn't against business customers, but the home screen is where consumer orders start, and nobody has asked what the banner costs the shop.

  • Rank decides, because disagreeing with the CEO feels risky.
  • The idea's cost (here, attention on the home screen) is invisible.
  • A flat refusal would make it a battle; quietly building it makes it a gamble.

2Real-life analogy the cockpit

Airlines learned this the hard way. In the cockpit, the first officer is trained to speak up to the captain with a standard pattern: “I'm concerned”, what the instruments say, and a proposal. It isn't insubordination; it's the job. The instruments decide, and good captains want to hear it.

3How it solves it from opinion to hypothesis

Grace went to Femke the same day: “I love that we want offices to see it. I'm worried the home screen costs us consumer orders. Can we show it to 10 % for a week and look together?” Femke agreed at once. The result: orders down 0.4 points in the banner group, about 4 % if shown to everyone, for 3 leads. Femke made the call herself: account menu, newsletter, and leads straight to Mo.

  1. Agree with the goal

    Start with what's right about it: “offices should know about this”.

  2. Name your concern

    One sentence, the specific risk: “the home screen is where orders start”.

  3. Write the hypothesis

    “A home-screen banner brings office leads without reducing orders.”

  4. Add a guardrail

    The metric that mustn't get worse (order conversion), with a threshold.

  5. Run the smallest test

    10 % of users for a week; or a fake door; or five interviews.

  6. Read it together

    Show the senior person the result first and let them decide.

Vocabulary for the exam

HiPPO
Highest Paid Person's Opinion.
Hypothesis
A testable statement: we believe X will cause Y.
Guardrail metric
A number that must not get worse during a test.
Holdout
Users who don't see the change, for comparison.
Conversion
Share of sessions that end in an order.
Speaking up
Raising a concern to a senior person, with data and a proposal.

# Test: Larder for Business banner on the home screen

Idea (Femke)   A large banner at the top of the app's home screen.
Hypothesis     The banner brings office leads without reducing consumer orders.
Guardrail      Order conversion (sessions → order) must not drop more than 0.2 pp.
Success        ≥ 10 qualified office leads in one week.
Design         10 % of app users see the banner, 90 % don't · 10–17 Mar · Dee

Result         A (no banner)   9.5 % of sessions → order
               B (banner)      9.1 % of sessions → order   (−0.4 pp: guardrail broken)
               Leads           3 (two under 40 staff)
               If shown to all: ≈ 4 % fewer orders ≈ 1,100 orders a week.

Decision       With Femke, 18 Mar: the banner moves to the account menu and the
               monthly newsletter; leads go straight to Mo. Banner code removed.

tools: a feature flag with percentage rollout (Unleash, LaunchDarkly, Statsig) and your analytics tool; for a smaller test, a fake door or five customer interviews.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • No battle: the question becomes “how would we know?”.
  • Costs become visible: here, 4 % of orders.
  • Senior people keep authority and decide on evidence.
  • Team members speak up when there's a safe pattern.
  • Good HiPPO ideas get through faster, with proof.

What we lose

  • Not every idea can be tested in a week.
  • Takes courage to raise the concern.
  • A test costs something: a week of 10 % of users.
  • Some HiPPOs don't want data: then it's an escalation.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Fighting the person

    “That's a bad idea.” Fix → agree with the goal, question the method.

  • Building it quietly

    Dee ships the banner; nobody measures. Fix → every senior idea gets a hypothesis and a guardrail.

  • No guardrail

    Leads go up; nobody checks orders. Fix → name the metric that mustn't get worse.

  • Reading results alone

    Grace presents “Femke was wrong”. Fix → read it together, let them make the call.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A senior stakeholder's idea with a hidden cost.
  • When the room goes quiet after the boss speaks.
  • Ideas that touch high-traffic screens or flows.
  • Any opinion that can be tested in a week.

Skip it when…

  • Strategic calls that are the leader's to make (which city next).
  • Legal or safety requirements: not up for testing.

8Self-check quiz

What does HiPPO stand for?

Highest Paid Person's Opinion.

How do you change the question?

From “who's right?” to “how would we know?”: a hypothesis and a test.

What's a guardrail metric?

A number that mustn't get worse during the test, like order conversion.

Why read the result together?

So the senior person decides on the evidence and keeps their authority.

try it

Think of the last idea that went ahead because a senior person liked it. Write it as a hypothesis, add one guardrail metric with a threshold, and design a one-week test. Next time a similar idea comes up, bring the card to the conversation.
