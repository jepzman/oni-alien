Outcome-Based Roadmaps

TL;DR

A roadmap of features answers what will we ship?; leaders and investors want what will change?. Put the outcome on top of each column (a measurable change, with a target), and the features underneath as current bets. Commit to the outcomes; keep the features swappable. When a feature turns out not to move the number, you change the bet, not the promise, and the roadmap survives contact with reality.

1The situation Larder, 23 March 2026

Femke's board deck has a slide titled Q2 features: approvals, the “no swaps” flag, live order updates. An investor asks the obvious question: and what will that do? Femke improvises an answer about refunds. The slide lists outputs; nothing on it says what success looks like, so the board can only judge whether features ship, not whether they work.

  • Feature lists invite “is it done?”, not “did it help?”.
  • When a feature disappoints, the roadmap has failed, even if the team learned fast.
  • Leaders can't see what the features are for.

2Real-life analogy the trip to Lyon

A list of train times (08:12, 11:40, 15:05) is a plan of outputs. The goal is “be in Lyon by Friday evening for the wedding”. If the 11:40 is cancelled, the goal makes it a detour: take the bus, or the 12:30. With only the list, a cancelled train is a failure, and you might not even remember why you were going.

3How it solves it outcomes on top, bets below

Grace rewrote the slide for Femke in an hour: three columns, an outcome with a target on top, the current bets below in dashed boxes. At the next board meeting, the investor's question was already answered on the slide, and the conversation moved to the right question: how will you know by June?

  1. Write each column's outcome

    A change in customer behaviour or a business number, with a target and a date.

  2. List the bets below

    Features and experiments you believe will move it, marked as current bets.

  3. Link to the strategy

    Each outcome should match a line in “we'll know by”.

  4. Commit to outcomes, not bets

    Say so on the slide: features may change.

  5. Report progress on the outcome

    The number first, then which bets helped.

  6. Swap bets openly

    When a bet fails, show the new one under the same outcome.

Vocabulary for the exam

Outcome
A measurable change in behaviour or results.
Output
Something shipped: a feature, a screen.
Bet
A feature or experiment expected to move the outcome.
Target
The number and date that count as success.
Leading indicator
An early signal the outcome is moving.
Impact
The business result outcomes add up to.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Leaders see what changes, not just what ships.
  • Bets can change without breaking a promise.
  • Progress reports focus on results.
  • Teams own the outcome, not a feature list.
  • Better questions from the board: how will you know?

What we lose

  • Harder to write: good outcomes take thought.
  • Some stakeholders still want feature dates.
  • Outcomes can lag: results show up after the release.
  • Needs measurement that actually works.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Outputs disguised as outcomes

    “Outcome: launch approvals.” Fix → an outcome is a measured change, not a release.

  • No target

    “Fewer refund tickets.” Fix → add a number and a date: 6.2 % → 4 % by June.

  • Bets set in stone

    Approvals are “committed” even if they fail. Fix → commit to outcomes; mark features as current bets.

  • Outcomes nobody measures

    “Customers know first”, no data. Fix → pick outcomes you can measure today.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Roadmaps for leadership, boards and investors.
  • When “is it done?” crowds out “did it work?”.
  • Teams trusted to choose their own solutions.
  • Alongside OKRs.

Skip it when…

  • Contract deliverables with fixed scope.
  • When you can't measure the outcome yet: fix measurement first.

try it

Take your current roadmap. Above each column or theme, write one outcome with a number and a date. Move the features underneath and label them “current bets”. Show it to a leader who usually asks for feature dates and note which question they ask first.
