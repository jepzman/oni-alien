Product Strategy on a Page

TL;DR

A backlog says what you'll build; a strategy says why those things and not others. Put it on one page: where we are (the honest numbers), who we serve, how we win (the few things that make customers choose you), the bets for the year, what we're not doing, and how we'll know it's working. One page is short enough to remember and to argue with, and every backlog item should point at one box.

1The situation Larder, 19 January 2026

Priya asks Grace for the ordering strategy for 2026. Grace has a lot: an opportunity tree, a north star, a baseline, 60 backlog items. But when she asks her team what are we trying to do this year?, Ben says “ship approvals”, Dee says “get the app ready for Rotterdam”, Sam says “fewer tickets”. All true; none of it adds up to a story, and the next loud request could reshuffle everything.

  • A backlog without a strategy is a list of things people asked for.
  • Each person fills the gap with their own priority.
  • Without a written “not doing”, every idea is still in play.

2Real-life analogy the coach's whiteboard

Before a match, a good coach puts the game plan on one whiteboard: where we are in the league, how we'll win (press high, use the wings), what we won't do (no long balls), and how we'll know at half-time (shots on target). Every player can repeat it. A forty-page plan would be better researched and completely useless at minute 60.

3How it solves it six boxes, one page

Grace drafted it in an afternoon from what she already had (the interviews, the tree, the north star) and spent a week arguing it with Priya, Ana and the team. The hardest box was not this year: three things people liked, written down as out. After that, every item in the Q2 plan pointed at one of the three bets, and the request triage had something to triage against.

  1. Start with the honest numbers

    Where we are: the north star, the baseline, the problem in one line.

  2. Name who you serve

    The two or three customer types you'll focus on, and who comes later.

  3. Say how you'll win

    The few things that make customers choose you; keep it to two or three.

  4. Pick the bets

    Three bets for the year, each linked to an opportunity on the tree.

  5. Write what you won't do

    Three things people would like, out for this year, with a line why.

  6. Say how you'll know

    One or two numbers with a date. Review the page every quarter.

Vocabulary for the exam

Strategy
The choices about where to focus and how to win; what you won't do.
Bet
A big piece of work you believe will move the numbers.
Not doing
Explicit choices to leave things out.
Diagnosis
Rumelt's “where we are”: the honest problem.
Guiding policy
Rumelt's “how we win”: the approach.
Coherent actions
The bets: work that follows from the policy.

# Ordering & customer apps · strategy 2026            owner: Grace · agreed with Priya

WHERE WE ARE   6.2 % of orders end in a substitution refund ticket (≈ €45k/month);
               customers with 2+ tickets order 30 % less. Perfect orders: 82 %.
               Rotterdam opens 27 Apr; six cities by end 2027.
WHO WE SERVE   Tonight-dinner parents (express, ~70 % of orders) and Sunday
               planners (slots). Offices: later, once Larder for Business is proven.
HOW WE WIN     Honest substitutions: customers decide, never surprised at the door.
               On time, every time, in every new city.
3 BETS         1. Approve or refuse substitutes in the app (2-minute window)
               2. "Never swap this" flag per item
               3. Live order updates with the substitute and its price
NOT THIS YEAR  Recipe ideas · meal kits · non-food ranges
WE'LL KNOW BY  Perfect orders 82 % → 88 % by December 2026 ·
               substitution refund tickets 6.2 % → 2 %
Review         every quarter with Priya; the next one in April.

tools: one page in the wiki, printed for the wall. Templates: Roman Pichler's Product Vision Board and Strategy Canvas, Rumelt's kernel (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions), the Playing to Win cascade.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • One story the whole team can repeat.
  • Every item points somewhere, or it's questioned.
  • Not-doing list stops ideas coming back each week.
  • Leadership alignment on one page, not a deck.
  • Quarterly review keeps it honest.

What we lose

  • Hard choices in writing: some people will be unhappy.
  • Too short for detail: link to the tree and the roadmap.
  • Can go stale without the quarterly review.
  • Tempting to fill with slogans instead of choices.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Slogans, not choices

    “Delight customers with world-class ordering.” Fix → each box must exclude something.

  • No not-doing list

    Every idea stays alive. Fix → write three things you won't do this year.

  • A deck, not a page

    Forty slides nobody rereads. Fix → one page; link details from it.

  • Written once

    January's strategy in September. Fix → review every quarter.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • The start of a year or a new product area.
  • When the team gives different answers to “what are we doing?”.
  • Before building a roadmap.
  • When requests keep reshuffling the backlog.

Skip it when…

  • A single short project with one clear goal.
  • When the company strategy already says it all for your area (rare).

8Self-check quiz

What six boxes make up the page?

Where we are, who we serve, how we win, the bets, not doing, and how we'll know.

Why is the not-doing box the hardest and most useful?

It's a real choice: written-down exclusions stop ideas coming back.

How do you test a backlog item against the strategy?

It should point at one of the bets; if not, question it.

How often should it be reviewed?

Every quarter.

try it

Write your product's strategy on one page with the six boxes, in under two hours. Ask three team members to read it and say back, in one sentence, what you're doing this year. Where their sentences differ, the page isn't clear yet.

remember!!

One whiteboard. How we win, what we won't do, how we'll know.
