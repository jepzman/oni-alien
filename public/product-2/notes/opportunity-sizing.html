Opportunity Sizing

TL;DR

A top-down number (“a €50 million market”) is true and almost useless: it's about everyone, not your customers. Size bottom-up instead: count the customers you could actually serve (where you deliver, the size you fit), multiply by what each would spend, then estimate what share you could realistically win by a date. Check it against real signals (a fake door, a first client), compare it with what you already have, and let the size decide the next step: an experiment, a pilot or a team.

1The situation Larder, 9 February 2026

At the January trade fair Femke met Korenwolf, and came back with a report: office catering in the Netherlands is a €50 million market. Larder for Business, she says, deserves its own team. Priya asks Grace a sharper question: how big is it for us? The €50 million counts every office in the country, including ones Larder can't reach and ones that would never buy pantry restocks. It can't tell anyone what to do next.

  • Top-down numbers are big, true and not about your customers.
  • Without a realistic share and a date, every market looks worth a team.
  • Real signals (the fake door, one client) sit unused next to the report.

2Real-life analogy opening a bakery

Someone opening a bakery could quote the €2 billion Dutch bread market. A good baker counts instead: 2,000 homes within a ten-minute walk, 60 % buy fresh bread, three times a week, €3 a time: about €560,000 a year if they won every one. Then: how many could we really win, from the bakery on the corner? That number decides whether to sign the lease.

3How it solves it count, multiply, check, compare

Grace built the sum with Joost's zone maps and a public company register: 1,500 offices of 50+ staff in Amsterdam and Rotterdam zones, roughly €43M a year if Larder won them all, and about €0.9M at a realistic 2 % by end 2027. The checks agreed: the fake door found 9 offices in two weeks, and Korenwolf alone would be €0.6M. Against €55M of consumer orders, the answer was clear: a pilot, not a team, and a pilot with Korenwolf was agreed for May.

  1. Define who you could serve

    The segment you can reach and fit: here, offices of 50+ staff inside delivery zones.

  2. Count them

    From registers, maps, your own data. A range is fine.

  3. Multiply by spend

    Per customer per period, from interviews, a first client or comparable products.

  4. Estimate a realistic share by a date

    What you could win, given your team and channels: 1–5 % is often honest.

  5. Check against real signals

    Fake door responses, a first client, inbound requests.

  6. Compare and decide the step

    Against what you already earn: experiment, pilot or team?

Vocabulary for the exam

Top-down
Starting from a whole-market number and slicing it.
Bottom-up
Counting reachable customers and multiplying by spend.
TAM / SAM / SOM
Total, serviceable and obtainable market.
Segment
A group of customers you could serve the same way.
Realistic share
What you could win by a date, honestly.
Fermi estimate
A rough calculation from a few reasonable guesses.

tools: a spreadsheet; public company registers (the Dutch KvK), your delivery-zone maps, a fake door or landing page for signals. Fermi-style estimation with ranges beats false precision.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • A number about your customers, not everyone's.
  • The next step falls out: experiment, pilot or team.
  • Assumptions are visible and can be checked.
  • Real signals get used: the fake door, a first client.
  • Calm conversations with enthusiastic founders.

What we lose

  • Rough: each number is a guess.
  • Can undersell a market that grows fast.
  • Takes a day of digging.
  • Disappointing to people who loved the big number.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Top-down only

    “1 % of a €50M market.” Fix → count reachable customers bottom-up.

  • Unrealistic share

    “We'll win 20 % by next year.” Fix → use an honest share for your team and channels.

  • No checks

    The sum is never compared with any signal. Fix → check against a fake door, a first client, inbound asks.

  • Size decides everything

    Big number, so build a team. Fix → let the size pick the next step: test, pilot or team.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • New segments or markets someone is excited about.
  • Before staffing a new team or product line.
  • When a top-down report is driving the conversation.
  • Business cases that need a revenue line.

Skip it when…

  • Small improvements to an existing product: use cost of delay.
  • When a cheap test would answer faster than a sizing.

try it

Pick a segment someone in your company is excited about. Count the customers you could actually reach, multiply by a spend you can defend, and take an honest share by a date. Check it against one real signal, then write one line: experiment, pilot or team?
