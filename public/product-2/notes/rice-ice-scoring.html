RICE & ICE Scoring

TL;DR

When every idea has a sponsor, score them the same way. RICE = Reach (how many customers or orders per quarter) × Impact (how much for each, on a fixed scale) × Confidence (how sure you are, from evidence) ÷ Effort (person-weeks). ICE drops reach for a quicker first pass. The number doesn't decide; it makes the assumptions visible, so people argue about reach and confidence instead of about whose idea it is. Score only ideas that serve the same outcome.

1The situation Larder, 2 February 2026

Q2 planning. Grace's wall has 23 idea cards: her own branch of the opportunity tree, Sam's auto-refund button, Joost's faster picking screen, Femke's recipe ideas, and more. Each has a sponsor who can explain why it matters. Then Priya asks the simple question: why substitution approvals before Joost's picking screen? Grace believes she's right, but her honest answer is a feeling, and a feeling loses to a sponsor.

  • Ideas are compared by how persuasive their sponsors are.
  • Big ideas with no evidence sit next to small ideas with lots, and look equal.
  • Nobody can say what would change the order.

2Real-life analogy the grant committee

A charity with one budget and fifty proposals asks the same four questions of each: how many people does it reach, how much does it help each, how sure are we it works, and what does it cost? People helped per euro isn't the final word (a committee still talks), but the village wells don't lose to the gala dinner just because the gala has a better speaker.

3How it solves it same four questions for every idea

Approvals came out top, with evidence behind every number (the concierge test, the prototype). The surprise was the picking screen in second place: huge reach, weak evidence. It serves a different outcome (picking speed, Joost's), so Grace didn't fight it: she took the score to Joost and Priya, and Joost ran his own interviews with pickers.

  1. Fix the scales first

    Write down what impact 3, 2, 1, 0.5 and 0.25 mean, and what earns 80 % confidence, before scoring.

  2. Use one outcome per table

    Score the ideas that compete for the same outcome; send others to their owners.

  3. Reach from data

    Orders or customers per quarter: 21 % of orders have a swap → 76,000 a quarter.

  4. Confidence from evidence

    A test or data → 80 %. Interviews → 50 %. Someone's hunch → 20 %. No exceptions for seniority.

  5. Effort from the team

    Person-weeks from the engineers who'll build it, rough is fine.

  6. Score, then discuss

    Look for surprises; change a number only with new evidence, and write down why.

Vocabulary for the exam

RICE
Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort.
ICE
Impact × Confidence × Ease: a quicker, rougher pass.
Reach
How many customers or orders an idea touches in a period.
Impact scale
A fixed scale (3 to 0.25) so “high” means the same for everyone.
Confidence
How much evidence backs the other numbers.
Person-week
One person working one week: the unit of effort.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Arguments about numbers, not about sponsors.
  • Evidence counts: confidence rewards tested ideas.
  • Small, sure ideas surface next to big, vague ones.
  • Surprises show up: the picking screen's reach.
  • Repeatable: next quarter's table uses the same scales.

What we lose

  • False precision: 15,200 looks exact; it's a guess.
  • Gameable: impact 3 for your own idea.
  • Misses timing: a deadline doesn't show (see cost of delay).
  • Mixes outcomes badly if you score everything together.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Scales made up on the spot

    Impact 3 for every idea of your own. Fix → write the scales first; score others' ideas before your own.

  • Confidence by rank

    The CEO's idea gets 80 %. Fix → confidence comes only from evidence: tests, data, interviews.

  • One table for everything

    Picking speed and refunds compete on one list. Fix → score ideas per outcome; send others to their owners.

  • Obeying the number

    15,200 beats 15,100, so that's decided. Fix → close scores mean discuss; the table starts the conversation.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Quarterly planning with many competing ideas.
  • When a sponsor's enthusiasm is winning the argument.
  • Comparing ideas for the same outcome.
  • Showing stakeholders why their idea is where it is.

Skip it when…

  • Deadlines and legal must-dos: they're not optional (see cost of delay).
  • Two or three ideas: just discuss them.
  • Ideas for different outcomes: decide the outcome first.

8Self-check quiz

What do the four letters of RICE stand for?

Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort: R × I × C ÷ E.

Where should confidence come from?

Evidence: tests and data score high, interviews medium, hunches low, whoever had the idea.

Why score one outcome at a time?

Ideas for different outcomes aren't really competing; the outcome choice comes first.

Two ideas score 15,200 and 15,100. What next?

Discuss them: the scores are too rough to separate them.

try it

Take the top ten ideas on your backlog that serve one outcome. Write your impact and confidence scales on top of a sheet, then score all ten in 45 minutes with an engineer. Circle the biggest surprise and take it to whoever owns that idea.

remember!!

Same four questions for every idea. The number starts the argument; evidence ends it.
