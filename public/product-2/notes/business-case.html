Business Case One-Pager

TL;DR

People who approve money read one page. Put it all there: the problem in euros, the benefit (with how you estimated it), the cost to build and to run, the payback, the evidence behind the numbers, the biggest risks, and the ask: how much, from whom, until when. Round the numbers, show the sums, and link the details. If it doesn't fit on one page, you don't understand it well enough yet.

1The situation Larder, 12 February 2026

Approvals need eight person-weeks of Ben's and Dee's teams, which Priya can approve, but Marco, the CFO, wants to see the case. Grace writes a thorough one: nine pages of interviews, opportunity trees, concierge results and screen designs. Marco reads page one and asks two questions: what do we get, and what does it cost? Both answers were on page seven.

  • Long cases hide the numbers decision-makers need.
  • Benefits without sums look like hope; costs without running costs look like tricks.
  • The ask (how much, from whom, until when) is often missing entirely.

2Real-life analogy the solar-panel quote

A good solar-panel quote fits on one sheet: it costs €6,000, saves about €900 a year, pays back in about seven years, and the one big risk is the neighbour's tree shading the roof in winter. The installer has forty pages of specifications, but you can decide from the sheet, and ask for page 23 if you care.

3How it solves it seven parts, one page

Grace rewrote it in an hour. The benefit came from the baseline: €45k a month of substitution refunds, and approvals targeting the two-thirds where customers weren't asked. The cost included the running cost pickers would feel (about 10 seconds per swap order, after the prototype fix). Marco read it to the end, asked one question about the risk, and it was approved the same afternoon.

  1. State the problem in euros

    From the baseline: what it costs today, per month.

  2. Estimate the benefit with its sum

    Which part of the problem this removes, and why you think so.

  3. Cost it fully

    Build cost (person-weeks × rate) and running cost (people's time, fees).

  4. Compute payback

    Cost ÷ monthly net benefit. Round it.

  5. Name evidence and the top risks

    What the numbers rest on; the two or three things that could break them.

  6. End with the ask

    How much, from whom, until when, and who decides.

Vocabulary for the exam

Business case
The argument for spending money or time, with numbers.
Benefit
Money saved or earned, or risk avoided, per period.
Running cost
What it costs every month once it's live.
Payback period
How long until the benefit covers the cost.
Loaded rate
The full cost of a person-week, including overheads.
The ask
The specific resources and decision you need.

# Business case: substitution approvals in the app        Grace · 12 Feb 2026

Problem    Substitution refunds cost ≈ €45k a month (refunds + support time).
           Customers with 2+ refund tickets order 30 % less the next month.
Benefit    Approvals target the ~2/3 of swap refunds where nobody asked:
           ≈ €30k a month, plus customers keeping their order frequency (not counted).
Cost       Build: 8 person-weeks × €6k ≈ €48k.
           Running: ~10 s extra picking per swap order × ~25k a month ≈ €1.8k a month.
Payback    €48k ÷ (€30k − €1.8k) ≈ 2 months.
Evidence   Concierge test at Noord (Dec): swap refund tickets 1.1 % vs 6.8 % at Zuid;
           81 % answered within 2 minutes. Prototype: 6 customers, 3 pickers.
Risks      Answer rate below 70 % at scale · picker slowdown at peak ·
           web orders (~20 %) can't approve until the web shop follows.
Ask        8 person-weeks of Ben's and Dee's teams, Feb–Mar. Decision: Priya,
           Marco consulted. Release plan: Noord staff test → Amsterdam → Rotterdam.

tools: a page in the wiki or a document; a spreadsheet behind it for the sums. Amazon-style six-page memos are for bigger decisions; for a team-sized ask, one page is right.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Decisions in an afternoon, not after three meetings.
  • The numbers are visible and can be challenged.
  • Running costs included, so no surprises later.
  • Finance trusts product when cases are honest.
  • Forces clear thinking: one page needs understanding.

What we lose

  • Rounding hides detail some people want.
  • Estimates can be wrong; the page makes them look firm.
  • Hard to fit uncertain benefits (retention).
  • Writing short takes longer than writing long.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Burying the numbers

    The benefit is on page seven. Fix → problem, benefit, cost, payback first.

  • Build cost only

    No running cost. Fix → add what it costs every month once live.

  • Benefits without sums

    “Customers will be happier.” Fix → show the sum, or mark it as not counted.

  • No ask

    A case that never says what it needs. Fix → end with how much, from whom, until when.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any ask for money, people or weeks that needs approval.
  • When finance or leadership must say yes.
  • Comparing two investments.
  • Before a big bet on the roadmap.

Skip it when…

  • Small work inside the team's agreed capacity.
  • Experiments costing days: a hypothesis card is enough.

8Self-check quiz

What are the seven parts of the one-pager?

Problem, benefit, cost, payback, evidence, risks and the ask.

Why include running cost?

It's paid every month once live; leaving it out makes the case look better than it is.

How is payback calculated?

Cost divided by monthly net benefit (benefit minus running cost).

What must the ask say?

How much, from whom, until when, and who decides.

try it

Take the next thing you need approval for. Write it on one page with the seven parts, rounding every number and showing each sum. Give it to someone from finance and ask them to tell you, in one sentence, what you're asking for and why.

remember!!

A quote, not a brochure: cost, savings, payback, the one big risk, the ask.
