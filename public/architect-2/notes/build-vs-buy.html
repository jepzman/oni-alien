Build vs Buy vs Open Source

TL;DR

For every capability, ask first: does this make customers choose us? If yes, it's core: build it and own it. If not, it's context: buy a good product if one exists. Open source is a third option: no licence fee, but you run, upgrade and secure it yourself. Compare the options on total cost over three years (not the first sprint), on fit with your constraints, and on how you'd leave. Most of the iceberg is under the water.

1The situation Larder, 8 June 2026

Korenwolf's pilot is going well, and Mo hopes for a three-office contract in September. But the May invoice took Mo and Marco six hours in a spreadsheet and had two VAT errors (the pantry goods at 9 %, the coffee-machine rental at 21 %). Ruben, Korenwolf's procurement lead, already wants invoices per office and per cost centre. Ben offers to build an invoicing module “in two sprints”. Ana has seen this before: invoicing is an iceberg.

  • Building looks cheap because only the first version is estimated.
  • Credit notes, numbering rules, reminders and Belgian e-invoicing (needed for Antwerp) are under the water.
  • Every engineer-week on invoicing is a week not spent on what makes Larder win.

2Real-life analogy the restaurant

A good restaurant bakes its own bread: it's what guests remember. It buys the napkins: nobody comes for the napkins, and a supplier makes them better and cheaper. And it might use a classic recipe from a book: the recipe is free, but the kitchen still has to cook it, every night.

In real lifeOn the project
The bread guests come forCore: substitution approvals, courier assignment, city configuration

3How it solves it core or context, then cost over three years

Invoicing doesn't make anyone choose Larder; on-time express and honest substitutions do. Good billing products exist, EU-hosted, with Dutch and Belgian VAT and e-invoicing built in. So buy (ADR 0013): Larder keeps the data about what was delivered and sends it to the billing service; the service owns invoices, VAT and reminders.

  1. Ask the core question

    Would customers notice, and choose us for it? If yes, it's core: lean towards building.

  2. Look for a good product

    For context: is there a product that fits the constraints (EU data, Belgian VAT, an API)?

  3. Cost three years, all options

    Build: first version + upkeep + the people. Buy: fees + integration. Open source: hosting + upgrades + security.

  4. Check fit and lock-in

    Constraints, data ownership, the API, the contract term, and how you'd leave.

  5. Decide the seam

    What you keep (the facts of the business) and what the product owns (invoices, VAT).

  6. Record it, with an exit

    ADR with the cost table; an exit plan before signing.

Vocabulary for the exam

Core
What makes customers choose you: worth building and owning.
Context
Necessary, but not why customers choose you: buy it.
Total cost of ownership
All the costs over the life of a choice, not just the start.
Open source
Free to use and change; you still run, upgrade and secure it.
Seam
The boundary between what you own and what the product owns.
Lock-in
How costly it is to leave a product later.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Engineers on what matters: the core, not invoices.
  • Honest costs: three years, not two sprints.
  • Expertise bought in: the vendor tracks VAT and e-invoicing rules.
  • Faster: ready for Korenwolf's contract in September.
  • A clear seam: Larder owns its business facts.

What we lose

  • Fees forever, rising with volume.
  • Lock-in: the vendor's roadmap and prices.
  • Less control over features and outages.
  • Integration work still lands on the team.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Estimating the tip

    “Two sprints” for invoicing. Fix → estimate upkeep and the rules under the water; cost three years.

  • Buying the core

    Outsourcing what makes customers choose you. Fix → build the differentiator, even if a product exists.

  • “Open source is free”

    No licence, but nobody budgeted for upgrades. Fix → cost hosting, upgrades and security like any other option.

  • No seam

    The vendor becomes the source of truth for your business facts. Fix → keep your own data; send the vendor what it needs.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any new capability that a product might cover.
  • When an engineer says “we can build that in two sprints”.
  • Before signing a multi-year vendor contract.
  • Reviewing whether old in-house tools are still core.

Skip it when…

  • Tiny utilities: a library choice, not a build-vs-buy.
  • When a constraint rules out all but one option.

8Self-check quiz

What's the first question to ask?

Does this make customers choose us? If yes it's core: build. If not, it's context: buy if a good product exists.

Why compare over three years?

Building looks cheap at the start; upkeep and changing rules dominate later.

What does open source cost?

No licence fee, but hosting, upgrades, security and expertise are yours.

When buying, what should you keep?

Your own business facts (here: what was delivered), sent to the product, plus an exit plan.

try it
