Architecture Decision Records

TL;DR

For every decision that is significant and hard to reverse, write a one-page record: the context, the options you considered, the decision, and its consequences, good and bad. Number it, review it like code, keep it in the repository next to what it explains, and never rewrite it: when you change your mind, write a new record that supersedes the old one. Months later, “why did we do this?” has an answer.

1The situation Larder, month eight

Larder chose Postgres with a transactional outbox to send orders to delivery, not direct Kafka writes. Eight months later a new engineer asks why. The people who decided have moved on, the chat thread is gone, and the wiki says only “we use the outbox”. So the team argues it all again for two weeks, and nearly undoes it.

  • Decisions live in meetings, chats and people's heads, all of which disappear.
  • Without the reasons, every decision looks arbitrary, so it gets re-litigated.
  • Or the opposite: nobody dares change anything, because maybe there was a reason.

2Real-life analogy the court judgement

A court doesn't just announce a verdict. A judgement records the facts, the arguments considered and the reasoning, then the verdict. It's numbered and never rewritten. A later court can overrule it, but it does so on purpose, in a new judgement that says why.

  • The judgement is the ADR; its number is the ADR number.
  • Arguments considered are the options; the reasoning is the decision drivers.
  • Overruling with a new judgement is superseding.

3How it solves it one decision, one page, kept forever

An ADR is short on purpose: one page someone can read in five minutes. The value isn't the document; it's that the reasoning survives the people and the meeting.

  1. Spot a significant decision

    Hard or costly to reverse, affects several teams, or will surprise a newcomer: datastores, integration style, build vs buy, a major library.

  2. Write it as a pull request

    Copy the template into docs/adr/NNNN-short-title.md, status proposed.

  3. Name the options honestly

    At least two real options, including “do nothing”, each with what it costs.

  4. Write the consequences, both kinds

    What gets easier, what gets harder, what we now have to do (and who).

  5. Review with the people affected

    Comments on the PR are the discussion; merge = accepted.

  6. Never edit, supersede

    Changing your mind? A new ADR that links to the old one; mark the old one superseded by NNNN.

Vocabulary for the exam

ADR
Architecture Decision Record: one decision, one short file.
Architecturally significant
Costly to reverse or wide in its effect.
Decision drivers
The forces that decided it (cost, deadline, skills, risk).
Status
Proposed, accepted, deprecated or superseded.
Supersede
Replace an ADR with a newer one, keeping the old on file.
Decision log
The numbered list of all ADRs, the index.
docs/adr/0007-orders-to-delivery-via-outbox.mdNygard-style template, filled in
# 7. Send orders to delivery via a transactional outbox

Status:   Accepted · 2026-03-14 · deciders: Ana (architect), Ben (orders), Cho (payments)

## Context
Orders must reach the delivery service exactly once. Payment and the order row
are written in one Postgres transaction. We run one Kafka cluster, with no
transactional producer experience in the team. Launch in Rotterdam is in 6 weeks.

## Options
1. Write to Kafka directly from the order service
   + simple
   – a crash between the DB commit and the publish loses the order
2. Transactional outbox in Postgres + a relay to Kafka
   + no lost orders
   – one more table and a relay to run
3. Change data capture (Debezium) on the orders table
   + no app changes
   – new infrastructure, and our table becomes a public contract

## Decision
Option 2. The order, the payment and the outbox row commit together.

## Consequences
+ No order is lost or sent twice (consumers dedupe on order_id).
+ Works with the skills we have; no new infrastructure before launch.
– Delivery sees orders up to ~2 s later (relay polling).
– We own the relay. Revisit if order volume passes 200/s (then consider option 3).

tools: plain Markdown in the repo is enough. adr-tools and Log4brains (CLI + a browsable site), MADR (a richer template), Backstage ADR plugin, Structurizr (ADRs next to C4 diagrams), joelparkerhenderson/architecture-decision-record (template collection).

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • The reasoning survives people leaving and chats expiring.
  • Faster onboarding: newcomers read the why, not just the what.
  • Fewer re-arguments: revisit on purpose, with the original reasons.
  • Better decisions: writing options down exposes weak reasoning.
  • Reviewed like code: the people affected get a say.

What we lose

  • Writing time: 30–60 minutes per decision.
  • Easy to over-do: an ADR for every library bump.
  • Easy to forget: records only help if they're written at the time.
  • Not a design doc: too short for detailed designs (link an RFC).

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Recording only the winner

    The ADR says “we chose X” with no alternatives. Fix → list at least two real options and why each lost.

  • Editing old records

    The 2026 decision is quietly rewritten in 2027. Fix → never edit; supersede with a new ADR and link both ways.

  • Writing them afterwards

    Six months later someone reconstructs the reasons from memory. Fix → write it as part of the decision, as a PR.

  • An ADR for everything

    Records for library bumps and naming choices bury the important ones. Fix → only decisions that are costly to reverse or wide in effect.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Choosing datastores, integration styles, platforms or vendors.
  • Decisions that affect several teams.
  • Anything a newcomer would ask “why?” about.
  • Regulated environments that need an audit trail of design choices.

Skip it when…

  • Easily reversible, local choices (a helper library, a naming convention).
  • Detailed designs: write an RFC or design doc, and an ADR for its key decision.
  • Throwaway prototypes.

8Self-check quiz

What makes a decision worth an ADR?

It's significant: costly or hard to reverse, affects several teams, or would puzzle a newcomer.

What are the four core parts of an ADR?

Context, options considered, the decision, and its consequences.

You've changed your mind about ADR 0007. What do you do?

Write a new ADR that supersedes it, and mark 0007 as superseded with a link; never edit the original.

Why keep ADRs in the repository?

They sit next to the code they explain, are reviewed like code, and are versioned with it.

try it

Pick one decision your team made in the last three months that a new joiner would question. Write its ADR in under 45 minutes using the template above, and open it as a pull request. Ask one person who was not in the original discussion whether the reasoning makes sense to them.

remember!!

Write the judgement, not just the verdict. Never rewrite it; overrule it with a new one.
