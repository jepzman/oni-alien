Trade-off Sliders

TL;DR

Qualities collide: date against scope, cost against reliability, speed against polish. Decide in advance which gives way. List the four to six things people care about as sliders, and put each on a different notch, with the business in the room: one is fixed, one is most flexible, no ties. Then every later trade-off has an answer that doesn't need a meeting: when two collide, the higher slider wins.

1The situation Larder, 31 March 2026

Four weeks before Rotterdam opens, the planning meeting stalls. Femke says the date is non-negotiable. Joost says reliability is. Grace wants substitution approvals live in Rotterdam from day one. Marco wants no extra cost. Dee wants the internal tools polished. Sanne's plan can't satisfy all five, and every collision is escalated to Femke, one at a time.

  • Everything is priority one, so nothing is.
  • Each trade-off becomes a fresh negotiation, often with whoever shouts loudest.
  • Teams make the call alone, differently from each other.
meanwhile, in Grace’s notebook
  • Roadmap vs Release Plan (Grace, vol. 2): Grace and Sanne's release plan moved approvals in Rotterdam (R3) from 27 April to June after this meeting.

2Real-life analogy the mixing desk

A sound engineer can't push every fader to the top: the mix just gets loud and distorts. A good mix puts the singer on top, and every other instrument gets its own, lower place. The engineer decides that before the concert, not during each song.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the project
A faderA slider: one thing people care about
The singer on topThe fixed slider: Rotterdam's date
Each instrument at its own levelEach slider on its own notch, no ties
Distortion from everything at maxPlans that can't be met, escalations
Set before the concertAgreed once, with the business, before the trade-offs come

3How it solves it one notch each, agreed with the business

The sliders worked within a week. When Rotterdam's store setup needed three values hard-coded to make the date, nobody escalated: the date slider is above “city as configuration”, so the team did it and logged it as debt. Substitution approvals went to Amsterdam first, as agreed.

  1. List what people care about

    Four to six things: date, scope, quality attributes, cost, polish. Use their words.

  2. Get the business in the room

    The people who'd escalate later must agree now: here, Femke, Joost, Priya, Sanne.

  3. One notch each

    Rank them: one fixed, one most flexible, no ties. Arguments here are the point.

  4. Say what “give” means

    For each lower slider, what may give: “approvals: Amsterdam first”, “hard-coding, logged as debt”.

  5. Publish it

    On the project one-pager, next to the drivers. Read it at planning.

  6. Re-rank when the world changes

    After launch, the date slider drops; reliability and cost rise.

Vocabulary for the exam

Trade-off slider
One concern, placed on a scale from “holds” to “gives”.
Fixed
The slider that doesn't move: everything else bends around it.
Give
What may slip, shrink or wait when sliders collide.
Iron triangle
The old trio: scope, time, cost (quality in the middle).
Escalation
Sending a trade-off up because nobody may decide it.
Re-ranking
Moving sliders on purpose when the situation changes.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Fewer escalations: most trade-offs answer themselves.
  • Shared priorities agreed by the people who'd argue later.
  • Faster team decisions that match each other.
  • Honest plans: something is allowed to give.
  • Debt is deliberate, because “give” is defined.

What we lose

  • An awkward meeting: ranking forces disagreements out.
  • Too coarse for subtle trade-offs inside one slider.
  • Goes stale if not re-ranked after launch.
  • Can be ignored by a senior person in a hurry.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Ties

    Two sliders on 5. Fix → one notch each; the argument about which goes higher is the value.

  • Only engineers in the room

    The business re-ranks it in the first escalation. Fix → the people who'd overrule it must agree it.

  • No meaning for “give”

    “Scope gives” means nothing at 17:00 on a Friday. Fix → write what giving looks like for each slider.

  • Never re-ranked

    The date slider still rules three months after launch. Fix → set a review date when you agree them.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A launch or project with a fixed date.
  • When the same trade-off keeps escalating.
  • Kick-offs, next to the business drivers.
  • When teams make conflicting shortcuts.

Skip it when…

  • Purely technical choices inside one team.
  • When one driver dwarfs all others and everyone already knows it.

8Self-check quiz

Why must every slider sit on a different notch?

Ties hide the real priority; the next collision gets escalated instead of answered.

Who must agree the sliders?

The people who'd overrule them later, usually the business sponsors, with the team.

Rotterdam needs three values hard-coded to make the date. What do the sliders say?

The date is above “city as configuration”, so hard-code them and log the debt.

When should sliders be re-ranked?

When the situation changes, e.g. after the launch date has passed.

try it

Write five things your stakeholders care about on your current project. Rank them yourself, one per notch, then ask your sponsor to rank them without seeing yours. Compare the two in a 20-minute conversation and publish the agreed version on the project page.

remember!!

Not every fader at the top. One notch each, and the singer on top.
