Technical Debt Register

TL;DR

Shortcuts are often the right call: Rotterdam's date beat “configuration, not code”, on purpose. What turns a shortcut into trouble is forgetting it. Keep a technical debt register: one line per known shortcut, with why it was taken, its interest (what it costs every month), its principal (the effort to fix), an owner and a payback trigger. Review it monthly, reserve a fixed share of each sprint, and pay the highest interest first; some debt you leave on purpose.

1The situation Larder, 15 June 2026

Rotterdam opened on time. To make the date, the team took six shortcuts, each agreed with a shrug and “we'll fix it after launch”: three Rotterdam values hard-coded, Korenwolf's orders entered by hand, Dispatch's 30-second loop, picker sync conflicts fixed by hand, and more. Three weeks later, preparing the next quarter, Ana asks which shortcuts matter most. Nobody knows. They're in TODO comments, sticky notes and memories, with no cost attached.

  • Debt you don't write down compounds silently.
  • Without interest figures, debt loses every priority fight against features.
  • Engineers fix what annoys them, not what costs most.
meanwhile, in Grace’s notebook
  • Talking Tech Debt with Engineers (Grace, vol. 3): A week earlier Grace and Ben agreed 20 % of each sprint for debt, after translating it into refund tickets and launch dates.

2Real-life analogy the household's loans

Most households carry debt, and that's fine. A mortgage is planned and worth it. A credit card is convenient and expensive: pay it first. The money you owe a friend is the dangerous one, because nobody wrote it down and it costs trust. A sensible household keeps one list, knows each interest rate, and pays the most expensive first.

3How it solves it interest, principal, a trigger

Plotting the six items made the order obvious. TD-1 (Rotterdam hard-coding) and TD-5 (picker sync) were cheap to fix and costly to keep: paid in July. TD-3 (the dispatch loop) was already planned with Engine A. TD-4 stays: the interest is tiny and ADR 0007 accepted it. TD-2 waits for its trigger.

  1. Collect the shortcuts

    Ask the teams, search TODOs, read the sliders and ADRs: “what did we accept to make a date?”

  2. One line each

    What, why it was taken (deliberate or accidental), where, linked ADR or ticket.

  3. Put a price on it

    Interest: hours, incidents or delay per month. Principal: effort to fix, in days.

  4. Give it an owner and a trigger

    “Before Utrecht prep starts”, “if a second office signs”, “if incidents > 2/month”.

  5. Budget for it

    A fixed share of each sprint (Larder: 20 %), highest interest first.

  6. Review monthly

    Update interest, close paid items, add new ones; decide to leave some on purpose.

Vocabulary for the exam

Technical debt
A shortcut that makes later change slower or riskier.
Interest
The ongoing cost of keeping the debt, per month.
Principal
The effort to remove it.
Deliberate debt
Taken knowingly, for a reason, with a plan.
Payback trigger
The event or date when it must be paid.
Debt quadrant
Fowler: deliberate or inadvertent, prudent or reckless.

tools: a Markdown table in the repo, or tickets with a “debt” label and fields for interest and principal. Code-level signals help find debt (static analysis, change-frequency hotspots with CodeScene), but the register is about decisions, not lint warnings.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Shortcuts stay visible after the launch rush.
  • Priced debt can win priority fights against features.
  • Highest interest first, not loudest annoyance.
  • Deliberate shortcuts become safe: they're tracked.
  • Some debt is left on purpose, with a reason.

What we lose

  • Interest is a guess for many items.
  • Monthly upkeep or it goes stale.
  • Can become a wish list of every refactor.
  • 20 % is a negotiation with product, every quarter.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • No price

    A list of complaints with no interest figures. Fix → estimate interest per month, even roughly.

  • Everything is debt

    Every wished-for refactor goes on the list. Fix → only shortcuts that cost something now or block something soon.

  • No budget

    Debt waits for a quiet quarter that never comes. Fix → a fixed share of every sprint.

  • Paying the wrong debt

    The fun refactor beats the expensive one. Fix → pay highest interest first; leave low-interest debt on purpose.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • After any launch where shortcuts were taken.
  • When “we'll fix it later” is heard in planning.
  • When product and engineering argue about refactoring time.
  • Before choosing what to fix in a quarter.

Skip it when…

  • Prototypes and spikes: their code is thrown away.
  • Code style issues: that's what linters are for.

8Self-check quiz

What's the difference between interest and principal?

Interest is what keeping the debt costs each month; principal is the effort to remove it.

Which debt should be paid first?

The highest interest relative to its principal.

Is all technical debt bad?

No: deliberate, prudent debt with a plan can be the right call; some low-interest debt is left on purpose.

What does every line need besides a description?

Why it was taken, interest, principal, an owner and a payback trigger.

try it

Ask your team for every shortcut taken in the last three months. Write each as a line with interest per month, principal in days, an owner and a trigger. Plot them on interest vs principal, and bring the top-left two to your next planning meeting with their monthly cost.

remember!!

Debt is fine; unwritten debt isn't. Pay the highest interest first.
