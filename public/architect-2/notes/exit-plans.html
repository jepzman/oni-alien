Exit Plans & Reversibility

TL;DR

Every vendor, platform and big dependency is a door that's easier to walk through than to leave. Before signing, and at least once a year after, write an exit plan: what you depend on, the data you'd need back and in what format, the alternatives, the effort and lead time to leave, the early warning signs, and what you do now to keep leaving cheap (an adapter in one place, regular exports, contract clauses). You hope never to use it; having it is what gives you choices and leverage.

1The situation Larder, 6 July 2026

A letter from StockPilot: from the renewal in July 2027, prices are per store. For the twelve stores Larder plans by the end of 2027 that's about three times today's bill. The contract ends in June 2027 with six months' notice, so the decision is due in December. Marco asks Ana: can we leave? Nobody can say what depends on StockPilot, how long leaving would take, or what data Larder would get back. Without an exit, there's no negotiation, only a price.

  • Lock-in is invisible until the price goes up or the vendor goes down.
  • Leaving often takes longer than the notice period.
  • The cheapest time to plan an exit is before you need one.

2Real-life analogy the theatre's emergency exits

No theatre plans for the audience to leave by the emergency exits. But the exits are marked, kept unlocked, and drilled. A blocked exit costs nothing on a normal night, and everything on the night it's needed. Checking them is part of running a theatre, not a sign you expect a fire.

3How it solves it keep the way out short

The good news was in an old decision: ADR 0005 put a stock cache and the only StockPilot adapter inside Larder's Stock module. No other module talks to StockPilot. Leaving means replacing one adapter, not rewriting Ordering, Fulfilment and Dispatch. Ana costed two alternatives; Marco goes into the autumn renewal talks with a real one.

  1. List your exits

    Every vendor, SaaS, platform and open-source dependency you couldn't remove in a week.

  2. Map the dependency

    Which parts of your system touch it, directly? Ideally: one adapter in one place.

  3. Secure your data

    What you'd need back, in what format, how often you export it today.

  4. Price the alternatives

    Two options with effort, lead time and cost; compare with the notice period.

  5. Watch for warnings

    Price changes, outages, acquisitions, end-of-life notices, slow support.

  6. Keep it cheap, now

    Adapters, regular exports, notice and data clauses in the contract; review yearly.

Vocabulary for the exam

Exit plan
How you'd leave a vendor or platform, and what it would take.
Lock-in
The cost and time of leaving.
Adapter
One place in your code that talks to the vendor.
Notice period
How early you must say you're leaving.
Data portability
Getting your data back in a usable format.
Leverage
The negotiating power a credible alternative gives you.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Leverage: a costed alternative changes the renewal talk.
  • No surprises: lead times known before notice periods run out.
  • Cheaper exits from design choices made now.
  • Data you own, tested regularly.
  • Calmer decisions when a vendor changes course.

What we lose

  • Upfront effort: a day per important vendor.
  • Adapters and exports cost a little, every month.
  • Can look like distrust to the vendor.
  • Plans go stale without a yearly review.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Planning at renewal

    The exit is costed after the notice deadline. Fix → write it before signing; review a year ahead.

  • Vendor everywhere

    Five modules call StockPilot directly. Fix → one adapter in one place; fitness function to keep it there.

  • Untested exports

    The export exists but nobody has opened it. Fix → restore it into something once a year.

  • Lead time > notice

    Leaving takes 7 months; notice is 6. Fix → start pricing alternatives before the notice window.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every vendor or platform you couldn't remove in a week.
  • Before signing or renewing a contract.
  • When a vendor changes prices, terms or owners.
  • Regulated environments that require exit plans.

Skip it when…

  • Commodity tools with drop-in alternatives (a CI service, a font).
  • Short trials you'd simply stop.

8Self-check quiz

What makes an exit cheap?

Few touch points (one adapter), your data regularly exported, and contract clauses for notice and help.

When should you write an exit plan?

Before signing, and review it at least yearly, well before the notice period.

Why compare lead time with the notice period?

If leaving takes longer than the notice, you have to start before you give notice.

What does an exit plan give you if you never leave?

Leverage in negotiations and calm, informed decisions.

try it

Pick the vendor your system would struggle most to lose. In one page, write what depends on it, where your code touches it, how you'd get your data back, two alternatives with rough effort, and the notice period. Then test the data export by loading it somewhere.

remember!!

Hope never to use the exit. Keep it marked, unlocked and drilled.
