Technology Evaluation

TL;DR

Choosing a technology from blog posts, conference talks or vendor demos picks the best-marketed option. Instead, funnel: a long list, cut by must-haves to a shortlist of two or three, then a bake-off on your own data and load. Write the criteria and their weights before anyone runs anything, timebox it (two weeks is plenty), include the people who'll run it, and end with an ADR.

1The situation Larder, 11 May 2026

Rotterdam opened on 27 April with the new Dispatch service, still running the old assignment loop every 30 seconds. Express on time is 89 % in Amsterdam and 84 % in Rotterdam; the target is 95 %. Dispatch needs a real routing engine by July. Eli wants the one from a conference talk; Ben wants to write their own; Marco wants whatever's cheapest. And Ana remembers the 2025 search-engine “look” that ran for three weeks without a decision.

  • Talks and demos show the best case, on someone else's data.
  • Without criteria set in advance, the evaluation ends in a debate, or never ends.
  • The people who'll run it at 3 a.m. are often not asked.

2Real-life analogy buying a used car

You start with twenty ads, drop the ones that fail your must-haves (too small, too old), view three, and test-drive the best on your own commute, not the dealer's smooth route. A mechanic checks what you can't see. Then you buy one, and you decided what mattered before you fell for the colour.

3How it solves it funnel, then a bake-off on your data

The evaluation took two weeks: two days for the long list and must-haves, three for docs and demos, and a week-long bake-off replaying Friday 8 May's Amsterdam peak at 300 couriers. Engine B was fastest, but nobody in the team could maintain its solver model. Service C was good and costly at six-city volume. Engine A won (ADR 0012).

  1. Write the question and criteria first

    Must-haves (pass/fail) and weighted criteria, agreed with the approver, before looking at products.

  2. Long list, fast

    Five to ten candidates from colleagues, the radar, analysts, open source. An afternoon.

  3. Cut with must-haves

    Anything failing a must-have is out, however good its demo.

  4. Shortlist two or three

    Read the docs, see a demo, ask a reference customer.

  5. Bake-off on your data

    Same data, same load, same hardware for each; involve the people who'll run it. Timebox a week.

  6. Score, decide, record

    Fill the scorecard, decide, write the ADR; add the winner to the radar as “trial”.

Vocabulary for the exam

Long list
Every plausible candidate, before filtering.
Must-have
A pass/fail criterion; failing it rules a candidate out.
Shortlist
Two or three candidates worth testing properly.
Bake-off
Testing the shortlist side by side on your own data and load.
Scorecard
The weighted criteria with each candidate's results.
Reference call
Talking to another customer who runs it.

Question   Which engine assigns orders to couriers best for six cities?
Owner      Eli (+ Ben, Dee for the courier app)     Approver: Ana · timebox 2 weeks

            p95 < 2 s for 300 couriers (QAS from Vol. 1 PoC) · licence OK for SaaS use
Long list   7 → must-haves → 4 → docs, demo, reference call → 3

Bake-off    Replay of Fri 8 May, Amsterdam, 17:00–19:30, scaled to 300 couriers
                                    weight   Engine A   Engine B   Service C
p95 assignment time                   3      1.4 s      0.9 s      1.1 s
on time in simulation                 5      93 %       94 %       93 %
team can run and change it            4      yes        weak       yes
cost / month at 6 cities              3      €300       €300       €4,800

tools: an evaluation card in the repo; load-test tools for the bake-off (k6, Locust); recorded production data for replays. Analyst reports and vendor comparisons are fine for the long list, never for the decision.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Decisions on your data, not on demos.
  • Ends on time: a timebox and criteria set in advance.
  • Operators have a say before they inherit it.
  • A plan B: the runner-up is already tested.
  • Defensible: the scorecard explains the choice.

What we lose

  • Two weeks of several people's time.
  • Replays aren't production: surprises remain.
  • Vendors push back on bake-offs, or tune for them.
  • Over-engineering for small choices.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Criteria after results

    The weights shift once the favourite is behind. Fix → write and agree criteria before the bake-off.

  • The vendor's data

    Benchmarks on the vendor's demo dataset. Fix → replay your own peak, at your target scale.

  • No timebox

    One more candidate, one more week. Fix → two weeks, then decide with what you have.

  • Operators left out

    The winner is fast and nobody can run it. Fix → the on-call team scores “can we run it”.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Platforms, engines and services you'll live with for years.
  • When people already have favourites from talks.
  • When cost or performance at scale is uncertain.
  • Before a multi-year contract.

Skip it when…

  • Small libraries inside one module: pick one, it's a two-way door.
  • When one option is the obvious standard and nobody objects.

8Self-check quiz

What must be written before the bake-off?

The question, must-haves and weighted criteria, agreed with the approver.

Why test on your own data?

Demos and vendor benchmarks show the best case on someone else's data.

What happens to the runner-up?

It's a tested plan B, noted in the ADR.

Who should score “can we run it”?

The people who'll operate it, e.g. the on-call engineers.

try it

Pick a technology your team is about to choose. Write the must-haves and four weighted criteria before looking at any product, and get your lead to agree them. Then design a one-week bake-off on a copy of your own data. Compare it with how the choice would have been made otherwise.

remember!!

Decide what matters before the viewing; test-drive on your own commute.
