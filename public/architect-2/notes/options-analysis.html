Options Analysis

TL;DR

Before choosing, write down three or four real options, always including “do nothing” (or the simplest thing by hand). Take the criteria from the drivers and constraints, give them weights before you score, score each option, and look at the totals. The matrix doesn't decide; it makes the reasoning visible and arguable. When the result surprises you, find out why: either a criterion is missing, or your favourite wasn't as good as you thought.

  • Origin: decision matrices (Pugh, 1981); ATAM and trade-off analysis (SEI)
  • Lives in: the Options section of an ADR or RFC
  • Pairs with: business drivers, ADRs, trade-off sliders

1The situation Larder, 13 April 2026

Mo has a pilot: Korenwolf's Amsterdam HQ gets a weekly pantry restock, delivered 07:00–09:00 by van, invoiced monthly, from 4 May. In the first meeting Femke says “just make it work”, Ben sketches a new B2B ordering service, Mo says Hanna wants it in three weeks, and Grace says “don't touch checkout”. Everyone argues for their favourite answer. Nobody has listed the options, and nobody has asked what “good” means.

  • Debates between favourites go round in circles: each person scores by different criteria.
  • “Do nothing” or “do it by hand” is rarely on the list, though it often wins.
  • Rotterdam opens in two weeks; the team can't afford the wrong big build.
meanwhile, in Grace’s notebook
  • Opportunity Cost (Grace, vol. 2): Grace brought the price of “just four weeks” to this meeting: about €30k of delayed approvals.

2Real-life analogy choosing a home

A family thinking about moving writes down what matters (commute, space, price) and how much, before the viewings, or the nicest kitchen wins. Each home gets a score per criterion. And staying put goes on the sheet too: sometimes it wins, and that's a fine answer. The totals don't buy the house; they make the family's reasoning visible, so they can argue about the right thing.

3How it solves it options, weights, scores, then talk

The team expected the matrix to back a new service. Instead option 0 (Mo's spreadsheet, with support entering one standing order a week by hand) won: the pilot is one office for twelve weeks, and the team's effort belongs to Rotterdam. The real insight wasn't the number; it was the trigger it exposed: build B when a second office signs.

  1. Frame the question

    One sentence, with its deadline: “How do Korenwolf's weekly orders reach the store by 4 May?”

  2. List 3–4 real options

    Including 0: do nothing, or do it by hand. Each option described in two lines.

  3. Pick criteria from the drivers

    Deadlines, ASRs, constraints, effort, reversibility. Five or six, no more.

  4. Weight before scoring

    Agree the weights with the approver first, so nobody tunes them to a favourite.

  5. Score and total

    1–5 each, with one line of reasoning per score; multiply by the weight.

  6. Discuss, decide, record

    Ask why the result surprises anyone; then decide and write the ADR, with the triggers for revisiting.

Vocabulary for the exam

Option 0
Do nothing, or the simplest manual way: the baseline every option must beat.
Criterion
A thing that makes an option better or worse, taken from the drivers.
Weight
How much a criterion matters, agreed before scoring.
Weighted score
Score × weight, summed per option.
Must-have
A criterion that rules an option out if it fails, before scoring.
Sensitivity check
Changing a weight a little: does the winner change?

# 11. Run the Korenwolf pilot on manual standing orders

Status: Accepted · 2026-04-15 · Approver: Femke · Driver: Ana · with Mo, Ben, Grace

## Question
How do Korenwolf's weekly pantry orders reach store Zuid, from 4 May,
for a 12-week pilot at one office?

## Options                                        weights: 5   4   3   4   2
0. Support enters one standing order a week                 5   5   1   4   5 = 74
1. (A) Normal orders with a "business" flag                 4   2   2   4   3 = 56
2. (B) Scheduled orders on business accounts, in Ordering   4   4   4   3   4 = 68
3. (C) A new Business Ordering service                      1   5   5   1   2 = 48
Criteria: ready by 4 May · safe for consumer checkout (ASR 4) · grows to
3 offices · effort during the Rotterdam launch · easy to undo

## Decision
Option 0 for the pilot: ~3 h/week of support time, invoices from Mo's sheet.
Sensitivity: B wins only if "grows to 3 offices" weighs 5 or more.

## Revisit when
Korenwolf (or any client) signs for more than one office → build B.

tools: a table in the ADR is enough; a spreadsheet for a sensitivity check. Heavier methods: ATAM and CBAM (SEI), Pugh matrices, AHP for many criteria. Most architecture tools don't add much here.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Visible reasoning: people argue about criteria, not favourites.
  • “Do nothing” gets a fair hearing, and often wins.
  • Criteria tie back to the drivers, not to taste.
  • Triggers fall out of it: what would change the answer.
  • Feeds the ADR directly: the Options section is written.

What we lose

  • False precision: 68 vs 74 looks exact; it isn't.
  • Gameable: weights tuned to a favourite.
  • Time: an hour or two with the right people.
  • Misses what isn't a criterion: politics, morale, timing.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Weights after scores

    The weights are adjusted until the favourite wins. Fix → agree weights with the approver before anyone scores.

  • No option 0

    Every option is a build. Fix → always score “do nothing” or “do it by hand”.

  • Trusting decimals

    68 vs 74 treated as a verdict. Fix → run a sensitivity check; close totals mean talk, not tally.

  • Straw-man options

    Two silly options next to the real one. Fix → only options someone would seriously defend.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Decisions with three or more defensible options.
  • When a meeting has gone round in circles.
  • Anything going into an ADR or RFC.
  • When stakeholders weigh things differently and need to see it.

Skip it when…

  • Two-way doors: try one and see.
  • When one option fails a must-have: no matrix needed.
  • Tiny decisions: a line in the decision log.

8Self-check quiz

Which option should always be on the list?

Option 0: do nothing, or the simplest manual way.

Why agree the weights before scoring?

So nobody tunes the weights until their favourite wins.

Where should the criteria come from?

The business drivers, ASRs and constraints, not personal taste.

The totals are 68 and 74. What next?

Check sensitivity and discuss why; close totals mean the criteria need talking about, not that 74 wins automatically.

try it

Take a decision your team is debating now. Write the question in one sentence, list three options plus option 0, and pick five criteria from your goals. Get the approver to set the weights before anyone scores. Score it in 30 minutes with two colleagues and note whether option 0 surprised you.

remember!!

Weights before scores. And stay put is always on the list.
