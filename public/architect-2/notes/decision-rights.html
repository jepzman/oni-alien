Decision Rights (RACI/DACI)

TL;DR

Many decisions don't stall because they're hard, but because nobody knows who makes them. Before a cross-team decision starts, name the roles: DACI (Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed) or RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed). The rule that matters: exactly one approver. Then write a small standing table of decision rights (“inside a module, the team decides; over €1,000 a month, the CFO”) so most decisions never need a chart at all.

  • Family: recording decisions
  • Origin: RACI from 1950s–70s project management; DACI popularised by Intuit and Atlassian
  • Lives in: the RFC or ADR header; a decision-rights page
  • Pairs with: decision log, RFCs, stakeholder map

1The situation Larder, 23 March 2026

December's risk-storming put a payment provider outage at peak on the top-five list. In February Cho researched a second provider. Then nothing happened. Marco thought it was his decision (it costs money); Ana thought it was architecture; Lena wanted to review the contract first; Cho thought she'd only been asked to research. After three weeks and four meetings, nobody had said “decided”, and Rotterdam's opening was five weeks away.

  • Everyone in the meeting has an opinion; nobody has the call.
  • Two people who each think they decide are as bad as none.
  • The architect becomes the default decider for everything, and a bottleneck.

2Real-life analogy planning a wedding

A wedding has lots of opinions and exactly one couple. The planner drives it: books, chases, keeps it moving. The couple approve: yes or no, and that's final. Parents and the caterer are heard before anything is decided. The guests are told the result. When the mother of the bride thinks she's the approver, that's when weddings go wrong.

3How it solves it name the roles, one approver

For a single big decision, put a DACI line at the top of the RFC or ADR. For the everyday flow, agree a standing table once: which kinds of decision belong to teams, to the architect, to finance, to legal. The table is what gives teams autonomy: inside their area, they don't need to ask.

  1. Spot the stuck decision

    Two meetings without a decision usually means unclear rights, not a hard problem.

  2. Name one approver

    The person accountable for the outcome. Never a committee, never two people.

  3. Name the driver

    Often not the approver: the one who gathers facts, writes the options and books the meeting.

  4. List contributors and informed

    Contributors are heard before; informed are told after. Keep contributors few.

  5. Set a date

    Put the decision in the log with its due date; the driver brings options by then.

  6. Write the standing table

    Once a quarter: kinds of decision → who decides. Publish it next to the decision log.

Vocabulary for the exam

DACI
Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed.
RACI
Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed: the older variant.
Approver / Accountable
The one person who makes the final call.
Driver
The person who moves the decision forward.
Veto
A right to block (e.g. the DPO on personal data), not to decide.
Decision rights
A standing agreement on who decides which kinds of thing.

# D-22: Do we add a second payment provider before the Christmas peak?

Driver        Cho (payments)                    → options by 30 Mar
Approver      Marco (CFO)                       → decides 2 Apr
Contributors  Ana (architecture), Lena (contract, DPO: veto on data terms),
              Sam (support impact), Joost (ops on peak days)
Informed      Femke, Priya, all tech leads (in the Friday update)

Options       1. Stay with one provider; accept the risk (4 h outage ≈ €60k lost orders)
              2. Second provider for cards + iDEAL, manual switch-over
              3. Second provider with automatic failover per transaction

Decision      2 Apr: Option 2. Live before 1 Sep 2026. ~€900/month + fees.
              Option 3 revisited after the Christmas peak.     → ADR 0009

  Inside one module, reversible        the team

tools: a header in the RFC or ADR, or a one-page decision-rights table on the wiki. Atlassian's DACI template, RAPID (Bain) for big organisations, and the Team Topologies interaction modes for team-to-team decisions.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Decisions get made: someone owns the call.
  • Fewer, shorter meetings: contributors give input, they don't vote.
  • Teams decide alone where the standing table says they can.
  • No surprise vetoes: legal and finance are named up front.
  • Less architect bottleneck: Ana isn't the default for everything.

What we lose

  • Feels formal for small teams.
  • Contributors can feel overruled when the approver disagrees.
  • Charts nobody reads if made for every decision.
  • Politics: naming the approver can start an argument.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Two approvers

    Marco and Femke both “approve”, so neither does. Fix → exactly one name in the A.

  • Contributors become voters

    The decision waits until all eight agree. Fix → contributors are heard; the approver decides.

  • A chart for everything

    DACI tables for library bumps. Fix → use the standing table; chart only stuck or cross-team decisions.

  • The architect approves all

    Every decision queues for Ana. Fix → push decisions to teams; keep the architect for the cross-cutting ones.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A decision that's been discussed twice without a result.
  • Decisions that cross teams, budgets or legal lines.
  • Big RFCs: put the roles in the header.
  • A growing organisation where “who decides?” keeps coming up.

Skip it when…

  • Decisions inside one team that the standing table already covers.
  • Emergencies: the incident lead decides; tidy up later.

8Self-check quiz

What do the letters in DACI stand for?

Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed.

What's the one rule you must not break?

Exactly one approver.

How is a contributor different from an approver?

Contributors are heard before the decision; the approver makes it.

Why write a standing decision-rights table?

So most decisions don't need a chart: teams know what they can decide alone.

try it

Find the decision in your team that has been discussed most often without a result. Write a DACI header for it with one approver, a driver and a due date, and send it to the approver for a yes. Then draft five lines of standing decision rights for your team and ask your manager which ones they'd change.

remember!!

Lots of opinions, one couple. Name the approver before the meeting, not after.
