Revisiting Decisions

TL;DR

Decisions age. Some should change when the world does; most shouldn't change just because someone new dislikes them. Give every ADR a revisit trigger: a measurable condition or a date (“if orders pass 200/s”, “if a second office signs”). Then hold a short decision review every quarter: check each trigger against today's facts, and decide keep, watch or supersede. Re-open decisions on purpose, with evidence, not on a hunch, and never keep one alive just because it was expensive.

1The situation Larder, 21 September 2026

One week in September, three decisions get re-opened in three different chats: a new on-call engineer says “the outbox is slow, let's switch to change data capture”; Femke asks whether Larder should “still be a monolith”; someone calls Kafka overkill. No new facts, just hunches. Meanwhile the one real change slips by: Korenwolf has signed for three offices, and their orders are still entered by hand under ADR 0011, a decision written for a twelve-week pilot.

  • Without triggers, decisions are reopened by whoever feels strongest this week.
  • The decisions that really should change are missed, because nobody is checking.
  • Sunk cost keeps bad decisions alive; fashion kills good ones.

2Real-life analogy the prescription with a review date

A good doctor prescribes medicine with a review date: take this for three months, then come back. At the review, the doctor checks the results and decides to continue, change or stop. Patients don't stop on a hunch halfway through, and they don't take it forever without a check.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the project
The prescriptionThe ADR

3How it solves it triggers, checked every quarter

The review took 45 minutes. Four decisions stayed: their triggers weren't close. Dispatch's routing (ADR 0012) went on watch: Rotterdam is at 91 % on time, below the 93 % trigger. And ADR 0011 was superseded: Ana proposed ADR 0014, scheduled orders on business accounts in Ordering, option B from April's analysis. The hunches got a polite answer: “show me the trigger”.

  1. Write a trigger in every ADR

    A measurable condition or a date: “orders > 200/s”, “a second office signs”, “review in 12 months”.

  2. Collect the facts

    Before the review: the numbers each trigger needs, from dashboards and the business.

  3. Review quarterly, briefly

    Architect and tech leads, 45 minutes: each trigger against the facts.

  4. Decide: keep, watch or supersede

    Watch = check again next month. Supersede = a new ADR that links the old one.

  5. Answer hunches with triggers

    “What fact would change it?” If it's new, check it; if not, the decision stands.

  6. Ignore sunk cost

    What it cost to build doesn't matter; only what's best from here.

Vocabulary for the exam

Revisit trigger
The condition or date that re-opens a decision.
Decision review
A regular check of triggers against facts.
Keep / watch / supersede
The three outcomes of a review.
Sunk cost
Money or effort already spent; irrelevant to what's best now.
Decision quality
Judging a decision by what was known then, not by the outcome alone.
Hunch
A feeling without new facts: not a reason to re-open.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Real changes get caught: Korenwolf's contract re-opened ADR 0011.
  • Fewer hunch debates: “show me the trigger”.
  • Decisions stay current without a rewrite.
  • Sunk cost loses: supersede when the facts say so.
  • Trust in decisions: people know they'll be checked.

What we lose

  • Triggers can be wrong, or the wrong metric.
  • A quarterly slot with five people.
  • Facts take effort to gather.
  • “Watch” can become forever without a re-check date.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • No triggers

    ADRs end at “Consequences”. Fix → add a measurable “revisit when” to every ADR.

  • Hunches win

    The loudest voice re-opens a decision weekly. Fix → ask what new fact would change it; check it at the review.

  • Watch forever

    ADR 0012 on watch for a year. Fix → every “watch” gets a re-check date.

  • Sunk-cost loyalty

    “We spent four months on it.” Fix → judge from here on; supersede if the facts say so.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every ADR, at the time it's written.
  • When a decision keeps being re-argued.
  • After big changes: a new city, a new client, a new team.
  • Quarterly, as a standing slot.

Skip it when…

  • Two-way doors: just change them.
  • Decisions about to be retired anyway.

8Self-check quiz

What makes a good revisit trigger?

A measurable condition or a date that would change the decision, e.g. “orders > 200/s”.

What are the three outcomes of a review?

Keep, watch (with a re-check date), or supersede with a new ADR.

Someone says “the outbox is slow”. What do you ask?

What new fact would change the decision? Then check it against the trigger.

Why ignore sunk cost?

What's been spent can't be recovered; only the best path from here matters.

try it

Take your five most important past decisions. For each, write one measurable “revisit when” trigger. Book 45 minutes with two colleagues, gather the current number for each trigger, and decide keep, watch or supersede. Note how many hunch debates you can now answer with a trigger.

remember!!

Every decision gets a review date. A hunch reopens nothing; a trigger reopens anything.
