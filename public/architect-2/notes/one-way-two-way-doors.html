One-way vs Two-way Doors

TL;DR

Most decisions are two-way doors: if you're wrong, you walk back out at low cost. Make those fast, close to the work, by the team, without an architecture meeting. A few are one-way doors: contracts, data you can't un-share or un-delete, interfaces others depend on, deadlines you can't move. Slow down only for those: an RFC or ADR, the right people, a careful look. The skill is sorting them, and spotting doors that look two-way but lock behind you.

1The situation Larder, 9 April 2026

Since the decision log started, every open question has gone to Ana's Thursday architecture meeting. It decides about eight a week. Now there are 31 in the queue, two weeks deep, and the log library waits as long as a two-year payment contract. Ben's question, whether the stock cache should expire after 60 or 120 seconds, has waited nine days, though it's one line of configuration.

  • Treating every decision as big makes the small ones slow and the architect a bottleneck.
  • Treating every decision as small lets an irreversible one slip through in a chat.
  • The team has stopped deciding things it's perfectly able to decide.

2Real-life analogy doors at the airport

In an airport terminal you walk in and out of shop doors without thinking: if it's the wrong shop, you leave. But at the door marked “no re-entry” you stop, check your passport, your bag and your gate, because once you're through, there's no coming back. Nobody checks their passport before entering a bookshop.

3How it solves it sort first, then pick the speed

Ana and Sanne sorted the 31 in one hour: 24 two-way, back to the teams by Friday; 7 one-way, each with an owner and an RFC or ADR. The Thursday meeting shrank to the seven. The tricky ones were in the middle: an event schema is two-way until another team consumes it; then it's a contract.

  1. Ask: what does undoing cost?

    Time, money, data, trust. Hours or days → two-way. Months, lost data or a contract → one-way.

  2. Look for hidden locks

    Other teams depending on it, customers seeing it, data leaving your hands, a long contract.

  3. Two-way: decide fast, near the work

    The team decides, logs one line, and watches the result. Use a flag to make it even cheaper to undo.

  4. One-way: slow down

    RFC or ADR, options analysis, the approver, legal or finance if they're affected.

  5. Make doors two-way where you can

    Feature flags, a trial period in the contract, a versioned API, a data export.

  6. Re-sort as things change

    A schema becomes one-way when a second team starts consuming it.

Vocabulary for the exam

One-way door
A decision that's costly or impossible to reverse; decide carefully.

# Door check                               (2 minutes, before a decision is queued)

1. If we're wrong, what does undoing it cost?
   [ ] hours or days of work            → two-way
   [ ] weeks of work                    → probably one-way: ask Ana
   [ ] money we can't get back, a contract, a public promise   → one-way
2. Does anyone outside the team depend on it? (consumers of an event or API,
   customers who'll see it, Korenwolf, StockPilot)              yes → one-way
3. Does data leave our control, or get deleted?                 yes → one-way
4. Can we make it two-way? (flag, trial period, versioned API, export)

Two-way → the team decides, one line in the log, review the result in 2 weeks.
One-way → owner + RFC/ADR + approver; Thursday architecture meeting.

Examples (Apr 2026): D-24 substitution window → two-way (flag, Grace decided)
                     D-22 second PSP contract (24 months) → one-way (Marco)
                     order.ready_for_dispatch v1 schema → one-way since Dispatch reads it

tools: a checklist in the decision log is enough. Feature flags (Unleash, LaunchDarkly, a config table) make many doors two-way; contract trial periods and data exports make vendor doors less one-way.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Small decisions go fast: days, not weeks in a queue.
  • The architect focuses on the few decisions that matter.
  • Teams own more, inside their areas.
  • Big decisions get real care, not a rushed slot.
  • Designs that stay reversible: flags, trials, versions.

What we lose

  • Misjudged doors: a hidden lock slips through as two-way.
  • Many small reversals if teams treat two-way as “don't think”.
  • Disagreement about the sort itself.
  • Making doors two-way costs: flags and versions need upkeep.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Hidden locks

    “Just a schema” that three teams already consume. Fix → ask who depends on it before calling it two-way.

  • Everything is one-way

    Fear turns every decision into a committee. Fix → most decisions are two-way; prove otherwise.

  • Two-way, never reversed

    A bad two-way decision lives on because nobody checks. Fix → review two-way decisions after two weeks.

  • No way to make it two-way

    A big-bang launch with no flag. Fix → spend a little to add a flag, a trial or a version.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When decisions queue for one person or meeting.
  • When teams wait for permission they don't need.
  • Before contracts, data changes and shared interfaces.
  • Designing for change: making doors two-way on purpose.

Skip it when…

  • Emergencies: make the call, then review.
  • When the team already sorts instinctively and nothing queues.

8Self-check quiz

What makes a door one-way?

Undoing it is costly or impossible: contracts, lost or shared data, interfaces others depend on, public promises.

Who should decide two-way doors?

The team closest to the work, quickly, with one line in the log.

When does an event schema become a one-way door?

When other teams consume it: changing it then breaks them.

How can you turn a one-way door into a two-way one?

Feature flags, trial periods in contracts, versioned APIs, data exports.

try it

List every decision waiting on someone in your team right now. Run the four-question door check on each. Send the two-way ones back to the people doing the work today, with a date to review the result, and see how long your queue is afterwards.

Walk through shop doors. Check your bag at no re-entry.
