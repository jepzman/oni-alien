Technology Radar

TL;DR

As a team grows, “can I use X?” arrives faster than an architect can answer it. A technology radar answers in advance: every technology the teams care about, placed in one of four rings: adopt (our default), trial (use it on real work, carefully), assess (explore, not in production) and hold (don't start anything new with it). Build it with the tech leads, give each blip a sentence and a link to its ADR, and refresh it every six months.

  • Origin: ThoughtWorks Technology Radar (2010–); Zalando and others publish their own
  • Lives in: a page in the repo or wiki, refreshed twice a year
  • Pairs with: technology evaluation, ADRs, golden paths

1The situation Larder, 22 June 2026

Four engineers joined in the spring (14 → 18). In May alone Ana got eleven “can I use X?” messages: Go for a small tool, a second message-queue library, MongoDB for business orders, a new date library. Each got an answer, but a different one depending on the day and who asked. One new hire had already written a tool in Go before asking.

  • Every new engineer brings favourites; without a shared default, the stack sprawls.
  • The architect becomes a help desk for technology choices.
  • Old decisions (ADRs) exist, but nobody new knows which technologies they settled.

2Real-life analogy the pantry list on the fridge

A busy household keeps a pantry list on the fridge: what's always stocked, what they're trying this month, what they've heard about, and what they don't buy any more (and why). Whoever does the shopping buys the right things without phoning home. Twice a year someone updates the list.

3How it solves it one page, four rings, twice a year

Ana and the four tech leads built the first radar in one afternoon: fourteen blips, each with one sentence and a link to its ADR or evaluation card. The rule that made it work: adopt and trial need no permission; assess means a spike first; hold needs an ADR to break.

  1. Collect the candidates

    Ask each team what they use, want to use, and wish they didn't. Twenty to forty items.

  2. Place them together

    An afternoon with the tech leads: ring and quadrant per blip. Disagreement is useful.

  3. Write one sentence each

    Why it's in that ring, and a link to the ADR, evaluation card or spike.

  4. Say what each ring allows

    Adopt and trial: go ahead. Assess: spike first. Hold: needs an ADR.

  5. Publish it where people look

    In the repo or wiki, linked from onboarding and the PR template.

  6. Refresh every six months

    Move blips, add new ones, explain each move in a sentence.

Vocabulary for the exam

Blip
One technology or technique on the radar.
Adopt
Our default; use it.
Trial
Use on real work, with care and a named owner.
Assess
Worth exploring; not in production yet.
Hold
Don't start anything new with it.
Quadrant
A grouping: techniques, tools, platforms, languages and frameworks.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Fast answers: most “can I use X?” questions answer themselves.
  • Less sprawl: shared defaults, deliberate exceptions.
  • Onboarding: newcomers see the stack and the reasons.
  • Safe experimentation: assess and trial are allowed, on purpose.
  • Links old decisions to everyday choices.

What we lose

  • Goes stale without the six-monthly refresh.
  • Can feel like a ban list if hold dominates.
  • An afternoon of tech leads twice a year.
  • Arguments about rings can drag on.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Never refreshed

    The December radar is still June's in March. Fix → put the refresh date on the page and in the calendar.

  • The architect's radar

    Ana fills it alone; teams ignore it. Fix → place blips with the tech leads.

  • No reasons

    A ring without a sentence invites arguments. Fix → one sentence and a link per blip.

  • Hold as a ban

    Hold blocks fixing old code that uses it. Fix → hold means nothing new; existing use is fine.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Teams growing past ten engineers.
  • When “can I use X?” reaches the architect weekly.
  • Onboarding new hires.
  • After a technology evaluation, to record where the winner sits.

Skip it when…

  • A single small team that already shares one stack.
  • As a substitute for ADRs: the radar points to them.

8Self-check quiz

What are the four rings?

Adopt, trial, assess and hold.

What does “hold” mean?

Don't start anything new with it; existing use can stay.

Who should place the blips?

The architect with the tech leads, together.

How often should it be refreshed?

About every six months, with a sentence explaining each move.

try it

List the ten technologies your team argues about most. Put each in a ring (adopt, trial, assess, hold) with one sentence of reasoning. Show it to two other engineers and move anything they can convince you about. Publish it with a refresh date six months out.

remember!!
