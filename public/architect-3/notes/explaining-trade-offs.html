Explaining Trade-offs to Non-Engineers

TL;DR

When a non-engineer asks for something expensive (“99.99 % uptime”), a technical answer sounds like “no”. Translate the trade-off into what they care about: minutes, euros, customers, dates. Show what each option buys and costs, find out which part of the request really matters to them, and offer a cheaper option that protects that part. Use an everyday comparison, then the numbers. Then let them choose.

1The situation Larder, 12 January 2027

On 18 December, during the Christmas peak, a database failover took checkout down for 38 minutes. In January Femke asks Ana for “99.99 % uptime” before Korenwolf's renewal talks in 2027. Ana starts explaining multi-region failover, replication lag and recovery objectives. Femke's eyes glaze over; what she hears is “no”, and she starts wondering if she needs a different architect.

  • Technical explanations answer the engineer's question, not the asker's.
  • Requests like “99.99 %” stand in for a real worry (here: peaks and Korenwolf's mornings).
  • Without costs in their terms, people can't make the trade-off themselves.

2Real-life analogy the second car

A family worried about breakdowns could buy a second car, just in case: €4,000 a year. Or a spare tyre and roadside assistance: €120 a year, covering most of the breakdowns that actually happen. The right answer depends on which breakdowns they care about: getting to work every day, or never being stranded on a holiday drive.

3How it solves it minutes, euros, and which minutes matter

Ana came back with one page. 99.9 % is about 43 minutes down a month; 99.99 % about 4. Getting there everywhere means a second region: ≈ €9,000 a month, two more engineers, six months. Then she asked which minutes Femke really meant. The answer: Friday evenings and Korenwolf's mornings. Protecting those (faster failover, no changes in those windows, Korenwolf's orders picked the night before) costs ≈ €1,500 a month and takes six weeks. Femke chose it in five minutes.

  1. Hear the worry behind the request

    “99.99 %” usually means “never again like Christmas”.

  2. Translate into their units

    Minutes down a month, euros a month, customers affected, weeks to build.

  3. Use one everyday comparison

    Spare tyre vs second car; then the real numbers.

  4. Show two or three options

    Including one that protects the part that matters, cheaply.

  5. Make the costs honest

    Running costs and people, not just the bill.

  6. Let them choose

    Then record it as an ADR with their name as approver.

Vocabulary for the exam

Nines
Availability as a percentage: 99.9 % is “three nines”.
Downtime budget
Minutes of outage a month a target allows.
Critical window
A time when outages hurt most (Friday peak, 07:00–09:00).
Failover
Switching to a standby when the main component fails.
Multi-region
Running in two cloud regions to survive a region failing.
Trade-off
Getting more of one thing by accepting less of another.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • The real worry gets solved, not the literal request.
  • Decisions by the right person, with real costs.
  • Cheaper, faster options appear.
  • Trust: the architect sounds like a partner, not a blocker.
  • Shared vocabulary: minutes and euros.

What we lose

  • Simplifies: some nuance is lost.
  • Analogies can mislead if stretched.
  • Needs good numbers ready.
  • Takes a second conversation after the first goes wrong.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Answering in jargon

    Replication lag and RPO. Fix → minutes, euros, customers, weeks.

  • Taking the request literally

    Building 99.99 % everywhere. Fix → ask which minutes matter.

  • Saying no

    “That's not possible.” Fix → show what it costs, and a cheaper option.

  • Stretching the analogy

    Arguing about cars instead of Larder. Fix → one comparison, then the real numbers.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Requests for “more” from executives or clients: uptime, speed, security.
  • After an incident, when demands run high.
  • Any trade-off a non-engineer must decide.
  • Contract and service-level talks.

Skip it when…

  • Purely technical choices inside the team.
  • When the listener wants the technical depth: then give it.

8Self-check quiz

How many minutes a month is 99.9 % and 99.99 %?

About 43 minutes and about 4 minutes.

What was the worry behind Femke's request?

Outages at the Friday peak and in Korenwolf's 07:00–09:00 window.

What did option B do?

Protected the critical windows: faster failover, no changes in them, business orders picked the night before.

Why use an everyday comparison?

It makes the shape of the trade-off clear before the numbers.

try it

Pick a request you've recently answered with “that's hard”. Write the worry behind it, then two options in minutes, euros and weeks, one of which protects just the part that matters. Take it back to the person as one page.

remember!!

Ask which breakdowns matter. A spare tyre may beat a second car.
