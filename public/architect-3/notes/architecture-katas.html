Architecture Katas

TL;DR

Architects get good by designing systems, and most engineers rarely get to. An architecture kata is a short practice: a brief (a made-up or future system with a few requirements and constraints), small teams, 90 minutes to design, ten minutes to present, and feedback on the requirements they found, the trade-offs they named and how clearly they explained them. Run it monthly with a new brief, and the next architects grow inside the team.

1The situation Larder, 19 January 2027

Larder has 18 engineers and one architect. In 2027 four cities open, and Antwerp brings a new country. Every design question queues for Ana. The four tech leads are good engineers, but none of them has ever designed a whole system from requirements to trade-offs, and there's no safe place to practise.

  • Design skills grow only by designing, and real projects come rarely.
  • The architect becomes a bottleneck and a single point of failure.
  • Engineers learn trade-offs from incidents, the expensive way.

2Real-life analogy chess puzzles

Chess players don't only improve by playing whole games. They solve puzzles: a set position, a time limit, find the best move, then compare with a coach. Hundreds of positions a year, each short and safe to get wrong. The whole games get better because the positions are familiar.

3How it solves it brief, teams, 90 minutes, feedback

The first kata used a real future problem: Antwerp. Three teams found different things: one spotted that Belgian VAT on business invoices needed the billing service early; one chose separate stock per country and named the cost; one forgot Bancontact refunds entirely, and learned more than the others. The feedback was about trade-offs and clarity, not right answers. Now it's monthly, and the tech leads take turns writing briefs.

  1. Write a brief

    One page: the business, users, a few requirements and constraints, and something deliberately unclear.

  2. Form small teams

    Three people, mixed teams and levels, one tech lead each.

  3. Design for 90 minutes

    Whiteboard or paper; questions to the “customer” allowed.

  4. Present for 10 minutes

    Context, the main choices, the trade-offs, the risks.

  5. Give feedback, not grades

    On requirements found, trade-offs named and clarity. What would you ask in a real review?

  6. Repeat monthly

    A new brief each time; let others write them.

Vocabulary for the exam

Kata
A short, repeated practice exercise.
Brief
The made-up or future system to design.
Customer role
Someone who answers questions as the client would.
Trade-off
What each choice gains and gives up.
Feedback
Comments on reasoning and clarity, not a single right answer.
Growing architects
Building design skills across the team.

# Architecture kata · Larder in Antwerp                 90 min design · 10 min present

The business  Larder opens in Antwerp (planning date Sep 2027): express and slots,
              consumers first, offices later. Two stores to start.
Users         customers (French and Dutch), pickers, couriers, Larder for Business
Requirements  - the apps in French and Dutch
              - Belgian VAT on invoices; Bancontact payments and refunds
              - stock and prices per country; a product can exist in one country only
              - customer data stays in the EU (it does today)
Constraints   18 engineers; no new database types without an ADR; modular monolith
Unclear       Will Dutch stores ever serve Belgian customers? (ask the customer: Grace)

Present       context · 3 main decisions · the trade-off you're least sure of · top risk
Judges        Ana, Eli; Grace plays the customer

tools: a room, a whiteboard per team, a timer. Ted Neward's kata list (nealford.com/katas) and the O'Reilly Architectural Katas competitions for ready-made briefs.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Design practice without real-world risk.
  • More people who can design: less architect bottleneck.
  • Shared vocabulary for trade-offs.
  • Real insights when the brief is a real future problem.
  • Fun: teams like it.

What we lose

  • Two hours a month for nine to twelve people.
  • Made-up briefs can feel artificial.
  • Feedback needs skill to be useful, not judgemental.
  • Doesn't replace real projects.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Grading

    A winner and two losers. Fix → feedback on reasoning; no single right answer.

  • Briefs too big

    A whole bank in 90 minutes. Fix → a few requirements and one thing unclear.

  • Same people every time

    The tech leads only. Fix → mix levels; let juniors present.

  • Once only

    A great afternoon, never repeated. Fix → monthly, with rotating brief writers.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Teams growing faster than their architects.
  • Preparing for a known future problem (a new country).
  • Building design skills in tech leads.
  • Team-building with a real purpose.

Skip it when…

  • Teams in crisis: fix the crisis first.
  • As a substitute for involving engineers in real designs.

8Self-check quiz

What are the parts of a kata?

A brief, small teams, a timebox to design, short presentations, feedback.

What should the feedback focus on?

Requirements found, trade-offs named, and clarity, not a single right answer.

Why use a real future problem?

Practice and real insight at the same time, like Antwerp's VAT and Bancontact.

How often?

Monthly, with a new brief and rotating brief writers.

try it

Write a one-page brief for a problem your team will face next year. Run a 90-minute kata with two teams of three, ten-minute presentations and 20 minutes of feedback. Note one insight you'll use for real.

remember!!

Practise positions, not just whole games. A new puzzle every month.
