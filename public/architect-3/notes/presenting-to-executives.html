Presenting to Executives

TL;DR

Executives decide many things a day with little time for each. Give them the answer first, then the options in their terms (money, risk, time, customers), your recommendation and why, the main risk and how you'll handle it, and the ask: exactly what you need them to decide, today. Five slides, ten minutes, and the technical detail in the appendix for anyone who asks.

1The situation Larder, 2 December 2026

StockPilot's renewal decision is due by 31 December (the exit plan from July). Ana presents to Femke, Marco and Joost with 22 slides, starting from StockPilot's API rate limits and the stock cache design. At slide six, Femke stops her: “what do you want from us?” The answer was on slide 21. The meeting ends without a decision, with four weeks left.

  • Architects present in the order they worked: problem, analysis, conclusion.
  • Executives need the conclusion first, then just enough to trust it.
  • Without a clear ask, a good analysis ends in “let's discuss next week”.

2Real-life analogy the pilot's announcement

A good pilot says: “We'll land 20 minutes late.” Then what it means for you: connections after 15:30 are fine. Then what to do: the crew will help everyone else. Nobody explains the jet stream unless a passenger asks. The passengers can decide what to do in ten seconds.

3How it solves it answer, options, why, risk, ask

A week later Ana came back with five slides and a one-page memo sent the day before. Slide one was the answer. Slide two compared renewing (≈ €252k a year), WMS B (≈ €118k a year plus €90k to migrate) and building their own (≈ six months of a team). The risk slide addressed Joost's worry: migrate store by store, not during the Utrecht launch week. Marco had already negotiated a three-month bridge with StockPilot using the alternative. Approved in the meeting; ADR 0016 followed on 15 December.

  1. Start with the answer

    One sentence: what you recommend, by when.

  2. Compare options in their terms

    Money, risk, time, customers; two to four options including “do nothing”.

  3. Say why, briefly

    The two or three reasons that decide it.

  4. Name the main risk and the plan

    The thing they'll worry about, answered before they ask.

  5. Make the ask precise

    What decision, what money or people, by when: “decide today”.

  6. Send a memo first; keep an appendix

    One page the day before; technical detail at the back.

Vocabulary for the exam

BLUF
Bottom line up front: the conclusion first.
Pyramid principle
Answer first, then grouped supporting arguments.
The ask
The specific decision or resources you need.
Pre-read
A short memo sent before the meeting.
Appendix
Detail kept for questions, not presented.
Decision meeting
A meeting that ends with a decision, not a discussion.

tools: five slides or a one-page memo; Amazon-style written pre-reads; the architecture one-pager and exit plan as sources. No tool fixes the order of the argument.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Decisions in the meeting, not next week.
  • Executives trust the analysis because it's in their terms.
  • Shorter meetings: ten minutes plus questions.
  • The risk is pre-empted, not discovered.
  • Architects get invited back.

What we lose

  • Feels backwards to engineers at first.
  • Detail is hidden: some executives want it.
  • Translating to money takes work.
  • A wrong answer up front is very visible.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Chronological story

    Rate limits first, recommendation last. Fix → answer first.

  • Technical terms

    “Bulk endpoint throughput.” Fix → money, risk, time, customers.

  • No ask

    “Thoughts?” Fix → say exactly what you need decided, and by when.

  • No pre-read

    Executives see the numbers for the first time in the room. Fix → send one page the day before.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any decision that needs executive approval.
  • Board and investor updates on architecture.
  • Budget requests for technical work.
  • Vendor and platform decisions.

Skip it when…

  • Design reviews with engineers: there, the reasoning is the point.
  • Status updates with no decision needed.

8Self-check quiz

What goes on the first slide?

The answer: the recommendation in one sentence.

In what terms should options be compared?

Money, risk, time and customers.

What's “the ask”?

Exactly what decision or resources you need, by when.

Where does the technical detail go?

In an appendix, for questions.

try it

Take a technical decision you need approved. Write the first slide as one sentence: what you recommend, by when. Then fill four more: options in money, risk and time; why; the main risk; the ask. Send it as a one-page memo the day before the meeting.
