Solution Design Document Done Right

TL;DR

Clients and big partners often ask for a solution design document (SDD) on their own template, usually long and written for a different kind of system. Don't fight the template, and don't fill it blindly. Ask what they need to decide (data, security, availability, integration, support, exit), answer those clearly with your existing views, put a one-page summary on top, and for every section that doesn't apply write one line: “not applicable, because…”.

1The situation Larder, 1 December 2026

Korenwolf's three-office service starts in January. Their IT department sends Ruben a solution design template: forty sections, including mainframe interfaces, the location of the disaster-recovery site and a printer inventory. Ruben forwards it to Mo: “needed before January”. Mo asks Ana. Most of the template doesn't apply to a pantry delivery service, but leaving sections blank looks evasive.

  • Client templates are written for big internal IT projects, not for a SaaS-like service.
  • Filling every section produces forty pages nobody reads.
  • Blank sections look like hiding something.

2Real-life analogy the job application

A weak job application sends the whole life story. A good one reads the advert, works out what the employer needs to decide, and answers their questions, for this job, with evidence. Where something doesn't apply (no driving licence needed), it says so in a line, so nobody wonders.

3How it solves it ask, answer, summarise, say n/a

Mo set up a 30-minute call with Korenwolf's IT lead. Their real questions were six: what data Larder holds (office managers' names and emails, office addresses; no staff data), how it's secured (single sign-on for office managers), availability (98 % complete, by 09:00), integration (invoices from the billing service, a monthly report), support, and exit. Ana answered those with existing views, put the one-pager on top, and wrote one line for each other section. Fourteen pages; approved on 18 December.

  1. Ask what they must decide

    A short call with their IT or security lead: which questions really matter?

  2. Map their sections to your docs

    Most answers already exist: one-pager, views, deployment, exit plan.

  3. Answer the real questions fully

    With diagrams, numbers and named owners.

  4. Write “n/a, because…” for the rest

    One line each, so nothing looks hidden.

  5. Put a summary on top

    The one-pager, adapted to their service.

  6. Keep the source

    Build it from docs/architecture/ so next year's update is quick.

Vocabulary for the exam

SDD
Solution design document: how a solution meets a client's needs.
Due diligence
A client's checks before relying on a supplier.
Security questionnaire
A client's list of security questions for suppliers.
SSO
Single sign-on: users log in with their own company's account.
Service level
A promised measure of service, like 98 % complete by 09:00.
Not applicable
A section that doesn't fit, with the reason.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • The client gets what they need to decide, quickly.
  • Nothing looks hidden: n/a lines explain gaps.
  • Reuses existing docs instead of writing from scratch.
  • Shorter review cycles with the client's IT.
  • Reusable for the next business client.

What we lose

  • A call to arrange before writing.
  • Some clients insist on every section, in full.
  • Commitments in writing: service levels must be real.
  • Another version to keep in step with the docs.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Filling every box

    Forty pages about printers. Fix → ask what they must decide; n/a the rest with a reason.

  • Blank sections

    Gaps look like hiding. Fix → one line: “not applicable, because…”.

  • Promises you can't keep

    “99.99 % availability” to sound good. Fix → state real service levels, measured.

  • Writing from scratch

    A new document unrelated to your docs. Fix → build it from the one-pager and views.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Enterprise clients and partners with procurement rules.
  • Security and due-diligence reviews.
  • Any time a client's template arrives.
  • Before signing contracts with service levels.

Skip it when…

  • Internal design work: use arc42, ADRs and views.
  • When a standard security questionnaire answer bank covers it.

8Self-check quiz

What's the first step with a client's SDD template?

Ask what they actually need to decide.

What do you write for a section that doesn't apply?

One line: “not applicable, because…”.

What were Korenwolf's six real questions?

Data, security, availability, integration, support and exit.

Where should the SDD's content come from?

Your existing docs: the one-pager, views, deployment, exit plan.

try it

Find the last long template a client or partner asked you to fill in. List the six questions it really needed answered. Write those answers in two pages from your existing docs, plus one line for every other section, and compare with what you sent.
