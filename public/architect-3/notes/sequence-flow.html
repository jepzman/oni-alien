Sequence & Flow Diagrams

TL;DR

Box-and-arrow diagrams show what talks to what; they hide when and in what order. A sequence diagram puts the participants across the top as lifelines, runs time down the page, and shows each message in order, with its trigger. Draw one for every flow where order matters: anything with money, retries, events or several teams. The bugs it prevents live in the gaps between “then” and “meanwhile”.

  • Family: diagrams
  • Origin: message sequence charts (ITU, 1990s); UML sequence diagrams (1997)
  • Lives in: docs/architecture/flows/ as Mermaid or PlantUML
  • Pairs with: views & viewpoints, event storming, transactional outbox

1The situation Larder, 27 October 2026

Korenwolf signed for three offices in September, and ADR 0014 moved business orders into Ordering as scheduled orders. The first week, the orders team assumed billing lines are created when an order is placed; the billing team assumed when it's delivered. Both were reasonable, neither was drawn. Korenwolf was invoiced for three items that were out of stock, and Ruben, their procurement lead, sent his first complaint.

  • Container diagrams don't show order, timing or triggers.
  • Each team pictures the flow from its own side.
  • Money flows break silently when “when” is assumed.

2Real-life analogy the stage manager's cue sheet

A theatre runs on a cue sheet: lights down, then music, then curtain, then the actor enters, each on a trigger. Everyone knows their cue and what comes before it. Without the sheet, each crew member works from memory, and one night the curtain goes up in the dark.

3How it solves it lifelines across, time down

Ana drew the standing-order flow with both teams in one hour. The fix turned out to be one arrow: Billing creates usage lines when it hears order.delivered, with what was actually delivered, not when the order is placed. The diagram now lives next to ADR 0014, and the three credit notes went out the same week.

  1. Pick a flow where order matters

    Money, retries, events, several teams, or something that broke.

  2. List the participants

    Systems, modules or people, as lifelines left to right in rough order of involvement.

  3. Write the messages in order

    Each with a label: name, trigger, and what it carries.

  4. Mark async vs sync

    Events and queues look different from calls that wait.

  5. Add the unhappy paths

    Items short, timeouts, retries: a second diagram if it gets busy.

  6. Walk it with every team

    One hour, everyone in the room; fix disagreements on the drawing.

Vocabulary for the exam

Lifeline
A participant's vertical line; time runs down it.
Message
A call or event from one lifeline to another.
Synchronous
The sender waits for an answer.
Asynchronous
The sender doesn't wait; usually an event.
Activation
The bar showing a participant is busy.
Flowchart
Steps and decisions, without separate participants.


tools: Mermaid (renders in GitHub and most wikis), PlantUML, sequencediagram.org, WebSequenceDiagrams. Keep the source in the repo next to the ADR it explains.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Order and timing are visible: when, and in response to what.
  • Teams agree on the flow before building.
  • Money and retry bugs show up on paper.
  • Easy to keep as code in Mermaid.
  • Great for onboarding and incident reviews.

What we lose

  • Busy fast: more than ten messages gets hard to read.
  • Happy path bias: errors need their own diagrams.
  • Goes stale when flows change.
  • Only one scenario per diagram.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Happy path only

    No items short, no timeouts. Fix → add the unhappy paths, in a second diagram if needed.

  • Too many lifelines

    Fifteen participants across the page. Fix → group or split; keep to five or six.

  • Unlabelled messages

    Arrows without names or triggers. Fix → name every message and say what triggers it.

  • Drawn alone

    The author's view of the flow. Fix → walk it with every team involved.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Flows that involve money, retries or events.
  • Anything spanning several teams or services.
  • Before building an integration.
  • Incident reviews: what happened, in what order.

Skip it when…

  • Simple request/response inside one module.
  • Structure questions: use C4 instead.

8Self-check quiz

What runs down a sequence diagram?

Time: messages are shown in order, top to bottom.

What caused Korenwolf's wrong invoice?

Two teams assumed different triggers for creating billing lines: placed vs delivered.

How do you show an event vs a call?

Asynchronous messages (events) use a different arrow from synchronous calls.

When should you draw one?

For flows where order matters: money, retries, events, several teams.

try it

Pick the flow in your system that involves money or events across two teams. Draw it as a Mermaid sequence diagram in 30 minutes, then walk it with someone from each team. Note every point where they say “wait, that's not when we do it”.

remember!!

Lifelines across, time down. Every message has a cue.
