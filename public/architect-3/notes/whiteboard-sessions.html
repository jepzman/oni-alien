Whiteboard Sessions

TL;DR

A whiteboard session is the fastest way to design together, and the easiest to waste. Prepare the board: write the question at the top, a legend in the corner, room for options side by side. One person holds the marker and draws what the room says. Timebox it (60 minutes), end with a decision box (what, who, by when) and turn the photo into a record: an ADR, an RFC or a line in the decision log.

1The situation Larder, 13 October 2026

Korenwolf's three offices start in January, with vans every morning. Today Joost's team plans the vans by hand on a sheet. Ana calls a whiteboard session: should vans be part of Dispatch? Eight people, five markers, ninety minutes. Everyone draws at once, in their own style; arrows cross; two side debates start. It ends with a photo of a messy board and no decision.

  • No written question, so people solve different problems.
  • Several markers mean several diagrams on top of each other.
  • No decision box, so the session ends when time runs out.

2Real-life analogy the newsroom's morning meeting

A newspaper decides its front page every morning in a short meeting. There's one question (what's on page one?), one editor with the pen listing the candidates side by side, and twenty minutes. It ends with a decision and who writes what. Nobody grabs the pen to sketch their own front page.

3How it solves it prepare the board, hold the marker

The second session, a week later, took sixty minutes. Ana wrote the question and a legend before anyone arrived, drew three options side by side as people described them, and kept the only marker. Joost explained the morning van routes, Eli the courier model, Mo the Korenwolf windows. Decision: vans become a courier type in Dispatch; Eli writes ADR 0015, with the photo attached.

  1. Write the question first

    On the board before people arrive, as one sentence that can be answered.

  2. Add a legend

    What boxes, circles and arrow styles mean today.

  3. Invite the right few

    The people who know the facts and the people who'll build it; six to eight.

  4. Hold the marker

    One facilitator draws; others talk. Hand it over on purpose, not by grabbing.

  5. Options side by side

    At least two, drawn the same way, so they can be compared.

  6. End with a decision box

    What, who, by when; photo into the ADR or decision log.

Vocabulary for the exam

Facilitator
The person running the session and holding the marker.
Timebox
A fixed length; when it ends, decide or schedule the next step.
Legend
What the shapes and lines on the board mean.
Parking lot
A corner for side topics, to handle later.
Decision box
What was decided, the owner and the date.
Record
The ADR, RFC or log line the photo becomes.

tools: a real whiteboard and good markers; for remote sessions, Miro, FigJam or Excalidraw with one person drawing. A phone camera and the ADR folder.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Decisions in an hour instead of a messy afternoon.
  • Everyone sees the same picture, drawn one way.
  • Options compared fairly, side by side.
  • A record: the photo and the ADR.
  • Quiet experts get heard: the facilitator asks them.

What we lose

  • Preparation takes half an hour.
  • The facilitator can steer too much.
  • Some people need to draw to think.
  • Remote sessions are harder to keep focused.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • No question

    Everyone solves a different problem. Fix → write one answerable question on the board first.

  • Many markers

    Five diagrams on top of each other. Fix → one marker, held by the facilitator.

  • No timebox

    It ends when the room is booked. Fix → sixty minutes, then decide or schedule.

  • The photo is the record

    A blurry board nobody can read later. Fix → write the decision up as an ADR, with the photo attached.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Design questions with several people who know part of the answer.
  • Comparing options quickly.
  • Kick-offs for RFCs.
  • When chat threads go round in circles.

Skip it when…

  • Decisions one person can make with an ADR.
  • Big domain discovery: use event storming.

8Self-check quiz

What goes on the board before anyone arrives?

The question, a legend, and space for options side by side.

Why one marker?

So there's one drawing, made in one style, of what the room agrees.

How should a session end?

With a decision box: what, who, by when.

What happens to the photo?

It's attached to the ADR, RFC or decision log line that records the decision.

try it

For your next design discussion, prepare the board: one question, a legend, three empty option boxes. Hold the only marker, set a 60-minute timer, and end with a decision box. Write the ADR within a day with the photo attached.

remember!!

One question, one marker, a decision box at the bottom.
