Architecture One-Pager

TL;DR

Most people who need to understand the architecture have ten minutes, not an afternoon. Give them one page: the purpose (what the system is for, in business terms), a small picture (C4 context), the five decisions that shape everything, today's numbers, the top risks, and what's next. Link the details; never paste them. Update it every quarter, and use it as the cover page for every longer document.

  • Family: documents
  • Origin: architecture practice; “architecture on a page” (Gregor Hohpe, Ruth Malan and others)
  • Lives in: docs/architecture/README.md, the first page anyone opens

1The situation Larder, 10 November 2026

Before the November board meeting, Femke needs to answer one question: is the platform ready for four more cities in 2027? Ana has a good answer, spread across a 30-page architecture document, fifteen ADRs and four diagrams. Femke has ten minutes before the board pack is due. She reads the table of contents, then calls Ana.

  • Long documents are written for completeness; readers need the gist first.
  • Executives, new hires and partners each need the same short summary.
  • Without a summary, every reader builds their own, differently.

2Real-life analogy the property sheet

An estate agent's property sheet has one photo, the key facts (rooms, size, price), the things to know (new roof, busy street, garden faces north) and the next step: book a viewing. One sheet tells you whether to look further. The survey report exists too, for the people who get that far.

3How it solves it six boxes, linked details

Ana wrote it that afternoon from what already existed: the drivers from Vol. 1, the ADR list, the latest dashboard numbers and the risk list. It went into the board pack as one page; the board asked about one risk (StockPilot) and nothing else. It now sits at the top of docs/architecture/ and is the first link in every new hire's onboarding.

  1. Write the purpose in business words

    What the system is for, from the drivers: “30-minute groceries, six cities by end 2027”.

  2. Add a small picture

    The C4 context diagram, shrunk; no more than eight boxes.

  3. Pick five decisions

    The ones that shape everything, with ADR numbers to follow.

  4. Show today's numbers

    Volume, peak, quality, cost, from the dashboards, with a date.

  5. Name the top three risks and what's next

    With owners and dates.

  6. Link, don't paste; update quarterly

    Every box links to the detail. Date the page.

Vocabulary for the exam

One-pager
The whole architecture summarised on one page.
Purpose
What the system is for, in business terms.
Key decision
A choice that shapes most of the system.
Fitness numbers
The few metrics that show the architecture is working.
Cover page
The one-pager used as the start of longer documents.
Progressive disclosure
Short first, details one click away.

# Larder platform · on one page                         Ana · 10 Nov 2026 · quarterly

PURPOSE    Groceries in 30 minutes (or a chosen hour), six cities by end 2027.
PICTURE    [context diagram: customers · pickers · couriers · offices → Larder ·
            StockPilot · PSP ×2 · billing · helpdesk]
DECISIONS  1 Modular monolith; services only where needed (0002, Dispatch)
           2 A city is configuration, not code (0003)
           3 Picker app works 30 min offline (0004)
           4 Orders leave via an outbox to Kafka (0006, 0007)
           5 Dispatch assigns with routing Engine A (0012)
TODAY      ~5,800 orders/day · peak 2.6 orders/s · express on time 94 % AMS,
           92 % RTM · cost per order €3.60 (target €2.80 by end 2027)
RISKS      StockPilot renewal (decide by 31 Dec) · 3 hard-coded Rotterdam
           settings (TD-1) · one Postgres at 5× peak (load test in Jan)
NEXT       Utrecht (Apr 2027) · warehouse decision (Dec) · C4 + arc42 docs (Nov)
More       arc42 docs · ADR log · views · runbooks

tools: a Markdown page in the repo, rendered in the wiki or docs site; a one-slide version for decks. The C4 context diagram can be embedded from the diagrams-as-code source.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Read in ten minutes by anyone.
  • Same summary for everyone: board, new hires, partners.
  • Forces priorities: only five decisions fit.
  • Entry point to the detailed documents.
  • Quarterly snapshot of how the architecture is doing.

What we lose

  • Hard to write short; easy to cram.
  • Hides nuance some readers need.
  • Goes stale without the quarterly update.
  • Numbers need a source that's kept current.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Cramming

    Two pages in small type. Fix → five decisions, three risks; link the rest.

  • Technology list

    “Python, Postgres, Kafka.” Fix → decisions and why, not just tools.

  • No numbers

    A page of claims. Fix → today's volume, quality and cost, dated.

  • Never updated

    June's page in November. Fix → date it and update it every quarter.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Board packs, investor questions, partner due diligence.
  • Onboarding: the first page a new hire reads.
  • The cover page of every longer architecture document.
  • Quarterly architecture check-ins.

Skip it when…

  • Detailed design discussions: use the full views.
  • Single-component changes: an ADR is enough.

8Self-check quiz

What six things go on the one-pager?

Purpose, a small picture, five key decisions, today's numbers, top risks, what's next.

Why link rather than paste?

To keep it to one page; the details live in the ADRs, views and arc42 documents.

How often should it be updated?

Every quarter, with a date on the page.

Who reads it?

Executives, the board, new hires, partners: anyone with ten minutes.

try it

Write your system's one-pager in 90 minutes: purpose, a picture with at most eight boxes, five decisions, four numbers, three risks, what's next. Give it to someone outside engineering and ask them to explain the system back to you in two minutes.

remember!!

One sheet, like a property listing. The survey report is one click away.
