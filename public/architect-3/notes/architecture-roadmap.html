Architecture Roadmap

TL;DR

A list of architecture work isn't a plan. An architecture roadmap describes a few transition states between today and where the business needs to be, each one a working platform, each tied to a business milestone (a city launch, a contract), each with what must be true by then and the decisions behind it. It answers the board's question, “what has to change, and when?”, and it tells teams why their item is in this quarter.

  • Origin: TOGAF transition architectures; capability-based planning
  • Lives in: one page linked from the one-pager; reviewed quarterly
  • Pairs with: architecture one-pager, now / next / later roadmap, migration planning

1The situation Larder, 2 February 2027

At the February board meeting, an investor asks Ana: what has to change in the platform for four new cities and a new country this year? Ana's architecture backlog has 60 items: TD-1, the WMS B migration, Belgian VAT, French in the apps, load tests, vans… all real, none in any order. The board sees a pile, not a plan, and teams can't tell which of their items matter for which launch.

  • Architecture work is listed, not sequenced.
  • Nothing ties technical work to the business milestones it enables.
  • Big-bang targets (“the 2027 architecture”) hide what must be true by each launch.

2Real-life analogy renovating while you live in it

A family renovating the house they live in doesn't rip everything out at once. Roof first (nothing else is safe without it), then the kitchen, then the bathrooms, then the garden. After every stage the house is livable, and each stage prepares the next. The plan isn't the list of jobs; it's the order and the livable states in between.

3How it solves it a few states, each tied to a launch

Ana drew four states, each tied to a city launch (planning dates, not commitments): T1 for Utrecht in April (a city is fully configuration, vans in Dispatch), T2 for The Hague in June and Antwerp in September (WMS B, French and Dutch, Belgian VAT and Bancontact refunds), T3 for Eindhoven in November (load-tested for 40,000 orders a day). Under each, what must be true, and the ADRs behind it. The 60 items found their homes; eleven belonged to no state and were parked.

  1. Describe today honestly

    T0: what the platform is and can't do yet.

  2. Anchor states to milestones

    City launches, contracts, peaks: dates the business already has.

  3. Write what must be true

    For each state: capabilities and quality targets, not tasks.

  4. Link the decisions

    ADRs and debt items that make each state possible.

  5. Sort the backlog under states

    Items that fit no state get questioned or parked.

  6. Review quarterly

    Move dates with the business; keep each state a working platform.

Vocabulary for the exam

Transition state
A planned, working version of the architecture on the way to the target.
Target architecture
Where the business needs the platform to be.
Baseline
The architecture as it is today (T0).
Milestone
A business date a state is tied to.
Capability
Something the platform must be able to do.
Planning date
A date to plan by, not a promise.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • The board sees a plan, tied to launches it knows.
  • Teams see why their item is this quarter.
  • Each step is safe: a working platform every time.
  • Backlog clean-up: items with no state get questioned.
  • Early warning when a state slips behind its launch.

What we lose

  • Dates move with the business; the page needs care.
  • Hard to place enabling work with no visible milestone.
  • Can look like a promise if the planning dates aren't labelled.
  • Coarse: states hide the weekly detail.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A big-bang target

    “The 2027 architecture” with nothing in between. Fix → two to four working states, tied to milestones.

  • Tasks, not states

    A Gantt chart of jobs. Fix → describe what must be true, then sort tasks under it.

  • Dates as promises

    Planning dates read as commitments. Fix → label them; review quarterly.

  • No parking

    Every item squeezed into a state. Fix → items that fit no state get questioned or parked.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Multi-quarter change tied to business milestones.
  • Board and investor questions about the platform.
  • Migrations and new markets.
  • When the architecture backlog has become a pile.

Skip it when…

  • Small systems with a single next step.
  • Week-to-week planning: that's the teams' backlogs.

8Self-check quiz

What's a transition state?

A working version of the architecture, on the way to the target, tied to a milestone.

What goes under each state?

What must be true by then, and the ADRs and debt items that make it so.

What happens to items that fit no state?

They're questioned or parked.

Why tie states to launches?

So technical work is anchored to dates the business already cares about.

try it

Write your platform's today (T0) in three lines. Pick the next two business milestones and, for each, write three things that must be true of the platform by then. Sort your architecture backlog under them and count what fits nowhere.
