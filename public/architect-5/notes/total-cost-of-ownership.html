Total Cost of Ownership

TL;DR

Total cost of ownership (TCO) adds up every cost of an option over several years: licences or usage, hosting, people to run and upgrade it, integration, training, support, and the cost of leaving. It's the only fair way to compare build, buy, open source and managed services, because the visible price is rarely the biggest line. Use the forecast volume, not today's.

  • Family: cost & balance
  • Origin: Gartner (1980s), IT procurement practice
  • Lives in: a TCO sheet attached to the ADR

1The situation Larder, 7 February 2028

Routing Engine A (open source, self-hosted, ADR 0012) has been on watch since 2026. Joost is tired of upgrade weekends, and a vendor offers a managed routing service. The debate is about monthly prices: Engine A is “free”, the service is about €4,000 a month. Nobody has counted the people, the upgrades, or how the service's fee grows with orders.

  • Licence prices hide the biggest costs.
  • Usage-based fees grow with the business.
  • People's time is real money, even if salaried.

2Real-life analogy the cheap printer

A cheap printer costs €60; its ink costs €15 a month and it jams every week. An expensive one costs €300 with cheap ink. Over three years, the price on the box is the smallest part, and the jams cost an evening each.

3How it solves it every cost, three years, forecast volume

Ana and Marco built a three-year sheet at forecast volume. Engine A: hosting ≈ €72k, half an engineer ≈ €135k, map data and upgrades ≈ €36k: ≈ €243k. The service: usage fees rising with orders ≈ €230k, integration ≈ €40k, a tenth of an engineer ≈ €27k: ≈ €297k, plus an exit cost if prices rise. Decision: keep Engine A, but fund the half engineer explicitly (Joost's weekends were the hidden cost), and review if volume or incidents change.

  1. Pick the period

    Usually three to five years.

  2. List every cost

    Licences, usage, hosting, people, integration, training, support, exit.

  3. Use forecast volume

    Not today's; usage fees grow.

  4. Price people honestly

    Fraction of an engineer × loaded cost.

  5. Add risk and exit

    Price changes, lock-in, migration cost.

  6. Compare totals, then qualities

    Cost isn't the only criterion.

Vocabulary for the exam

TCO
Total cost of ownership over a period.
Loaded cost
Salary plus taxes, benefits, equipment and overhead.
Usage-based pricing
Fees that grow with volume.
Exit cost
What it costs to leave an option later.
Opex vs capex
Running costs vs one-off investment.
Hidden cost
A real cost not on any invoice (people's time).

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Fair comparisons across build, buy and open source.
  • Hidden costs visible: people, upgrades.
  • Growth priced in.
  • Finance trusts the numbers.
  • Decisions with review points.

What we lose

  • Estimates of the future.
  • People costs are sensitive to discuss.
  • Takes a day or two.
  • Cost can crowd out other qualities.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Licence only

    “Open source is free.” Fix → count people and hosting.

  • Today's volume

    Usage fees at current orders. Fix → forecast volume.

  • Forgetting exit

    No cost to leave assumed. Fix → price migration and lock-in.

  • Cost only

    Cheapest wins, whatever it does. Fix → compare qualities too.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Build vs buy vs open source decisions.
  • Renewals and vendor changes.
  • Comparing hosting models.
  • When “free” software takes a lot of time.

Skip it when…

  • Small, cheap tools with no lock-in.
