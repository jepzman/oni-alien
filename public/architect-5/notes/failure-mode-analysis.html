Failure Mode Analysis

TL;DR

Failure mode analysis (FMEA) lists, for each part of a design, how it can fail (not just “down”: slow, wrong, partial, late), what happens then, and scores severity, likelihood and how hard it is to detect. Multiplying the three ranks the risks; you fix the top ones first. It moves attention from the failure everyone remembers to the quiet ones nobody has looked at.

  • Family: availability
  • Origin: FMEA, US military (1949), then aerospace and automotive
  • Lives in: a table per critical path, reviewed before peaks
  • Pairs with: availability maths, risk-storming, circuit breaker

1The situation Larder, 6 December 2027

With Christmas three weeks away, the risk talk goes straight to last year's database failover, which now takes under two minutes. Nobody looks at the quiet failures: a payment provider that slows down without failing, push notifications arriving late, a store's Wi-Fi at peak. Teams fear what happened last time, not what might happen next.

  • “Down” is only one way to fail; slow and wrong are worse.
  • Recent incidents get all the attention.
  • Failures that are hard to detect last longest.

2Real-life analogy the pilot's checklist

A pilot's checklist comes from how planes actually fail: for each part, what can go wrong, what happens then, how likely it is, and how you'd notice. The worst combinations (severe, likely, hard to notice) get a drill in the simulator, whether or not they happened last month.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the project
Each part of the planeEach part of the checkout path
What can go wrongSlow, down, late, wrong, partial

A drill in the simulatorA circuit breaker, switch-over and an alert, tested

3How it solves it severity × likelihood × detection

Ana, Cho and Eli walked the checkout path part by part, scoring each failure mode 1–10 for severity, likelihood and detection. Top: the payment provider slowing down (216): threads wait, checkout stalls, no alarm fires. Then delayed pushes (140) and store Wi-Fi (90); the famous failover came fourth (48). Cho added a 3-second timeout, a circuit breaker and automatic switch-over to the second provider, plus a latency alert. On 17 December provider A slowed for 22 minutes; the breaker switched in 40 seconds.

  1. Pick a critical path

    Checkout, picking, dispatch: one at a time.

  2. List each part's failure modes

    Down, slow, wrong, late, partial, duplicated.

  3. Write the effect

    What the customer, picker or courier sees.

  4. Score S, L and D

    1–10 each; multiply into a priority number.

  5. Act on the top few

    Reduce severity, likelihood, or improve detection.

  6. Re-score after fixes

    And before each peak season.

Vocabulary for the exam

Failure mode
One specific way a part can fail.
Effect
What happens downstream when it does.
Severity
How bad the effect is.
Detection
How hard the failure is to notice (10 = invisible).
RPN
Risk priority number: severity × likelihood × detection.
Grey failure
A part that's partly broken: slow or flaky, not down.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Quiet failures found: slow, late, wrong.
  • Attention by risk, not memory.
  • Detection improved, not just prevention.
  • A ranked list for limited time.
  • Proved on 17 December.

What we lose

  • Scores are subjective.
  • Time-consuming for long paths.
  • Misses combinations of failures.
  • Goes stale as the design changes.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Only “down”

    Every row says “service unavailable”. Fix → slow, late, wrong, partial too.

  • Recency bias

    Last incident ranked first by default. Fix → score every mode the same way.

  • No detection score

    Silent failures rank low. Fix → make invisible failures score high.

  • Write and forget

    A table from two years ago. Fix → re-score before peaks and after changes.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Critical paths before peaks or launches.
  • After an incident, to look beyond it.
  • New designs with external dependencies.
  • Deciding where to add timeouts and fallbacks.

Skip it when…

  • Non-critical internal tools.

8Self-check quiz

What are the three scores in FMEA?

Severity, likelihood and detection (how hard it is to notice).

What was Larder's top failure mode?

The payment provider slowing down without failing.

Why score detection?

Failures nobody notices last longest and do most harm.

What happened on 17 December?

try it

Take your most important path. For each part, write three failure modes (down, slow, wrong), their effect, and S, L, D scores. Fix the top one, and add an alert if its detection score is above 5.
