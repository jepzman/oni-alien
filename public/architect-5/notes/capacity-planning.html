Capacity Planning

TL;DR

Capacity planning forecasts demand, checks every resource against it, and adds capacity before the first one runs out. For each resource, write three numbers: usage at today's peak, the multiple of today's peak where it runs out, and the lead time to add more. Act on the smallest gap and the longest lead time first, with dates, and keep headroom (around 30 %) for surprises.

  • Family: size & speed
  • Origin: operations and SRE practice; queueing basics
  • Lives in: a capacity sheet reviewed monthly, and before every peak
  • Pairs with: back-of-the-envelope estimation, load profiles, cost-aware architecture

1The situation Larder, 18 October 2027

Last year Larder's database was resized in a hurry on 16 December, two days before the Christmas peak; the resize itself needed a failover. Every resource has a different lead time (minutes for VMs, weeks for a connection pooler, a quiet window for a database resize), and nobody tracked how close each was to its limit. The estimate says the database matters again this year.

  • The first resource to run out is often not the one people watch.
  • Slow-to-change resources get found last.
  • Changes made just before a peak are risks themselves.

2Real-life analogy next September's classrooms

A school counts next September's children and checks each resource: desks (a week to order), teachers (three months to hire), classrooms (a year to build). It starts with the one that takes longest to add, not the one that runs out first, and keeps a few spare places for late arrivals.

3How it solves it used, out at, lead time, then dates

Ana and Eli filled one table. Database connections: 310 of 400 at Friday peak, out at 1.3× today, a pooler takes two weeks. Database CPU: 55 %, out at 1.8×, a resize needs a failover in a quiet window. VMs, Kafka and Dispatch had room or autoscaled. December's forecast is 2.2×, so: pooler by 1 November, a read replica for browsing by 8 November, the primary one size up on 15 November, and a change freeze from 13 December. Extra cost: about €2,100 a month.

  1. Forecast demand

    By month, from the business plan; peak, not average.

  2. List every resource

    Compute, database (CPU, connections, storage), queues, third-party limits.

  3. Measure usage at peak

    From monitoring, at the real peak hour.

  4. Find where each runs out

    As a multiple of today's peak, with headroom.

  5. Note the lead time

    Minutes, weeks, a quiet window, a contract.

  6. Plan with dates

    Slowest and smallest-gap first; freeze before the peak.

Vocabulary for the exam

Capacity
The most load a resource can handle while meeting its targets.
Utilisation
How much of a resource is used at a moment.
Headroom
Spare capacity kept for surprises.
Lead time
How long it takes to add capacity.
Saturation
When a resource is full and work starts queueing.
Change freeze
No risky changes in the weeks before a peak.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • No emergency resizes before peaks.
  • The first bottleneck found in time.
  • Lead times respected.
  • Spending planned, not panicked.
  • Freeze dates everyone knows.

What we lose

  • Forecasts are wrong, sometimes badly.
  • Headroom costs money.
  • Needs good peak data.
  • Monthly upkeep.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Watching averages

    50 % average, 100 % at peak. Fix → measure at the peak hour.

  • Forgetting hidden limits

    Connections, quotas, vendor rate limits. Fix → list every limit, not just CPU.

  • Ignoring lead times

    A two-week job started two days before. Fix → plan slowest first.

  • No headroom

    Planned to exactly 100 %. Fix → keep around 30 % spare.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Before peak seasons and launches.
  • Monthly, for growing systems.
  • When a forecast changes (a new city, a new client).
  • Before signing capacity-based contracts.

Skip it when…

  • Fully serverless parts with high limits (check the quotas once).
