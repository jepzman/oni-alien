When to Go Multi-Region

TL;DR

Running in more than one cloud region protects against a whole region failing, but it is one of the most expensive and complex steps in architecture: double infrastructure, data replication, harder deployments, more people. Decide with numbers: what a region outage would cost, how often it happens, what a second region costs, and any legal need. If the answer is “not yet”, agree the triggers that would change it, and keep a cheaper fallback (backups or a pilot light) meanwhile.

1The situation Larder, 24 January 2028

ADR 0017 promised to revisit a second region when Antwerp opened. Antwerp has been live for three months; Femke assumes the answer is yes: Larder is international now, and two regions sound safer. Nobody has put a number on what a region outage would cost, how often one happens, or what a second region would cost every month.

  • “Safer” is easy to agree with and expensive to buy.
  • Multi-region changes deployments, data and on-call for good.
  • A decision without triggers gets re-argued every quarter.

2Real-life analogy the second car

A family wonders whether to buy a second car. It costs every month (insurance, parking, upkeep) whether it's driven or not. They agree: we'll buy one when both of us commute by car, or the first car leaves us stranded twice in a year. Until then, there's a taxi number on the fridge.

3How it solves it the cost, the loss, the law, the triggers

Ana put four numbers on a page. A second active region: about €9,000 a month plus two engineers, roughly €300k a year. Region outages: one incident in two years, 34 minutes at night; a peak hour is worth about €120k of orders, so the expected loss is well under €100k a year. Lena confirmed Belgian rules don't require another region. ADR 0026: stay in one region across three zones, add a pilot light for Tier 1 in a second region (≈ €1,200 a month, RTO 4 hours), and revisit when any of four triggers fires.

  1. Price the second region

    Infrastructure, data transfer, people, slower delivery.

  2. Price the outage

    Revenue per hour at peak, contract penalties, reputation.

  3. Estimate how often

    Your provider's history in your region.

  4. Check the law and contracts

    Data residency, client requirements.

  5. Choose the cheapest fallback that fits

    Backups, pilot light, warm standby, active-active.

  6. Write the triggers

    Numbers that would reopen the decision.

Vocabulary for the exam

Region
A geographic area of a cloud provider, with several zones.
Availability zone
A separate data centre within a region.
Active-active
Both regions serve traffic all the time.
Pilot light
A minimal copy (mostly data) ready to be scaled up.
Warm standby
A smaller running copy that can take over.
Trigger
A condition agreed in advance that reopens a decision.

# 26. Stay in one region (three zones), pilot light in a second, with triggers
Status: Accepted · 2028-01-24 · Driver: Ana · with Eli, Marco, Lena, Femke · revisits ADR 0017

Second active region   ≈ €9,000/month + 2 engineers ≈ €300k/year; slower deploys; data replication
Expected outage loss   1 regional incident in 2 years (34 min, 03:10, Mar 2027)
                       peak hour ≈ €120k of orders → expected loss well under €100k/year
Law and contracts      EU only (GDPR); Belgium adds no residency rule; Korenwolf: 99.8 %/month

Decision   one region, three zones (as now) + pilot light in Paris for Tier 1:
           database replica + infrastructure as code, ≈ €1,200/month, RTO 4 h, RPO 5 min
Triggers   any one → reopen: 60,000 orders/day · a region outage > 1 h ·
           a client contract requiring regional failover · a city outside the EU

tools: the cloud provider's status history, the availability sheet and TCO model, cross-region replication and infrastructure as code for the pilot light.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • No €300k a year spent on a feeling.
  • A cheaper fallback in place now.
  • Triggers end the re-arguing.
  • Simpler operations for the team.
  • Legal question settled with Lena.

What we lose

  • A region outage means hours, not minutes.
  • The pilot light must be drilled too.
  • Estimates of rare events are shaky.
  • Femke's instinct overruled by numbers.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Multi-region by default

    “Serious companies do it.” Fix → price the outage and the cure.

  • Active-active or nothing

    No middle option considered. Fix → backups, pilot light, warm standby.

  • No triggers

    Re-argued every quarter. Fix → write the conditions that reopen it.

  • Untested fallback

    A pilot light never lit. Fix → drill it with the DR tiers.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When leaders ask for “more resilience”.
  • Expanding into new countries.
  • Before signing contracts with regional failover clauses.
  • Revisiting an earlier region decision.

Skip it when…

  • When law or a contract already requires it: then decide how, not whether.

8Self-check quiz

What four things decide multi-region?

The cost of a second region, the cost and frequency of outages, and legal or contract needs.

What did Larder choose?

One region across three zones, a pilot light in a second region, and four triggers.

What is a pilot light?

A minimal copy, mostly data, ready to be scaled up in another region.

Why write triggers?

So the decision is reopened when the facts change, not every quarter.

try it

Estimate what one hour of your platform being down at peak would cost, how often your region has had a serious outage, and what a second region would cost a year. Write a one-paragraph decision with at least two triggers.
