Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation

TL;DR

Back-of-the-envelope estimation turns a few known numbers (users, orders, requests per order, bytes per record) into rough totals in minutes, using round numbers and simple multiplication. The aim isn't precision; it's to find which part of the design matters and which doesn't, by an order of magnitude, before anyone argues or builds. Write the assumptions down so others can check and change them.

  • Family: size & speed
  • Origin: Fermi estimation; Jeff Dean's “numbers everyone should know”
  • Lives in: a one-page estimate next to the design or ADR
  • Pairs with: capacity planning, load profiles, quality attribute scenarios

1The situation Larder, 4 October 2027

Antwerp opens on 18 October and Eindhoven in November. At the leadership meeting Femke asks: “Will Christmas hold?” Eli says it should; Ben worries about Kafka; Joost remembers last Christmas's 38-minute outage. Nobody has a number, so the debate is all feelings, and the meeting ends with “let's keep an eye on it”.

  • Opinions about scale can't be compared without numbers.
  • Worry goes to the familiar parts, not the risky ones.
  • A full load test takes weeks; the question needs an answer this week.

2Real-life analogy the road trip on a napkin

A family plans a road trip on a napkin: 900 km at about 100 an hour is nine hours of driving; the car does 600 km on a tank, so one fuel stop; two drivers can swap there. Nobody needs a route planner to see that fuel isn't the problem, but nine hours of driving is.

3How it solves it five known numbers, multiplied out

Ana took twenty minutes and one page. 27,000 orders a day forecast for December; Friday's peak second carries about 0.045 % of the day: 12 orders a second; designed for five times that (ASR 3): 60. At about 400 API calls per order, 24,000 requests a second, 80 % browsing a cache can serve. At about 30 writes per order, 1,800 database writes a second against roughly 2,400 measured on the current primary. Dispatch: 180 messages a second, trivial. The answer for Femke: the database and checkout, not Kafka.

  1. Write the question

    “Can checkout take Christmas at 5× Friday peak?”

  2. List known numbers

    Orders, peak share, requests per order, writes per order, sizes.

  3. Use round numbers

    One or two significant figures; powers of ten.

  4. Multiply it out

    Per day → per second → per component.

  5. Compare with limits

    Measured or documented limits of each part.

  6. Write the assumptions

    So anyone can check and change one number.

Vocabulary for the exam

Fermi estimate
A quick estimate from rough, reasoned assumptions.
Order of magnitude
A factor of ten: 100 vs 1,000.
Peak factor
How much busier the peak is than the average.
Design point
The load the design must handle (here 5× Friday peak).
Throughput
Work done per second: orders, requests, writes.
Headroom
The gap between the expected load and the limit.

# Will Christmas 2027 hold?  (Ana, 4 Oct 2027 · round numbers · check my assumptions)

Orders/day, December (5 cities + Eindhoven)     27,000          forecast, Marco's model
Friday peak second = 0.045 % of the day         ≈ 12 orders/s   from Nov 2026: 2.6/s at 5,800/day
Design point (ASR 3: 5× Friday peak)            ≈ 60 orders/s
API requests per order (browse, basket, pay)    ≈ 400   →  24,000 req/s, ~80 % browsing (cacheable)
DB writes per order (order, lines, events…)     ≈ 30    →   1,800 writes/s   vs ≈ 2,400 measured  ← WATCH
Kafka: ≈ 10 events per order                    →     600 msg/s    vs > 50,000 capacity
Dispatch: 900 couriers × 1 update / 5 s         →     180 msg/s    trivial

Answer: the database (writes, connections) and the checkout path. Not Kafka, not Dispatch.

tools: a spreadsheet or a markdown page; latency and throughput reference tables; your own monitoring for the measured limits.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Answers in minutes, not weeks.
  • Worry goes to the right part.
  • Assumptions in the open, easy to challenge.
  • A shared number ends feelings-based debates.
  • Tells you what to test properly next.

What we lose

  • Rough: an order of magnitude, not a promise.
  • Only as good as the inputs.
  • Misses non-linear effects (locks, queues).
  • Can look more certain than it is.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • False precision

    “1,847.3 writes per second.” Fix → round numbers, ranges.

  • Averages only

    Daily average hides the peak. Fix → always compute the peak second.

  • Hidden assumptions

    Nobody can check the maths. Fix → write each assumption on its own line.

  • Treating it as a test

    “The estimate says it's fine.” Fix → use it to choose what to test.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Early in a design, before choosing technology.
  • When someone asks “will it scale?”
  • Before a peak season or a launch.
  • Checking a vendor's or a team's claims.

Skip it when…

  • When you already have real load test results for the same question.

8Self-check quiz

What is a back-of-the-envelope estimate for?

Finding which part of a design matters, by an order of magnitude, quickly.

Why compute the peak second, not the daily average?

Systems fail at the peak; averages hide it.

What did Ana's estimate point at?

The database's writes and the checkout path, not Kafka or Dispatch.

Why write the assumptions down?

So others can check them and change one number at a time.

try it

Take your system's busiest day. From orders (or users) per day, work out the peak second, the requests and database writes per second, and compare each with a measured limit. Write each assumption on its own line and circle the smallest headroom.

remember!!

Plan the trip on a napkin: find out whether it's the fuel or the driving hours.
