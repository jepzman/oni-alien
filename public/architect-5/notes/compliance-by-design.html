Compliance by Design

TL;DR

Compliance by design turns legal and regulatory rules into platform features (checks, events, configuration, jobs) instead of manual procedures and yearly audits. Each rule gets an owner, a feature that enforces it, and automatic evidence that it works. New markets then mean new configuration, not new scrambles. It's privacy by design (GDPR Article 25) applied to every rule.

1The situation Larder, 8 November 2027

Antwerp brings new rules: structured e-invoices for Belgian business customers, beer and wine from 16 but spirits from 18 (the Netherlands: 18 for all), and more deletion requests. Today compliance is a yearly scramble: Sam's team deletes customers by hand in nine systems (about three days a month), and Lena collects audit evidence from screenshots.

  • Manual procedures fail quietly as volume grows.
  • Each new country multiplies the procedures.
  • Evidence collected once a year proves little.

2Real-life analogy the lift that won't move

A modern lift isn't kept safe by an inspector hunting for problems once a year. The lift itself won't move with the doors open or overloaded, and it logs every service and fault. The rule is built into the machine; the inspector reads the log.

3How it solves it each rule a feature, with its own evidence

Ana and Lena listed the rules and made each a feature. Age checks: the courier app asks for an ID check at the door for age-restricted items, using the city's rule (16 or 18), and stores only “checked: yes/no”. Erasure: one customer.erasure.requested event all nine systems act on, with a confirmation report. Retention: per data class, as configuration, with a nightly job. E-invoices: Belgian business invoices sent by the billing service in the required format. A compliance page is generated weekly for Lena. Deletions went from three days by hand to four hours, automatically.

  1. List the rules

    With legal: law, contracts, standards; per country.

  2. Give each an owner

    Who answers for it.

  3. Turn each into a feature

    A check, an event, a setting or a job.

  4. Make it configuration per market

    Age 16 or 18 is a city setting.

  5. Produce evidence automatically

    Logs and reports, not screenshots.

  6. Review with legal quarterly

    New rules, changed rules, failures.

Vocabulary for the exam

Compliance by design
Building rules into the system instead of procedures around it.
Privacy by design
Data protection built in from the start (GDPR Art. 25).
Right to erasure
A person's right to have their data deleted (GDPR Art. 17).
Retention policy
How long each kind of data is kept.
E-invoicing
Invoices in a structured, machine-readable format.
Evidence
Records that show a control works.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Rules enforced every time, not remembered.
  • New markets become configuration.
  • Evidence for free: Lena reads a page.
  • Days of manual work saved monthly.
  • Less personal data stored (yes/no, not IDs).

What we lose

  • Engineering effort up front.
  • Rules change; features must follow.
  • Legal and engineering must work closely.
  • Bugs in a control fail at scale.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Procedures first

    A wiki page instead of a check. Fix → make the system enforce it.

  • Storing too much

    Scanning and keeping ID cards. Fix → store the fact (yes/no), not the document.

  • Hard-coded rules

    Age 18 in the code. Fix → per market configuration.

  • No evidence

    It works, nobody can show it. Fix → generate evidence automatically.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Entering a new country or regulated market.
  • When manual compliance work grows with volume.
  • Before audits and client questionnaires.
  • Designing features that touch personal data or regulated goods.

Skip it when…

  • One-off obligations that won't recur (a manual step may be fine).
