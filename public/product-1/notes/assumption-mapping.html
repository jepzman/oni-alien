Assumption Mapping

TL;DR

Every idea rests on things that must be true: customers want it (desirable), they can use it (usable), we can build it (feasible), and it works for the business (viable). List them, then place each on a grid of importance (does the idea fail if it's false?) against evidence (how sure are we?). Test the important, unproven ones first, as cheaply as you can, before building anything.

1The situation Larder, a team in love

The team picks approve in the app from the opportunity tree, and everyone loves it: nine votes out of nine. Ben is already sketching the API. But the idea stands on legs nobody has checked: that customers answer within two minutes, that pickers can wait two minutes without slowing the line, that push notifications reach customers, that charging a substitute's price difference is allowed. If the first one is false, the whole feature is wasted.

  • Excitement hides assumptions; the more a team likes an idea, the fewer it questions.
  • Teams test what's easy (can we build it?) instead of what's risky (will they use it?).
  • The riskiest assumption is usually found after launch, the most expensive moment.

2Real-life analogy the outdoor wedding

Planning an outdoor wedding, some beliefs matter more than others. “It won't rain” is very important and you have no evidence, so you book a tent. “The caterer can serve 120” is important, but they did it last month. “Guests like fish” barely matters. You spend your worry, and your money, on the top-left corner.

3How it solves it list, place, test the top-left

Brainstorm assumptions with the whole team using the four risk types as prompts, so you don't only list technical ones. Place each sticky on the grid together, arguing about evidence. The top-left quadrant is the test plan.

  1. Pick one idea

    One solution from the tree: “approve or reject a substitute in the app, within 2 minutes”.

  2. List assumptions by risk type

    Desirable, usable, feasible, viable. Ask “what must be true?” for each.

  3. Write them as statements

    “Customers answer within 2 minutes”, not “speed?”.

  4. Place them on the grid

    Importance (fails if false?) × evidence (how sure, and from what?).

  5. Test the top-left first

    The cheapest test that could prove it wrong: a concierge test, a prototype, a data query.

  6. Re-map after each test

    Evidence moves stickies right; failed ones change the idea.

Vocabulary for the exam

Assumption
Something that must be true for an idea to work.
Desirability
Customers want it.
Usability
Customers can figure out how to use it.
Feasibility
We can build and run it.
Viability
It works for the business: cost, legal, operations.
Leap of faith
An important assumption with no evidence yet.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Risk made visible: the leaps of faith are named.
  • Right test first: desirability and viability, not just feasibility.
  • Cheaper failures: a two-week test instead of a two-month build.
  • Healthy doubt without killing the team's enthusiasm.
  • Shared risk picture with operations and legal.

What we lose

  • Slows the start of a popular idea.
  • Evidence ratings are judgement calls.
  • Tests cost time, and some can't be done cheaply.
  • Can become a ritual if tests never change the idea.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Only feasibility

    The map lists APIs and databases. Fix → use all four risk types as prompts.

  • Vague stickies

    “Speed?” “Adoption?” Fix → write testable statements with a number.

  • Map, no tests

    A lovely grid; the build starts anyway. Fix → the top-left quadrant is the test plan, with dates.

  • Evidence inflation

    “We all feel strongly” counts as evidence. Fix → evidence means data, interviews or tests, with a source.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Before building any idea that takes more than a sprint.
  • When the team is very sure of an idea.
  • When operations, legal or finance are affected.
  • To choose between experiments.

Skip it when…

  • Small, reversible changes: ship and watch the data.
  • When the assumptions were already tested.
  • Pure maintenance with no customer or business risk.

8Self-check quiz

Name the four risk types.

Desirable, usable, feasible, viable.

What are the grid's two axes?

Importance (does the idea fail if it's false?) and evidence (how sure are we?).

Which assumptions do you test first?

Important ones with little evidence: the top-left quadrant.

What were Larder's two test-first assumptions?

Customers answer within 2 minutes; pickers can wait 2 minutes without slowing down.

try it

Take the idea your team is most excited about. In 15 minutes, write ten assumptions, at least two per risk type. Place them on the grid together. What's in the top-left corner, and what's the cheapest way to test it this week?
