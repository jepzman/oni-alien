Opportunity Solution Tree

TL;DR

Put one outcome at the top: a change in customer behaviour or a business number you can measure. Under it, the opportunities: customer needs and pains you found by listening, in the customer's words. Under each opportunity, several solutions. Under the solution you pick, the experiments that test it cheaply. The tree shows everyone why an idea is on the board, makes you compare ideas instead of falling for the first one, and turns stakeholder requests into branches you can weigh.

  • Family: framing the problem
  • Origin: Teresa Torres, Continuous Discovery Habits (2021); producttalk.org
  • Lives in: a wall or whiteboard, then a one-page tree the team updates weekly
  • Pairs with: customer interviews, assumption mapping, concierge MVP

1The situation Larder, Grace's third week

Three weeks in, Grace has four requests on her desk. Femke wants ML recipe ideas (an investor suggested it), Joost wants a faster picking screen, Sam wants an automatic refund button for substitutions so support can stop doing it by hand, and Priya wants a smarter substitute algorithm. All four are reasonable. None says which outcome it serves. Sam's button is already planned for the next sprint, and it would make refunds faster, not fewer: 6 % of orders would still end in a refund.

  • Backlogs fill up with solutions; the problem behind each one is never written down.
  • The idea with the loudest or most senior sponsor wins, not the one that moves the number.
  • Nobody compares options, so the first idea for a need is the one that gets built.

2Real-life analogy the doctor's notes

A good doctor doesn't start with the prescription. They agree a goal (blood pressure down to 130 by spring), find the likely causes by listening and examining (salt, stress, sleep), list a few treatments for each cause, and try one with a small test: two weeks, then measure. If it doesn't work, the notes show what to try next.

3How it solves it outcome → opportunities → solutions → experiments

The order matters. Pick the outcome first, with your manager. Fill opportunities only from what customers actually said or did; if it came from a stakeholder, it's a solution and goes lower down. Compare at least three solutions for the opportunity you pick. Then test the riskiest assumption of the chosen one before building anything.

  1. Agree one outcome

    A measurable change, owned by the team: “refund tickets 6 % → 2 % of orders by end Q2 2026”. Not a feature.

  2. Map opportunities from evidence

    From interviews, tickets and data. Write them as the customer would say them: “let me say yes or no to a swap”.

  3. Structure them

    Group similar needs; break big ones into smaller ones you could solve in weeks.

  4. Pick one opportunity

    The one that most affects the outcome and that the team can do something about now.

  5. Generate and compare solutions

    At least three. Stakeholder requests go here, next to the team's own ideas.

  6. Test before building

    List the chosen idea's assumptions, test the riskiest cheaply, and update the tree every week.

Vocabulary for the exam

Outcome
A measurable change in customer behaviour or a business result.
Output
Something the team ships: a feature, a screen, a button.
Opportunity
A customer need, pain or desire that, if met, moves the outcome.
Solution
One idea for meeting an opportunity.
Experiment
A cheap test of a solution's riskiest assumption.
Continuous discovery
Talking to customers every week while building, not only at the start.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Every idea has a why: you can trace it up to an outcome.
  • Options get compared: at least three solutions per opportunity.
  • Requests become branches, not orders: Sam's button is weighed, not just built.
  • Shared picture: the team, Priya and Femke see the same tree.
  • Faster learning: experiments sit right under the idea they test.

What we lose

  • Discovery work: interviews and data take time each week.
  • Hard conversations: parking a CEO's idea needs care.
  • Can grow wild: 40 opportunities and nothing chosen.
  • Needs a real outcome: a vague goal makes a useless tree.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Solutions disguised as opportunities

    “Customers need an approve button” sits on the opportunity row. Fix → write opportunities as the customer would say them; buttons are solutions.

  • An output as the outcome

    “Ship substitution approval by March” at the top. Fix → the top is a measured change: refund tickets, retention, time saved.

  • One solution per opportunity

    The first idea goes straight to the backlog. Fix → compare at least three, including “do nothing”.

  • Drawn once, never updated

    The tree from November still hangs on the wall in March. Fix → update it weekly as interviews and experiments come in.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A team owns an outcome and has more requests than capacity.
  • Stakeholders keep sending solutions instead of problems.
  • Before a big build: to check the idea is the best one for the need.
  • Running continuous discovery alongside delivery.

Skip it when…

  • Pure maintenance or compliance work with no choice to make.
  • When there's no outcome yet: agree one with your manager first.
  • Tiny fixes with an obvious cause and cure.

8Self-check quiz

What are the four levels of the tree?

Outcome, opportunities, solutions, experiments.

Where does a stakeholder's feature request go?

At the solution level, under the opportunity it serves, next to other ideas.

Why was Sam's auto-refund button not chosen?

It makes refunds faster, not fewer: it doesn't move the outcome of 6 % → 2 %.

How should opportunities be worded?

As the customer would say them, based on interviews and data, not as features.

try it

Write your team's current outcome on a sticky note. Under it, put the last five requests you received. For each one, ask “which customer need does this serve?” and write that need as a separate sticky above it. How many requests share a need, and how many serve none?

remember!!

Start from the outcome, not the prescription. Every idea hangs from a customer need.
