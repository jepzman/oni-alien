Customer Interviews

TL;DR

Talk to customers every week, and ask about what they actually did, not what they'd like or would do. “Tell me about the last time…” gets you a real story; “would you use…?” gets you a polite guess. Dig into each story (what happened next, how it felt, what they did about it), write one snapshot card per interview, and look for patterns across the cards. Twelve good conversations beat a survey of thousands when you don't yet know the right questions.

1The situation Larder, Grace's first week

On day one Priya hands Grace “the customer research”: a September survey where 31 people asked for “better substitutes”, and the in-app wish list (recipes, more brands, dark mode). Both are what customers think they want. Neither tells Grace what actually happened the last time a substitution went wrong, or why it hurt. So she books twelve interviews in her first two weeks.

  • Surveys and wish lists collect opinions and solutions, not problems.
  • People are bad at predicting what they'd do, and kind enough to say yes to your idea.
  • Without stories, the team builds what was asked for, not what was needed.

2Real-life analogy the detective

A good detective asks a witness “what did you see at nine o'clock?”, not “do you think the butler did it?”. Facts about one specific moment in the past are reliable. Opinions, theories and predictions are not, however sincerely they're given. And the detective keeps asking “and then?” until the story is complete.

3How it solves it one story, one card, then patterns

Each interview is 30 minutes about one real story. Grace interviews in pairs (with Sam or an engineer) so one person can listen and note. After each call they fill a snapshot card within the hour. Patterns only count once they show up across several cards.

  1. Recruit a mix

    Recent, frequent and lapsed customers; both express and slots. Offer a €15 voucher. Aim for 3–5 a week.

  2. Open with context

    Who they are, how often they order, for whom. Two minutes, no more.

  3. Ask for one specific story

    “Tell me about the last time something in your order was substituted.” Not “in general”.

  4. Dig, don't pitch

    “What happened next?”, “How did you feel?”, “What did you do?”. Never show your solution.

  5. Write a snapshot card

    Within the hour: quote, story, needs and pains, insights, one photo-worthy line.

  6. Synthesise weekly

    Lay out the cards; a pattern needs several independent stories behind it.

Vocabulary for the exam

Story-based interview
An interview built around one specific past experience.
Leading question
One that suggests the answer (“wouldn't it be great if…?”).
Snapshot card
A one-page summary of one interview.
Synthesis
Finding patterns across interviews.
Continuous interviewing
A small number of interviews every week, forever.
Recruiting screener
Questions used to pick the right people to interview.

# Interview snapshot · 16 Oct 2025 · “Rik”            interviewers: Grace, Sam

Who       38, two kids (one with a dairy allergy), Amsterdam-Oost
Orders    express 2–3 × a week, around 17:30; slots for the Sunday shop
Quote     “I don't mind a swap. I mind finding out on the doormat.”

The story (last time something was substituted)
  - Tue 7 Oct, ordered at 17:20: pasta, vegetables, lactose-free milk
  - 17:48 courier at the door. Milk swapped for regular milk. No message.
  - Couldn't use it (allergy). Went to the corner shop anyway: 20 minutes.
  - Wrote to support; refund two days later. “The refund wasn't the point.”

Needs / pains
  - Be asked before a swap, quickly (“I'm on my phone anyway at that time”)
  - Some items must never be swapped (allergy)
  - Know before the courier arrives

Insight   The pain is the surprise, not the substitute.
Follow up Ask the next 4 express customers about allergies.

tools: a calendar link, a video call and a €15 voucher. Recruit in the app or the order confirmation email; record with permission and keep recordings per your privacy policy (Lena signed off Larder's). Dovetail, Notion or a shared folder for snapshot cards.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Real problems, in customers' own words.
  • Surprises: the pain was the surprise, not the substitute.
  • Quotes that persuade stakeholders better than charts.
  • Cheap and fast: twelve calls in two weeks.
  • A habit: weekly interviews keep the team close to customers.

What we lose

  • Small samples: twelve people can mislead; check with data.
  • Skill needed: leading questions are easy to slip into.
  • Recruiting effort every week.
  • Privacy care: recordings and notes hold personal data.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Asking about the future

    “Would you use an approve button?” “Sure!” Fix → ask about the last time it happened; past behaviour is evidence.

  • Pitching your idea

    Half the call is a demo of the solution. Fix → never show the solution in a problem interview; test it later.

  • Only happy customers

    Everyone recruited orders twice a week and loves Larder. Fix → include lapsed and unhappy customers on purpose.

  • Notes in heads

    Great conversations, nothing written down. Fix → fill a snapshot card within the hour, every time.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Starting on a new product area or a new problem.
  • When the team argues about what customers want.
  • Every week, as a habit, while building.
  • Before a big bet, to check the problem is real.

Skip it when…

  • Questions data can answer better (how many, how often).
  • Testing usability of a screen: do a prototype test instead.
  • When you'd only be confirming a decision already made.

8Self-check quiz

Why ask about the last time instead of what they'd like?

Past behaviour is evidence; opinions and predictions are unreliable and often polite.

What three questions keep a story going?

What happened next? How did you feel? What did you do?

What is a snapshot card?

A one-page summary of a single interview: quote, story, needs, pains, insights.

What did Grace's interviews reveal about substitutions?

Customers don't mind substitutes; they mind not being asked, and finding out at the door.

try it

Book two 30-minute calls with customers this week. Write one opening question that starts with “Tell me about the last time…”. After each call, fill a snapshot card within the hour. Did either story surprise you?

remember!!

Ask what they did, not what they'd like. Then ask “and then?”
