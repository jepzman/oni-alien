Testable Hypotheses

TL;DR

Write every idea you test as a bet with terms set in advance: we believe [this change] for [these customers] will result in [this outcome]; we'll know we're right when [a measurable signal passes a threshold]. Add the test and the decision rule (pass, then what; fail, then what). Without a pass mark set beforehand, every result looks like success and nothing ever gets stopped.

  • Origin: the scientific method; Lean Startup (Eric Ries, 2011); Strategyzer test cards
  • Lives in: a hypothesis card per experiment, next to the assumption map
  • Pairs with: assumption mapping, concierge MVP, fake door test

1The situation Larder, last year's experiments

Larder ran experiments in 2025: “try SMS”, “try calling”, “try a banner”. None said what result would count as success. Afterwards, people looked at the numbers and found a reason each one had sort of worked: “promising”, “needs more time”. Nothing was ever stopped, and nothing was ever decided.

  • Without a pass mark, any result can be read as success.
  • Experiments drift on because stopping feels like failing.
  • The team learns nothing it can act on.

2Real-life analogy the bet on the napkin

Two friends write a bet on a napkin before kick-off: “Ajax win by two or more goals; the loser buys dinner.” After the final whistle there's nothing to argue about. Compare the friend who says after the match, “I knew they'd win”: they can never be wrong, so what they say tells you nothing.

3How it solves it belief, signal, pass mark, decision

A hypothesis tests one assumption, the riskiest from the map. The signal must be something the test can actually measure, and the pass mark must be agreed with whoever will act on the result (Priya, Joost). Then the decision is already made; the test only tells you which branch to take.

  1. Pick the riskiest assumption

    From the assumption map's top-left: “customers answer within 2 minutes”.

  2. Write the belief

    We believe [change] for [who] will result in [outcome].

  3. Choose a measurable signal

    Something the test can measure directly: answer rate, time to answer.

  4. Set the pass mark

    A threshold agreed in advance with the decision-maker: ≥ 70 %.

  5. Write the decision rule

    Pass → next step. Fail → which branch of the tree next.

  6. Run, record, decide

    Report the result against the card; don't move the pass mark afterwards.

Vocabulary for the exam

Hypothesis
A testable statement of what you believe will happen.
Signal
What you'll measure in the test.
Pass mark
The threshold that counts as success, set in advance.
Decision rule
What you'll do if the test passes or fails.
Falsifiable
Could be shown wrong by evidence.
Moving the goalposts
Changing success criteria after seeing results.

tools: a card in the repo or on the wall. Strategyzer's Test Card and Learning Card are good templates; Jira Product Discovery and similar tools can hold hypotheses next to ideas.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Clear decisions: the result picks the next step.
  • Honest learning: no reading success into noise.
  • Experiments stop when they fail, freeing the team.
  • Stakeholders agree in advance, so results aren't re-argued.
  • One assumption per test, so you know what you learned.

What we lose

  • Picking thresholds is hard before you have data.
  • Feels rigid to people used to “let's see”.
  • Small tests give noisy results near the pass mark.
  • Can be over-formalised for tiny tweaks.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • No pass mark

    “Let's see what happens.” Fix → write the threshold before starting, with the decision-maker.

  • Moving the goalposts

    65 % answered, so the bar is lowered to 60 %. Fix → keep the original mark; decide by the rule; re-test if needed.

  • Testing several things at once

    New flow, new copy and a discount in one test. Fix → one assumption per hypothesis.

  • Signals the test can't measure

    “Customers love it.” Fix → choose something countable in the test.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any experiment, interview round or pilot meant to inform a decision.
  • When stakeholders tend to re-argue results.
  • When experiments keep running without ending.
  • Before spending money on a test.

Skip it when…

  • Pure exploration where you don't know what to look for yet: interview first.
  • Tiny copy tweaks: ship and watch.
  • Decisions already made: don't stage a test for show.

8Self-check quiz

What are the parts of a hypothesis card?

Belief (change, who, expected result), signal, pass mark, test, decision rule.

Why set the pass mark in advance?

Otherwise any result can be read as success and nothing is decided.

What was H1's pass mark for answering?

At least 70 % answer within 2 minutes.

What happens if H1 fails on the answer rate?

The team tests the “never swap this” flag branch instead.

try it

Take one experiment your team is running or planning. Write its hypothesis card with a pass mark and a decision rule. Show it to the person who'll act on the result and ask: “if it lands at exactly the pass mark, what will you do?”

remember!!

Write the bet before kick-off. Don't move the goalposts.
