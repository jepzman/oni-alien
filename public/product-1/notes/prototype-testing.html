Prototype Testing with Users

TL;DR

Put a clickable prototype (screens that look real, no code behind them) in front of about five real users per kind of user, give each a realistic scenario, and watch what they do without helping. Where they hesitate, misread or fail is where the design is wrong. Fix it and test again. It costs days, finds most usability problems before engineering starts, and the people who designed it can't do it for themselves.

  • Family: testing ideas cheaply
  • Origin: usability testing (Jakob Nielsen, “five users”, 2000); Google Ventures design sprints
  • Lives in: a test plan, session notes and a findings list per round
  • Pairs with: concierge MVP, proto-personas, journey map

1The situation Larder, the design review

With the concierge test passed, Dee designs the screens for approve-in-app. In the review, Grace, Dee and Priya agree they're clear. But all three know exactly how the feature works. Nobody has watched a parent at 17:40 with a toddler on one arm, or a picker wearing gloves, try to use it. The people who built it can't see what's confusing.

  • Designers and product owners know too much to judge clarity.
  • Usability problems found after release cost a sprint each to fix.
  • Staff-facing screens (pickers) are rarely tested at all.

2Real-life analogy the kitchen taped on the garage floor

Before ordering a new kitchen, a family tapes the floor plan onto the garage floor at full size and walks through cooking a dinner. They find that the dishwasher door blocks the fridge when it's open. Moving tape costs nothing. Moving a fitted kitchen costs thousands.

3How it solves it scenario, watch, fix, repeat

Test each kind of user separately (customers and pickers here), in their real setting where possible: their own phone, the store floor, gloves on. Observers say nothing and note where people hesitate. Five people per group finds most problems; then fix and test again rather than testing more people.

  1. Build a realistic prototype

    Figma or similar; real products, prices and wording, not lorem ipsum.

  2. Write scenarios, not instructions

    “It's 17:40, you ordered dinner, your phone buzzes.” Never “tap the approve button”.

  3. Recruit 5–6 per user type

    Customers matching the persona; pickers from Noord. Test in context.

  4. Watch, don't help

    Ask them to think aloud; note hesitations, errors and quotes. One facilitator, one note-taker.

  5. List findings by severity

    What, how many hit it, how bad, proposed fix.

  6. Fix and re-test

    A second round on version two, before engineering starts.

Vocabulary for the exam

Prototype
A model of the product that looks real enough to react to.
Usability test
Watching real users try to complete tasks with a design.
Think-aloud
Asking participants to say what they're thinking as they go.
Task scenario
A realistic situation that gives the participant a goal.
Severity
How badly a problem blocks users, and how many it affects.
Fidelity
How close the prototype is to the real thing (paper → clickable).

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Most usability problems found before engineering starts.
  • Real context: gloves, toddlers, 17:40 stress.
  • Cheap fixes: a design change, not a sprint.
  • Persuasive: watching users struggle convinces faster than arguments.
  • Staff screens tested too: pickers are users.

What we lose

  • Not real behaviour at scale: a test, not live use.
  • Recruiting and scheduling take effort.
  • Prototype fidelity limits: some issues only show with real data and speed.
  • Small samples can't measure rates, only find problems.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Instructions instead of scenarios

    “Tap the approve button.” Fix → give a goal in a realistic situation; don't name the UI.

  • Helping participants

    “It's up there, on the top.” Fix → stay quiet; note where they got stuck.

  • Only testing customers

    Pickers get the screens on launch day. Fix → test every kind of user, in their real setting.

  • One round only

    Fixes are never checked. Fix → re-test version two before engineering starts.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any new flow or significant screen change.
  • Staff-facing tools used under pressure or with gloves.
  • After a concierge test, before building.
  • When the team disagrees about a design.

Skip it when…

  • Measuring rates or preferences: use data or A/B tests.
  • Tiny copy changes.
  • When you don't yet know if the problem is worth solving: interview and test the value first.

8Self-check quiz

Why can't the designers judge the design themselves?

They know how it works, so they can't see what's confusing.

How many users per group find most problems?

About five; then fix and test again.

What's the difference between a scenario and an instruction?

A scenario gives a goal in a realistic situation; an instruction names the UI to use.

What did the pickers' test reveal?

Buttons too small for gloves, and pickers waiting idle for the answer.

try it

Take one screen your team will build next. Write a one-sentence scenario for it without naming any button. Show the design to three people outside the team and watch silently. Write down every place they hesitated.
