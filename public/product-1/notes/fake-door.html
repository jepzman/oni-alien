Fake Door Test

TL;DR

Before building something, add the entry point to it (a link, a button, a menu item) and count who uses it. Behind the door, be honest: “not ready yet, tell us more”, and ask for an email or a detail you can follow up. It measures real interest with real behaviour in days, for almost nothing. Keep it short, small and honest, and never fake a payment.

  • Family: testing ideas cheaply
  • Origin: Lean Startup “smoke tests” (2000s); painted-door tests at large web companies
  • Lives in: a small link in the product for one or two weeks
  • Pairs with: testable hypotheses, concierge MVP, jobs to be done

1The situation Larder, Femke's business idea

Femke wants to start Larder for Business: weekly pantry deliveries to offices. Her plan is to hire a business salesperson (€70,000 a year) and build an office ordering portal (three months). The evidence: two of Grace's interviews mentioned an office pantry, and a feeling. Grace suggests finding out who knocks before building the door.

  • Big bets get made on enthusiasm and a few anecdotes.
  • Asking people “would you use it?” gets polite yeses.
  • Building first is the most expensive way to find out nobody wants it.

2Real-life analogy the dish on the menu

A restaurant can add “mushroom risotto” to the menu before buying a single mushroom. When a guest orders it, the waiter is honest (“it's not ready yet, here's a free dessert”) and the chef counts the orders. Twenty-three in a week: develop it. Two: drop it, having spent almost nothing.

3How it solves it a small link, an honest page, a pass mark

Show the door to a small share of users for a short time, with a hypothesis and pass mark written first. The landing page must be honest and give something back: a promise to get in touch, a way to leave details. The follow-up conversations are often worth more than the click count.

  1. Write the hypothesis

    “At least 0.5 % of sessions click, and at least 5 offices with 40+ staff leave details.”

  2. Place the door where it fits

    Account menu and the order confirmation email: where office managers would look.

  3. Build an honest landing page

    Not ready yet; tell us about your office; we'll be in touch. No fake checkout.

  4. Limit it

    Two weeks, a share of users; check with legal and privacy (Lena) for the form.

  5. Count and follow up

    Clicks, emails, team sizes. Call the most promising ones within a week.

  6. Decide and remove the door

    Pass → next step (a pilot). Fail → park the idea. Take the link down either way.

Vocabulary for the exam

Fake door test
An entry point to something that doesn't exist yet, to measure interest.
Smoke test
A landing page or ad that measures demand before building.
Painted door
Another name for a fake door.
Click-through rate
Share of people who saw the door and clicked.
Qualified lead
Someone who matches the target (offices with 40+ staff).
Honest disclosure
Telling people right away that it's not ready.

tools: a feature flag to show the door to a share of users (LaunchDarkly, Unleash, or your own), a simple form, and your analytics tool. Check the landing page wording with legal and keep it honest.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Real behaviour, not polite opinions.
  • Days, not months, for almost nothing.
  • Leads to talk to: the follow-up calls.
  • Evidence for big bets: Femke's trade-fair numbers.
  • Easy to stop: remove one link.

What we lose

  • Can annoy users who expected a real feature.
  • Trust risk if overused or dishonest.
  • Interest isn't purchase: clicks overstate demand.
  • Only tests the entry, not whether the product works.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Dishonest door

    A fake checkout takes card details. Fix → say “not ready yet” immediately; never fake payment.

  • Running it forever

    The link stays for months; users get annoyed. Fix → set an end date and remove it.

  • Clicks as demand

    140 clicks = 140 customers. Fix → follow up; qualify; treat clicks as interest only.

  • No pass mark

    Any number looks interesting. Fix → write the hypothesis and pass mark first.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A new feature or business line with unknown demand.
  • Before a big hire or build.
  • Choosing between several possible features.
  • When surveys keep giving polite yeses.

Skip it when…

  • Anything involving payment, health or safety promises.
  • Very small user bases where a few clicks mean nothing.
  • When you'd build it anyway: don't stage a test.

8Self-check quiz

What does a fake door test measure?

Real interest, through behaviour (clicks, details left), before anything is built.

What must the page behind the door do?

Tell people honestly it's not ready, and give them a way to leave details.

Why follow up with the people who clicked?

Clicks overstate demand; conversations show who's really a customer.

What did Larder's fake door lead to?

Numbers Femke took to the trade fair where she met Korenwolf, and a pilot before any portal.

try it

Pick one feature idea your team is debating. Write a fake-door hypothesis with a pass mark, an honest landing-page text and an end date. Ask your legal or privacy contact to read the text before anything goes live.

remember!!

Put the dish on the menu, count the orders, and always be honest at the table.
