Problem Statements

TL;DR

Before anyone proposes a fix, write the problem down in one short paragraph: who struggles, with what, why it matters to them, what it costs the business, and how you'll know it's solved. No solution words. A good problem statement ends solution debates that start too early, lets the team find better fixes, and gives everyone the same target.

  • Family: framing the problem
  • Origin: design thinking (“point of view” statements); Lean UX problem statements
  • Lives in: the top of every brief, ticket epic and roadmap item
  • Pairs with: opportunity solution tree, outcomes over outputs, customer interviews

1The situation Larder, the inherited brief

The Q4 roadmap Grace inherits says: “Build a substitution approval feature.” In the first meeting about it, the team spends twenty minutes on button colours, modals and timeouts. Nobody can say who it's for, what exactly it fixes, or how they'll know it worked. The brief is a solution with no problem attached.

  • Briefs written as solutions lock in the first idea.
  • Teams argue about details because they don't share the goal.
  • Without a “solved when”, nobody can tell if the work succeeded.

2Real-life analogy calling the plumber

One caller tells the plumber “install a new pipe under the sink”. Another says “the kitchen floor is wet every morning near the dishwasher, since Tuesday”. A good plumber much prefers the second: it says where, when and how bad, and leaves the fix to the expert. The first might get a new pipe and a floor that's still wet.

3How it solves it five parts, no solution

Write it with the team, from interview and data evidence. Read it back and delete every word that describes a solution (button, screen, notification). If the team can't agree on the paragraph, they wouldn't have agreed on the feature either, and it's far cheaper to find that out now.

  1. Name who

    A persona or job, not “users”: “the tonight-dinner parent, ordering express”.

  2. Describe the struggle

    What goes wrong, when, in the customer's terms.

  3. Say why it matters to them

    The consequence for the customer: no dinner ingredient, a trip out, allergy risk.

  4. Add the cost to the business

    Numbers: refund rate, support hours, lost orders.

  5. Write “solved when”

    The measure that shows it's fixed: swap refunds below 2 % of orders.

  6. Strip out solutions

    Delete product words; agree the paragraph before any design starts.

Vocabulary for the exam

Problem statement
A short description of a problem, without a solution.
Point of view
Design-thinking name for a user + need + insight statement.
Success criterion
The measurable sign that the problem is solved.
Solution bias
Jumping to a fix before the problem is understood.
Problem space / solution space
Understanding the need / designing the answer.
Brief
The document that starts a piece of work.
product/substitutions/problem.mdproblem statement, agreed 7 Nov 2025
# Problem: substitution surprises                      agreed: Grace, Priya, Ben, Dee, Sam

Who        Express customers, mostly the tonight-dinner parent (product/personas/)
Struggle   When an item is out of stock, the picker swaps it without asking.
           The customer finds out at the door, too late to do anything.
Matters    No ingredient for tonight's dinner; sometimes an allergy risk;
           a second trip to the corner shop.
Costs us   6 % of orders end in a refund ticket (re-tagged baseline, 400 tickets)
           ≈ €45,000 / month in refunds and support time
           Customers with 2+ refund tickets in a month order 30 % less next month
Solved     Refund tickets from substitutions < 2 % of orders, by end Q2 2026,
when       with no drop in express on-time rate (88 % today)

Not in this statement: any button, screen or notification. That's the team's job.

tools: a paragraph at the top of the brief. Lean UX and IDEO's design-thinking guides have problem-statement templates; some teams use a “how might we…?” question to open ideation once the statement is agreed.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Earlier agreement on what matters, before design starts.
  • Better fixes: the team can find options you didn't think of.
  • A finish line: “solved when” says when to stop.
  • Faster reviews: every design is checked against one paragraph.
  • Stakeholders see the cost, so priority is easier to argue.

What we lose

  • Feels slow to people who already “know” the solution.
  • Needs evidence: numbers and interviews take time.
  • Can be gamed: a solution disguised as a problem.
  • Uncomfortable when the inherited brief gets rewritten.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A solution in disguise

    “Customers lack an approval button.” Fix → delete product words; describe the struggle.

  • No “solved when”

    Nobody knows when to stop or if it worked. Fix → add a measurable success criterion.

  • “Users” as the who

    Everyone, so no one. Fix → name the persona or job.

  • Written alone

    Grace writes it; the team never reads it. Fix → agree it with the team before design starts.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • At the start of any epic, project or roadmap item.
  • When a brief arrives as a solution.
  • When design debates go round in circles.
  • Before asking for budget or priority.

Skip it when…

  • Tiny fixes where the problem is obvious (a typo, a broken link).
  • Pure tech maintenance with its own clear goal.
  • When the problem isn't understood yet: interview first.

8Self-check quiz

What are the five parts of Grace's problem statement?

Who, the struggle, why it matters to them, the cost to the business, and “solved when”.

What's wrong with “build a substitution approval feature” as a brief?

It's a solution: it says nothing about who, what problem, or how to know it worked.

Why remove all product words?

So the team can find the best fix instead of the first one.

What was Larder's “solved when”?

Refund tickets from substitutions below 2 % of orders by end Q2 2026, with no drop in on-time rate.

try it

Take the top item on your roadmap. Write its problem statement in five parts without any product words. Read it to the team and ask each person to name one solution. If they all name the same one, ask for two more each.
