Jobs to Be Done

TL;DR

Customers don't buy products; they hire them to make progress in a particular situation. Describe that job as a short story: when [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]. Jobs explain behaviour that age and income can't, show who you really compete with, and tell you what “good” means in each situation. The same person can have several jobs, and a feature can help one job while hurting another.

  • Family: understanding customers
  • Origin: Clayton Christensen, Competing Against Luck (2016); Bob Moesta; Alan Klement's job stories
  • Lives in: two or three job stories at the top of the product brief
  • Pairs with: customer interviews, proto-personas, north star metric

1The situation Larder, Priya's segments

Larder's marketing segments customers by age and income: “young professionals, 25–34”. But in her interviews Grace meets Rik, who orders express on Tuesday at 17:30 (six items, dinner for the kids) and a slot on Sunday morning (41 items, the week's shop). Same person, same segment, and he wants opposite things: speed on Tuesday, completeness on Sunday. A substitution means something different each time.

  • Demographics describe people, not why they act.
  • Features get designed for an average customer who doesn't exist.
  • The team doesn't see its real competitors: the corner shop, a takeaway.

2Real-life analogy the morning milkshake

A fast-food chain wanted to sell more milkshakes and asked customers how to improve them: thicker, cheaper, more flavours. Sales didn't move. Then they watched who bought them: commuters, before 8 a.m., facing a long, boring drive. They weren't hiring a dessert; they were hiring something that lasts the drive and fits in one hand. Its real competitors were bananas, bagels and boredom.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the product
The morning commuteThe situation: 17:30, nothing for dinner
Something that lasts the driveThe progress: dinner on the table by 18:30, no trip out
Bananas and bagelsReal competitors: the corner shop, a takeaway
Thicker shake for morningsDesign for the job: fast, and ask me before swapping
A different job at lunch (a treat for the kids)The Sunday slot: a different job, same customer

3How it solves it situation, motivation, outcome

Write each job as a job story from interview evidence, then add what matters in that job and what it competes with. A job is stable over years; the solutions change. Grace's two jobs explain why the substitution flow must be fast for express and careful for slots.

  1. Start from stories

    Use interview snapshots, not brainstorms: when did they order, and what were they doing?

  2. Find the struggling moment

    What made them reach for Larder right then? (17:30, empty fridge, kids hungry.)

  3. Write job stories

    When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]. No product words.

  4. Note what matters

    The two or three things that make it a success or a failure in this job.

  5. List the real competition

    What else could they hire? Corner shop, takeaway, a supermarket's own delivery.

  6. Design per job

    Check each feature against each job: does it help one and hurt another?

Vocabulary for the exam

Job to be done
The progress a customer is trying to make in a situation.
Job story
When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].
Hire / fire
Choosing a product for a job, or dropping it for another.
Struggling moment
The moment the job becomes urgent enough to act.
Functional / emotional job
What gets done / how they want to feel.
Non-consumption
Doing nothing or a workaround: often the biggest competitor.

tools: interview snapshots and a whiteboard. Bob Moesta's “switch interview” (Re-Wired Group) and Jim Kalbach's job map are useful deeper methods; jtbd.info has many examples.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Explains behaviour that segments can't.
  • Real competitors come into view.
  • Clear success criteria per situation.
  • Stable: jobs change slowly, solutions fast.
  • New markets spotted: the office pantry job.

What we lose

  • Abstract at first: teams need examples to get it.
  • Easy to make vague (“I want to feel good”).
  • Needs good interviews to be more than guesses.
  • Doesn't size the market: pair with data.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Product words in the job

    “I want to use the approve button.” Fix → describe the progress, never the product or a feature.

  • Too vague

    “I want to feel happy.” Fix → anchor the job in a situation and a concrete outcome.

  • Jobs from a brainstorm

    Written in a room with no customers. Fix → derive them from interview stories.

  • One job for everyone

    Express and slots merged into “get groceries”. Fix → split jobs where what matters is different.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When segments don't explain what customers do.
  • Before designing a flow that serves different situations.
  • Looking for new markets or the real competition.
  • Writing the product strategy.

Skip it when…

  • Small usability fixes inside a flow.
  • When you have no interview evidence yet: interview first.
  • As a replacement for data on how many people have each job.

8Self-check quiz

What is the job story format?

When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].

Why did segments fail to explain Rik's behaviour?

The same person had two jobs (express dinner, the weekly shop) with opposite needs.

Who are the real competitors of the express job?

The corner shop, a takeaway, or making do: not other grocery apps only.

What does a substitution mean in each Larder job?

Express: fine if asked. Slots: swapping a staple ruins the plan.

try it

Pick your product's most common use. Write it as one job story (when…, I want to…, so I can…) without naming your product. List three things customers could hire instead, including “do nothing”. Which of them is your real competitor?

remember!!

Customers hire products for a job. Know the job, and you know the competition.
