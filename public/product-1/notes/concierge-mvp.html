Concierge & Wizard-of-Oz MVP

TL;DR

Before building software, deliver the value by hand to a small group of real customers: people do what the product would do. A concierge test is open about it (a person calls you); a Wizard of Oz test shows a product front end while people work the levers behind the curtain. You learn whether customers want the result, what they really need, and what the process should be, in days, with no code. Build only once it's proven.

  • Lives in: a two-week pilot with a few staff and a spreadsheet

1The situation Larder, eight weeks or a phone

The team is ready to build approve-in-app: new app screens, push notifications and SMS, a picker app change, a two-minute timer, a backend for answers. About eight weeks of work. But the riskiest assumption on the map, that customers answer within two minutes, could be tested next week, with a phone.

  • Building to learn is the slowest, most expensive way to learn.
  • Once code exists, the team is reluctant to throw it away, even if the idea fails.
  • The real process (who calls, what's said, what pickers do meanwhile) is unknown until someone does it.

2Real-life analogy the laundry service by hand

To start a laundry pick-up service you could build an app and rent a laundry. Or you could collect bags from neighbours by bike, wash them at home and bring them back folded, for two weeks. If neighbours keep coming back and paying, you know the service is wanted, and you know exactly what the app needs to do.

3How it solves it people do what the software would

Keep it small (one store, express only, three hours an evening), time-boxed (two weeks) and tied to a hypothesis card. Use what already exists: the picker app's “item missing” button, a phone, a spreadsheet. Watch the process as closely as the numbers.

  1. Start from the hypothesis

    H1: ≥ 70 % answer within 2 minutes; refund tickets < 2 %; picking time ≤ +60 s.

  2. Design the manual process

    Who notices, who calls, what they say, where it's written down, what the picker does meanwhile.

  3. Keep it small and safe

    One store, express orders, 17:00–20:00, two weeks. Brief the staff; tell Lena about the calls.

  4. Run it, watch it

    Grace spends evenings at Noord. Note surprises, not just numbers.

  5. Compare to a baseline

    Same weeks at Zuid without calls; same ticket rule.

  6. Decide by the card

    Pass → prototype the in-app version. Fail → the next branch of the tree.

Vocabulary for the exam

MVP
Minimum viable product: the smallest thing that tests whether something is worth building.
Concierge MVP
Delivering the value by hand, openly, to a few customers.
Wizard of Oz
A product-looking front end, with people doing the work behind it.
Manual-first
Doing a process by hand before automating it.
Comparison group
Similar orders without the change (Zuid), for a fair comparison.
Operational load
The extra work a test puts on staff.

# Concierge test · asking before a swap (H1)          Noord · 8–19 Dec 2025, 17:00–20:00

Setup     Picker taps “item missing” (existing button) → alert to a support agent
          → agent + picker pick a substitute → agent phones the customer → answer
          to picker → pack. Logged in a spreadsheet. 2 agents (Sam's team).

Result    412 swaps on 1,960 express orders
          answered < 2 min       81 %   ✓ (≥ 70 %)
          accepted the swap      74 %
          chose “no swap”        15 %
          no answer               11 %  → picker's choice (as today)
          swap refund tickets    1.1 %  ✓ (< 2 %)   Zuid same weeks: 6.8 %
          picking time           +40 s per affected order ✓ (≤ 60 s)

Surprises - customers asked the price difference in 1 of 3 calls → show it
          - 9 calls mentioned an allergy → “never swap this” matters (O3)
          - pickers waited idle for the answer → let them continue (redesign)
Decision  PASS. Next: clickable prototype test (Jan), then build. Calls stop 19 Dec.
          Cost: ≈ 60 agent-hours.

tools: a phone, a spreadsheet and the systems you already have. For a Wizard of Oz version, a feature flag shows a real-looking screen while a person sends the answers by hand from an admin tool.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Answers in two weeks without code.
  • The real process is discovered: price questions, allergies, idle pickers.
  • Strong evidence: 1.1 % vs 6.8 % in the same weeks.
  • Better design: the build starts from what actually happened.
  • Easy to stop if it fails.

What we lose

  • Staff time: about 60 agent-hours.
  • Doesn't scale: the manual version can't run everywhere.
  • Differs from the product: a phone call isn't a push notification.
  • Disruption to the store during the test.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Too big

    All stores, all evenings, from day one. Fix → one store, one segment, a short window.

  • No comparison

    1.1 % sounds great, but compared to what? Fix → run a comparison group in the same weeks.

  • Never stopping

    The manual process becomes permanent. Fix → set an end date; decide by the card.

  • Only counting

    Numbers recorded, surprises missed. Fix → be there; note what people say and do.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When the riskiest assumption is about customer behaviour.
  • Before building a new service or workflow.
  • When the process itself is unclear.
  • When a manual version is possible for a small group.

Skip it when…

  • When the value only exists at scale or speed (real-time pricing).
  • Where manual handling is unsafe or illegal.
  • When the assumption is purely technical: spike it.

8Self-check quiz

What's the difference between a concierge and a Wizard-of-Oz MVP?

Concierge is openly manual; Wizard of Oz shows a product front end with people working behind it.

Why compare with Zuid in the same weeks?

So the result isn't explained by season, weather or anything else that changed.

Name two surprises from the Noord test.

Customers asked about the price difference; allergies came up often; pickers waited idle.

What decided the next step?

The hypothesis card: all three pass marks were met, so the next step was a prototype test.
