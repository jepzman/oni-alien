Proto-Personas

TL;DR

A proto-persona is a quick, one-page sketch of a main kind of customer: their situation, needs and pains, drawn from real interviews, with a note of how much evidence is behind it. Keep two to four, give them plain names that describe the situation (“the tonight-dinner parent”), leave out invented details, and update them as you learn. They replace “the user” in meetings with someone specific everyone can picture.

1The situation Larder, the persona deck

Larder has personas: a 28-page deck from a marketing agency starring “Urban Ursula”, with a stock photo, a favourite coffee order and a star sign. None of it came from research, and nobody uses it. In meetings, Ben pictures a student, Dee a busy mum, Joost his own parents. Everyone argues for “the user”, and everyone means someone different.

  • Invented details make personas fun and useless.
  • Without a shared picture, “the user” becomes whoever wins the argument.
  • Heavy research personas take months and go stale.

2Real-life analogy the police sketch

A police sketch is drawn from several witness statements. It's rough (no birthday, no favourite colour), but good enough for people to recognise someone, and the artist updates it as more witnesses come in. A good sketch also says how many witnesses agreed.

3How it solves it a few cards, all evidence

Group the snapshot cards by situation and needs, not by age. Each group with enough stories becomes a card: a descriptive name, the situation, three needs or pains, and the evidence count. A card with two interviews is marked low confidence; it's a hypothesis, not a fact.

  1. Group the interviews

    Sort snapshot cards by situation and needs. Two to four groups usually appear.

  2. Name by situation

    “The tonight-dinner parent”, not “Urban Ursula”. The name should remind you of the need.

  3. Write only what matters

    Situation, how they use Larder, top three needs and pains, a quote. Nothing invented.

  4. Show the evidence

    Number of interviews and data behind it; mark confidence high, medium or low.

  5. Put them on the wall

    Use the names in stories, reviews and designs: “would the parent see this in time?”

  6. Revise each quarter

    Merge, split or retire personas as new interviews come in.

Vocabulary for the exam

Persona
A representative description of a kind of customer.
Proto-persona
A quick, evidence-light persona, treated as a hypothesis.
Evidence count
How many interviews or data points support it.
Anti-persona
Someone you're deliberately not designing for.
Archetype
A pattern of behaviour, rather than a fictional person.
Segment
A group defined for marketing (age, income), often not behaviour.

tools: one page per persona in a shared doc or on the wall. Lean UX's proto-persona template is a good start; tools like Miro and FigJam have persona templates (delete the star-sign fields).

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • One shared picture instead of “the user”.
  • Fast: a few hours from existing interviews.
  • Honest: evidence counts show what's solid and what's a guess.
  • Better reviews: “would the parent see this in time?”
  • Spots new customers: the office stocker.

What we lose

  • Can harden into stereotypes if never revised.
  • Small samples: low-confidence cards can mislead.
  • Easy to decorate with invented detail.
  • Not a market size: pair with data.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Invented detail

    Star signs, coffee orders, stock photos. Fix → only include what changes product decisions.

  • Personas by demographics

    “Female, 25–34, urban.” Fix → group by situation, needs and behaviour.

  • Too many

    Nine personas, so nobody remembers any. Fix → two to four; merge the rest.

  • Never revised

    The 2025 cards still rule in 2027. Fix → revise each quarter; show the evidence count.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When “the user” causes arguments.
  • After a first round of interviews.
  • Onboarding designers and engineers to the customers.
  • Choosing which customer a feature is for.

Skip it when…

  • When you have no interviews yet: interview first.
  • To size a market: use data.
  • When jobs to be done already give the team enough focus.

8Self-check quiz

How is a proto-persona different from a classic persona?

It's quick, based on the evidence you have, marked with its confidence, and treated as a hypothesis.

Why name personas by situation?

The name reminds everyone of the need (“the tonight-dinner parent”).

What should never be on a proto-persona?

Invented details that don't change product decisions: star signs, coffee orders, stock photos.

Why is the office stocker marked low confidence?

Only two interviews support it; it's a hint to test, not a fact.

try it

From your last five customer conversations, sort the people into groups by situation and need. Write one proto-persona card for the biggest group, with an evidence count. Read it to a colleague who joined the calls: would they recognise the people?

remember!!

A sketch from witnesses, not a novel. Say how many witnesses agree.
