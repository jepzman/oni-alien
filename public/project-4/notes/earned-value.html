Is the Money Buying Progress?

TL;DR

Earned value puts progress and money in the same unit. Three numbers: planned value (PV), the budget for the work that should be done by now; earned value (EV), the budget for the work actually done; and actual cost (AC), what that work really cost. EV − PV says if you're ahead or behind; EV − AC says if you're over or under on cost. Divide instead of subtract and you get two indices, SPI and CPI: 1.0 is on plan. Spending on plan means nothing until you know what was earned.

  • Family: watching the money
  • Lives in: three numbers and two ratios, once a month (or on a key date)
  • Pairs with: the budget's S-curve, status reports, forecasting

1The situation Larder, 20 March 2026

Marco has two pieces of news that don't fit. Spending is close to plan: about €178k of work paid for or received, against €178k planned by now. Progress, on Sanne's must-have count, is a little behind: 45 done, the plan line is about 47. He asks: “if we're behind but spending on plan, are we paying more for less?” Neither number alone can say: one is in euros, the other in must-haves.

  • Spending on plan can mean on plan, or overpaying for less.
  • Progress and money are counted in different units.
  • The comfortable answer (“on budget”) may be wrong.

2Real-life analogy fuel money on a road trip

Halfway through the planned time of a road trip, you've used half the fuel money. Good news? Only if you've also covered half the distance. If you've driven only 40 % of the way, the money is going faster than the trip, and at this rate you'll run short before you arrive. Money spent only means something next to progress made.

3How it solves it three numbers on 20 March, line by line

Sanne and Marco work it out by line, at budget prices, against the €374k cost baseline (€360k plus the fridge upgrade). Planned value: by 20 March the builder should have done €90k of work, equipment €48k, recruiting €20k, marketing €20k: €178k. Earned value: the builder is exactly on its activities (€90k; the inspector's re-booking is three days away), equipment delivered (€48k), 34 of 40 hires made (€17k), marketing on plan (€20k): €175k. Actual cost: builder €90k (fixed price), equipment €48.5k, recruiting €19.5k, marketing €20k: €178k. So: a little behind (SV −€3k, SPI 0.98) and a little over (CV −€3k, CPI 0.98), both in the people line. Marco's answer: yes, slightly more for slightly less, and only in hiring. Sanne asks the agency for a fixed fee for the last six hires.

  1. Use the time-phased budget as planned value

    The S-curve.

  2. Measure work done in budget money: earned value

    By work package or line.

  3. Get actual cost for that work

    Not commitments, not cash.

  4. Subtract: SV = EV − PV, CV = EV − AC

    Negative is bad.

  5. Divide: SPI = EV ÷ PV, CPI = EV ÷ AC

    Below 1 is bad.

  6. Find the line causing it

    Then act there.

Vocabulary for the exam

Planned value (PV)
The budget for work scheduled to be done by now.
Earned value (EV)
The budget for work actually done by now.
Actual cost (AC)
What the work done actually cost.
Schedule variance (SV)
EV − PV. Negative: behind.
Cost variance (CV)
EV − AC. Negative: over cost.
SPI and CPI
EV ÷ PV and EV ÷ AC. Below 1.0: behind, or over cost.
Budget at completion (BAC)
The total cost baseline: €374k here.

tools: the S-curve, a way to measure work done in budget money, and actual costs from finance.

＋Going deeper the three curves, measuring “earned”, fixed prices, and the limits

Earned value has a reputation for being heavy. Its core is three numbers you can work out on one page, as Rotterdam's shows. This chapter draws the curves, explains how to measure “earned” honestly, shows what happens with a fixed-price contract, and says what earned value can't see.

1Three curves

Plot the three numbers over time. Planned value follows the budget's S-curve. Earned value rises as work is done. Actual cost rises as money is spent on that work.

  • EV below PV: behind schedule (in money terms).
  • AC above EV: over cost for what's been done.
  • On 20 March Rotterdam's three lines are almost on top of each other: a small, explainable gap.

2Measuring “earned” honestly

Earned value is only as good as the way “done” is counted. Three rules that kept Rotterdam's honest:

  • 0 / 100 for small items: equipment is earned when it arrives, not when it's ordered.
  • Units completed where work is countable: 34 of 40 hires = 34 × €500.
  • Activity progress for the builder, from the network: an activity half done earns half its value, judged with Kees on site, not from his invoice.

Never earn from money spent (“we've spent 60 %, so we're 60 % done”). That makes CPI 1.0 by definition and hides exactly what earned value is for.

3Fixed-price contracts

With a fixed price, Larder pays the builder for work done at contract prices, so for that line AC equals EV and the cost variance is zero by construction. The builder's own costs can overrun; that's the builder's risk, which is why the contract is fixed. What earned value still shows for that line is schedule: EV against PV. That was the one to watch, and three days later the inspector proved it.

4What earned value can't see

  • Which work was done. Earning €3k on the wrong activities while the critical path waits looks fine in money. Use the critical path alongside it.
  • Quality. Rushed, wrong work earns as much as good work until someone rejects it.
  • Value to the customer. EV measures progress against the plan, not whether the plan is still worth doing.

Used once a month (or on a key date), with these limits said out loud, three numbers answer Marco's question better than any amount of colour.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • Ask: what fraction is done, what fraction is spent?
  • Compare.
  • No indices.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • PV, EV and AC by line on a key date.
  • SV, CV, SPI, CPI.
  • Find the line behind the gap.

Heavy

large contracts

  • Earned value system by control account.
  • Monthly EV reports to the client.
  • Formal thresholds for SPI and CPI.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Earning from spending

    CPI always 1.0. Fix → measure work done, not money spent.

  • Commitments as actual cost

    Everything looks over. Fix → AC is work done or goods received.

  • Indices without lines

    0.98 of what? Fix → find the line behind the gap.

  • EV instead of the critical path

    Right money, wrong work. Fix → use both.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When someone asks “are we paying more for less?”
  • On key dates and monthly on larger projects.
  • Before forecasting the final cost.
  • With mixed predictive and countable work.

Skip it when…

  • Purely adaptive work: use the burn-up and velocity instead.

8Self-check quiz

PV 178, EV 175, AC 178. What are SV and CV?

SV = −3, CV = −3 (€k).

And SPI and CPI?

175 ÷ 178 ≈ 0.98 for both.

Why is the builder's CV zero?

Fixed price: Larder pays contract value for work done, so AC = EV for that line.

A team says “we've spent 60 %, so we're 60 % done”. What's wrong?

That's earning from spending: CPI becomes 1.0 by definition and hides overruns.

Earned value shows SPI 1.0, but the critical path is late. Is the project on schedule?

No: non-critical work is ahead and hides it. Check the critical path alongside EV.

try it

For your project on today's date, estimate three numbers: the budget for the work that should be done by now (PV), the budget for the work actually done (EV), and what it cost (AC). Work out SPI and CPI. Which line explains most of the gap?
