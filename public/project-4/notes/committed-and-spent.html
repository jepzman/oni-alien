How Much Have We Spent?

TL;DR

“How much have we spent?” has three honest answers. Committed: everything promised by an order or contract. Spent (actual cost): work done and goods received. Forecast: where each line will end. Report all three, line by line, against the budget, every week. Committed tells you how much freedom is left; spent tells you about progress; the forecast tells you whether you'll fit. One number labelled “spent” misleads everyone, usually in the comfortable direction.

  • Family: watching the money
  • Lives in: a four-column table by line, every Friday
  • Pairs with: money rules, earned value, status reports

1The situation Larder, 27 February 2026

This is Sanne's mistake for this collection. At the steering group of 27 February, Femke asks what Rotterdam has spent. Sanne says €61k, the figure Marco's team gave her on 13 February. Marco corrects her, gently: €335k is committed. The cold room (€78k) was ordered on 13 February, the equipment (€48k) on 23 February, and the builder's €148k contract was signed yesterday. Nearly nine tenths of the budget is already promised; the €61k was only the recruiting and marketing contracts of mid-February.

  • “Spent” was used for three different things.
  • The comfortable number was the wrong one.
  • A total hides which line is going wrong.

2Real-life analogy the bank, the card and the booking

To know how much you really have left this month, the bank balance isn't enough. There are card purchases not yet taken, and a holiday already booked that isn't billed yet. Each is real money. Someone who only looks at the bank balance always thinks they have more than they do.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the project
Bank balancePaid: cash out of the bank
Card purchases pendingSpent: work done, goods received, not yet paid
The booked holidayCommitted: orders and contracts signed
What's leftBudget minus committed
Where the month will endForecast, line by line

3How it solves it four columns, by line, every Friday

From the next Friday the money goes in a table of four columns by line: budget, committed, spent (work done or goods received) and forecast. Marco's team fills the first three from the orders and invoices; each line's lead gives the forecast. On 27 March: committed €355.5k, spent €265.5k, forecast €379k against €379k (after the weekend work). The table shows what the total hid: people has €45k committed but a forecast of €62.5k, because the agency charges more per hire than planned; marketing will land at €37k, because Priya is holding €3k back. One line over, one under, and a reserve of €1k between them and trouble.

  1. Separate committed, spent and paid

    Name each, every time.

  2. Get committed and spent from finance

    Orders, contracts, invoices.

  3. Get the forecast from each line's owner

    Where will it end?

  4. Show them by line against the budget

    Totals hide the problem line.

  5. Explain every forecast variance

    Over or under, and why.

  6. Report the reserve beside it

    What's left to absorb surprises.

Vocabulary for the exam

Committed cost
Money promised by signed orders and contracts.
Actual cost (AC)
Cost of work done and goods received so far.
Paid
Cash that has left the bank.
Forecast (estimate at completion)
Where a line, or the project, will end.
Cost variance (by line)
Budget minus forecast.
Accrual
Recording a cost when the work is done, not when it's paid.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small budget

  • Keep a list of what's ordered and what's arrived.
  • One forecast number.
  • Monthly.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Budget, committed, spent, forecast, by line.
  • Weekly.
  • Variances explained.

Heavy

large projects

  • Cost accounts per work package.
  • Accruals and commitments from the finance system.
  • Monthly cost reports with variance analysis.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • One word, three meanings

    “Spent”. Fix → committed, spent, paid: name them.

  • Totals only

    The bad line hides. Fix → by line.

  • Forecast = budget

    Nobody really forecasts. Fix → the line owner gives a number and a reason.

  • Using finance's figure without the date

    Two weeks stale. Fix → date every number.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Every week once money is being committed.
  • Before every steering group.
  • When finance and project numbers differ.
  • After any big contract is signed.

Skip it when…

  • Never, once there's a budget.

8Self-check quiz

Committed, spent or paid: the builder's contract on the day it's signed?

Committed (all €148k).

Why was “€61k” misleading on 27 Feb?

It was two weeks old and counted only the recruiting and marketing contracts; €335k was committed.

Which line was forecast over, and why?

People, by €2.5k: the agency charges €575 a hire, not €500.

The total forecast equals the budget. Is everything fine?

Not necessarily: people is over and marketing under. Look by line.

On 3 April Joost asks for €4k of extra training. What does Sanne check first?

The people line's forecast (already €2.5k over) and the reserve (€1k): it needs a change for Joost to decide, with marketing's €3k as a possible source via Marco.

try it

For your project, write four numbers per budget line: budget, committed, spent, forecast. Which line's forecast differs most from its budget? Can its owner explain why in one sentence?

remember!!

Bank, card and booking: what's left is the budget minus all three.
