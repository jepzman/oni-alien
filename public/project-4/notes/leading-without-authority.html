How Do I Lead People Who Don't Report to Me?

TL;DR

Most of the people a project needs don't report to the project manager. You lead them with what you have: a shared goal they care about, facts nobody else has assembled, help that removes what's blocking them, exchange (something back for them or their manager), and credit given in public. The sponsor's authority is real, but every time you borrow it, you spend trust you'll need later. Use it last.

  • Lives in: conversations, not documents
  • Pairs with: the role card, let the team decide, escalation

1The situation Larder, 16 March 2026

Two Rotterdam app items are slipping: zones and slots in the app, and the store page. Dee's team has given its sprint to approvals and an Android fix, both Grace's priorities, both sensible. Sanne isn't Dee's manager. She could ask Joost to tell Priya to tell Dee. It would work once, and she needs Dee's team for five more weeks.

  • The people doing the work don't report to the project manager.
  • Their own priorities are reasonable.
  • Orders through the sponsor cost trust.

2Real-life analogy the village choir

A conductor of a village choir employs nobody. She can't make anyone come on a Thursday night. They come because the music is worth singing, because she's prepared, because she makes their evening easy (parts printed, car-sharing sorted), and because at the concert she thanks them by name. A conductor who had to threaten people would soon have no choir.

3How it solves it ask, unblock, show the facts, offer back, give credit

Sanne starts with a question to Dee: “what's blocking the Rotterdam items?” The answer: the Dutch store-page texts from Priya's team, waiting nine days. Sanne gets them the next day. Then she shows Dee the facts nobody had put together: app ready is 20 April, the burn-up says two items are at risk, and Tomás's training plan depends on them. She offers something back: Yara's new pickers can test approvals on real handhelds in their training week, which Grace has been asking for. And in Friday's status, Dee's team is named for the Android fix. Dee puts both items into the sprint of 23 March; both are done by 3 April. Joost is never asked. That reserve of trust is still there when Sanne needs it on 23 March, for the builder.

  1. Ask what's in their way

    Before asking for anything.

  2. Remove a block yourself

    Fast, visibly.

  3. Show facts they don't have

    Dates, dependencies, consequences.

  4. Offer something back

    To them or their manager.

  5. Give credit in public

    By name.

  6. Borrow the sponsor's authority last

    And rarely.

＋Going deeper sources of influence, tactics that work, the favour bank, when to use the sponsor

Every project manager in a matrix organisation leads people they don't manage. The skill can be learned. This chapter lists the sources of influence you actually have, the tactics that work and those that backfire, how the favour bank works, and when the sponsor's authority is the right tool.

1What you have instead of authority

A project manager rarely has positional power over the team. What they have, often more than anyone:

  • Information: the whole plan, the dates, who waits for whom. Nobody else has assembled it.
  • Expertise: how launches go, where they go wrong.
  • Relationships: built before they're needed, in the first weeks, over small things.
  • Reciprocity: help given earlier.
  • The sponsor's authority: real, borrowed, and expensive to use.

2Tactics that work, and two that backfire

Research on influence finds that the tactics that work best with peers are rational persuasion (facts and consequences), consultation (asking for their idea), inspiration (the goal), and collaboration (removing a block for them). Sanne used all four with Dee.

Two backfire with peers: pressure (deadlines repeated more loudly) and going over their head without telling them. Both can get compliance once and cost commitment afterwards.

3The favour bank

Every favour is a deposit: texts fetched in a day, a tester found, a name in the status. When you need something, you make a withdrawal. A project manager who only ever asks runs an overdraft, and people start being busy when they see them coming.

The deposits that count are the ones that cost you something and help them with their goals, not the project's. Yara's pickers testing approvals helped Grace, not Rotterdam.

4When to use the sponsor

Use the sponsor's authority when a decision is above everyone in the room (a threshold is crossed, two priorities conflict at the top), not to make a peer do what you want. Then do it openly: tell the person first that you're taking it up, and why. On 23 March Sanne went to Joost about the builder because €5,000 and a milestone were at stake. With Dee, two items in a sprint weren't, so the sponsor stayed out of it.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small team you know

  • Ask, help, thank.
  • No plan needed.
  • Every day.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Know what each team needs and what blocks it.
  • Unblock fast, show facts, exchange, credit.
  • The sponsor kept in reserve.

Heavy

large matrix organisations

  • Stakeholder and team engagement plans.
  • Formal agreements with resource managers.
  • Coaching in influence for project managers.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Ordering through the sponsor

    Works once. Fix → influence first; sponsor last.

  • Asking before helping

    An overdraft at the favour bank. Fix → remove a block first.

  • Pressure

    Compliance, not commitment. Fix → facts and consequences instead.

  • Credit kept for yourself

    People stop helping. Fix → name them in public.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • With every team you don't manage.
  • When a team's priorities clash with yours.
  • Before escalating.
  • From day one: build relationships before you need them.

Skip it when…

  • Never; even with your own reports these work better than orders.
