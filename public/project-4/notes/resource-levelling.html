Who Is Overloaded?

TL;DR

A plan can be fine activity by activity and still ask one person to work an eight-day week. Draw each key person's load by week (a resource histogram) across all their work, not just your project. Where it passes 100 %, smooth (move work within its float, so no date moves), re-assign (someone else does it), stop what shouldn't be there, or, if nothing else works, level (move work even if a date moves). Plan people at about 80 %: meetings, support and surprises take the rest.

  • Family: leading and balancing
  • Lives in: a load chart for each key person, checked weekly
  • Pairs with: float, scope creep, acquiring resources

1The situation Larder, 23–24 March 2026

This is Sanne's mistake for this collection. Her plan has Eli at 100 % on Rotterdam. But Eli is also Larder's platform engineer, and the week of 23 March holds: the Rotterdam platform set-up (2½ days), the Dispatch RFC with Ben (1½), his turn on call (1), the store's internet line (1), two extras he said yes to (1½) and the alert fix from the quality review (½). Eight days of work in a five-day week. He hasn't complained. He's just slower, and every lead waiting on him is slower too.

  • Each plan was fine; together they weren't.
  • The project manager saw only her project's slice of Eli.
  • Overload shows up as delay, not as a complaint.

3How it solves it one chart per key person, across all their work

Sanne sits down with Eli on 24 March and draws his load for six weeks, everything he does, not just Rotterdam: 80, 100, 160, 120, 70, 60 %. Then they move things. The on-call week is swapped with another engineer (Ana arranges it). Ben takes the Dispatch code changes; Eli only reviews. The alert fix moves a week, inside its float. The store dashboard, one of the extras, is paused (three days later the extras list makes it “after opening”). The zone checks move to the week of 6 April, when Eli has room. After: 80, 100, 100, 100, 95, 75 %. No milestone moved: that's smoothing, not levelling. Sanne also changes her plan's assumption: key people are planned at 80 %, because meetings, support and surprises take the rest.

  1. Pick the people everyone waits for

    The bottlenecks.

  2. Draw their load by week, across all their work

    Ask them; don't guess.

  3. Find the weeks over 100 %

    Or over 80 % for key people.

  4. Stop what shouldn't be there

    Extras, nice-to-haves.

Vocabulary for the exam

Resource histogram
A chart of a person's or team's load over time.
Over-allocation
More work assigned than a person can do in the time.
Resource smoothing
Moving work within its float so no dates change.
Resource levelling
Moving work to remove over-allocation, even if dates change.
Utilisation
The share of time spent on planned work.
Multitasking cost
Time lost switching between pieces of work.

＋Going deeper histograms, smoothing vs levelling, 80 % plans, and the cost of switching

Overload is the most common hidden cause of slow projects, because it shows up as delay rather than as a complaint. This chapter shows how to see it, the three ways to fix it, why 100 % is already too much, and what switching between projects costs.

1See it: the histogram, across everything

A resource histogram is a bar per week of how much work one person has. The trap is drawing it for your project only. Eli was at about 50 % on Rotterdam in the week of 23 March, and at 160 % overall. Only he knew the whole picture, and he didn't think it was his place to say so.

Draw it for the few people everyone waits for (here: Eli, Ben, Tomás), and draw it with them. It takes ten minutes each and is usually the first time anyone has added it up.

2Fix it: smooth, level, re-assign, stop

  • Stop work that shouldn't be there. The store dashboard was an extra; stopping it cost nothing.
  • Re-assign: someone else does it. Ben took the Dispatch code; another engineer took on-call.
  • Smooth: move work within its float. The alert fix and the zone checks had float before their milestones.
  • Level: move work until nobody is overloaded, even if a date moves. Rotterdam didn't need to; if it had, the date that moved would have gone to Joost as a change.

3Why 100 % is already too much

A person planned at 100 % on project work has no time for the things that always happen: meetings, questions from colleagues, support, illness, the unplanned. In practice their plan slips by exactly that amount, every week. Planning key people at about 80 % isn't generosity; it's accuracy.

The same logic applies to machines and teams: queues grow sharply as utilisation approaches 100 %. The last 20 % of a person's week is where the waiting time of everyone downstream comes from.

4The cost of switching

Six pieces of work in one week don't just add up to six pieces of work. Each switch costs time to pick up where you left off. Eli's 160 % week would probably have been nearer 180 % in practice.

The cure is fewer things at once, not more hours: finish one, then start the next. That's why “stop” comes first in the list above, and why the extras list (Vol. 3) was also a resource tool.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small team

  • Ask at the weekly meeting: anyone over 100 %?
  • Move one thing.
  • No charts.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • A load chart for each bottleneck person, across all work.
  • Smooth, re-assign, stop.
  • Plan key people at 80 %.

Heavy

large programmes

  • Resource management tools across projects.
  • Portfolio-level capacity planning.
  • Formal levelling in the schedule.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Your project only

    Others' work is invisible. Fix → draw everything the person does.

  • Planning at 100 %

    The plan slips every week. Fix → about 80 % for key people.

  • More hours as the fix

    Burn-out, mistakes. Fix → stop, re-assign, smooth.

  • Waiting for a complaint

    Overload shows as delay. Fix → ask the bottleneck people weekly.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When one person is on everyone's critical path.
  • When work slows for no visible reason.
  • Before a busy week.
  • When people work on several projects.

Skip it when…

  • A dedicated team with nothing else to do (rare).
