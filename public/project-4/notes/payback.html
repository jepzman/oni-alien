Was It Worth It?

TL;DR

Being on budget says how well money was controlled, not whether it was worth spending. Put the investment next to what the result brings back. Payback: how long until the money returns. Return on investment: how much comes back per euro. Net present value: what future money is worth today. Use the simplest one your sponsor trusts, write the assumptions down, and check them later. Often the most useful number is the value of time: what each week earlier is worth.

1The situation Larder, 22 May 2026

The closing report says €379.6k of €380k. The steering group is pleased. Femke reads it twice and asks the question it doesn't answer: “and what do we get back for it, and when?” On budget says nothing about worth. A launch that cost exactly what was planned could still never pay for itself.

  • Budget control and value are different questions.
  • The value arrives after the project closes.
  • Nobody has written down the sum the investors will ask about.

2Real-life analogy solar panels

Solar panels for a house cost €6,000 and save about €100 a month. They pay for themselves in five years and keep saving for twenty. Whether the installer finished on budget matters, but it's a small part of the story. The question that decided the purchase was how long until they pay back, and how sure you are about the saving.

3How it solves it the investment, what comes back, and the value of speed

Sanne asks Marco for the sum and writes it into the closing report. Investment: €379.6k. Each Rotterdam order contributes about €4 after picking, delivery and store costs (Marco's figure). In week four Rotterdam did 590 orders a day; Marco assumes 550 from June. That's about €66k a month, so the launch pays back in about six months of steady trade: around December 2026. Then the number that matters most for the next city: each week earlier a city opens is worth about €15k (7 × 550 × €4). Opening Utrecht in eight weeks instead of Rotterdam's fifteen is worth about €100k, which is what the playbook is for. The assumptions are written down, with a date to check them: the start of Utrecht.

  1. Write down the investment

    All of it, including reserve draws.

  2. Estimate what comes back per period

    From the business's own numbers.

  3. Work out payback

    Investment ÷ return per period.

  4. Work out the value of a week

    What speed is worth.

  5. Write the assumptions

    And who owns each.

  6. Check them later

    At a named date.

Vocabulary for the exam

Payback period
How long until the money returned equals the investment.
Return on investment (ROI)
Gain from the investment, compared with its cost.
Net present value (NPV)
Future cash flows discounted to today's money, minus the investment.
Contribution
What each sale leaves after its variable costs.
Cost of delay
What waiting costs per week.
Benefit review
A later check of whether the value arrived.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small changes

  • One sentence: what it saves or earns, and when.
  • No discounting.
  • Checked once.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Payback from the business's own figures.
  • The value of a week.
  • Assumptions with owners and a check date.

Heavy

large investments

  • NPV and IRR over several years.
  • Sensitivity analysis.
  • Formal benefits realisation reviews.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • On budget = success

    Worth is never asked. Fix → put the return next to the cost.

  • Invented returns

    Nobody believes them. Fix → use the business's own figures.

  • No assumptions

    Can't be checked. Fix → write them, with owners.

  • Never checked again

    The sum stays a hope. Fix → a named date to check.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • In the business case before.
  • In the closing report after.
  • When choosing between projects.
  • When arguing for speed.

Skip it when…

  • Mandatory work (law, safety) where the choice isn't financial.

8Self-check quiz

€379.6k back at €66k a month. Payback?

About 5.8 months.

What is a week earlier worth for a new city?

About €15k (7 days × 550 orders × €4).

What isn't counted in the investment?

Larder's own people's time, rent and lease, brand value.

Why write the assumptions down?

So they can be checked later: the €4, the 550 a day, the rival's prices.

At Utrecht's start Rotterdam is doing 420 a day, not 550. What changes?

Payback stretches to about 7½ months; the benefits review says why, and Joost owns the order assumption.

try it

For your project, write three numbers: what it cost, what it brings back per month, and what a week earlier would be worth. Write the assumptions and who owns each. When will you check them?

remember!!

On budget is the installer's story. Payback is the house's.
