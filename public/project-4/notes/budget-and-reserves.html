a.k.a. developing the budget: cost baseline, reserves and the S-curve

How Much Do We Set Aside?

TL;DR

A budget is the cost estimates added up, plus reserves, spread over time. The estimates make the cost baseline: what the plan should cost if things go as planned. On top sits a contingency reserve for risks you know about, visible, with a rule for drawing on it, and sometimes a management reserve for surprises nobody can name, held by the sponsor. Spread over time, the baseline becomes an S-curve: slow start, steep middle, flat end. Money with no name gets spent; reserves need a name, a size and a gauge.

1The situation Larder, 30 January 2026

The bottom-up estimate leaves €20k under the €380k. Joost sees it at once: a nicer shop sign, a launch event, better uniforms. Marco sees the same €20k and asks what happens if the builder is late, if hiring costs more, if the cold room needs a part nobody quoted. Same money, two plans for it. If it isn't named and fenced today, it will be spent on the first good idea, and there'll be nothing left for the first bad day.

  • Unnamed money gets spent.
  • A total says nothing about when cash is needed.
  • Nobody has said what the spare is for, or who may use it.

2Real-life analogy the spare quarter tank

For a long car journey you fill the tank for the planned route, plus about a quarter tank for detours and traffic. The spare isn't for a longer route chosen on a whim; it's for the things that might happen. And it has a gauge: everyone in the car can see how much is left, and the driver says out loud when they start using it.

3How it solves it name the reserve, spread the baseline over time

Sanne writes the budget in three layers. The cost baseline: the five lines, €360k. The reserve: €20k, for known risks. She lists the five things that could cost money (late builder, hiring above rate, a cold-room part, equipment prices, a lost week of training) and their likely total, about €10k; Marco doubles it for a first city. The rule: drawn only through a logged change, and reported every Friday. Then she spreads the baseline over the months from the plan: €4k by the end of January, €64k by February, €238k by March, €352k by April, €360k by May. That S-curve is what Marco needs for cash, and what earned value will measure against. Joost's sign, event and uniforms go on a list for the operations budget.

Vocabulary for the exam

Cost baseline
The approved, time-phased budget excluding management reserve.
Contingency reserve
Money for identified risks, controlled within the project.
Management reserve
Money for unforeseen work, held by the sponsor or organisation.
Budget at completion (BAC)
The total of the cost baseline.
S-curve
Cumulative planned spend over time; usually slow, steep, then flat.
Funding limit
How much money is available in a given period.

＋Going deeper how a budget is stacked, sizing the reserve, and using it

“Add a bit for contingency” is how most reserves are made, and why most disappear. This chapter shows the layers of a budget, a simple way to size a reserve from the risks you know, the S-curve and why finance cares about its shape, and Rotterdam's reserve from €20k to €0.4k.

1The layers

A budget stacks up in layers, each controlled by someone:

  • Work package estimates add up to lines (here, five).
  • The lines add up to the cost baseline: what the plan should cost. Progress and earned value are measured against it.
  • A contingency reserve covers risks you have named. The project manager manages it, by a rule.
  • A management reserve covers surprises nobody can name. The sponsor holds it, outside the baseline.

Larder chose not to keep a separate management reserve for Rotterdam: Femke treats the company's cash runway as that. It's a legitimate choice, and writing “none” makes it a decision rather than an oversight.

2Sizing a reserve from the risks

A percentage (“add 10 %”) is better than nothing. Better still is to list the risks that would cost money and put a rough likely cost on each: Rotterdam's five came to about €10k. Vol. 5 shows the proper sum (expected monetary value); the idea is already here: the reserve should be about the size of what's likely to go wrong, plus a margin for how new the work is.

Marco doubled it because Rotterdam was the first new city. That judgment is the kind a finance lead is for. Writing both numbers down (€10k from the list, €20k chosen) means the next city can start from Rotterdam's real draws instead of a guess.

3The S-curve, and why finance cares

Spread over time, the baseline makes an S: a slow start (planning, deposits), a steep middle (March and April: the builder, the cold room, equipment, training) and a flat end (retention, final invoices). The steep part is what a CFO watches: €174k in March alone in Rotterdam's plan. If the company's cash is tight, the shape matters as much as the total.

The S-curve is also the planned value line that earned value measures against (three notes on).

4Using the reserve: the gauge

Rotterdam's reserve went €20k → €6k (fridges with doors, 2 Feb), → €1k (weekend work, 23 Mar), → €0.4k (the 4G router, 1 Apr). Every draw was a logged change with a decider. None was a sign, an event or uniforms.

Two habits made that work. The reserve had its own line in every Friday status, so the steering group watched it shrink in real time. And when it reached €1k, Sanne said in writing that it could no longer absorb a surprise, which turned the next problem into a decision for Joost instead of a quiet overspend.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • A total and a small spare.
  • Say what the spare is for.
  • No S-curve.

Standard

Rotterdam

  • Baseline by line, reserve sized from known risks.
  • Monthly S-curve.
  • Reserve reported weekly; drawn by change.

Heavy

large or public projects

  • Contingency per risk; management reserve held separately.
  • Time-phased baselines by control account.
  • Funding released in tranches against the curve.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Unnamed slack

    Spent on the first idea. Fix → name it: reserve, with a rule.

  • Hidden padding in lines

    Nobody knows the real reserve. Fix → honest lines, one visible reserve.

  • Only a total

    Cash runs out in March. Fix → spread it over time.

  • No gauge

    The reserve vanishes quietly. Fix → report it every week.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • After the cost estimate, before spending.
  • When a gap appears between estimate and budget.
  • When the sponsor asks for a cash forecast.
  • At each new phase.

Skip it when…

  • Tiny work with no real risk of overrun.
