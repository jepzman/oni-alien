Changing Things Safely

Volume 3 of The IT Operations Notebook: change enablement, release and deployment, testing and keeping the platform current, from the ITIL 4 practices with the SRE and DORA view of safe, frequent change. 15 notes in four collections: deciding about changes, safety nets, moving big things, and keeping it current. Every note follows Eli at Larder from January to June 2027, in the same months as Ana's warehouse migration and Sanne's Antwerp plan.

the story so far

January 2027. The Christmas failover cost 38 minutes and about 900 orders, and on 12 January Femke chose option B (ADR 0017): failover in under two minutes, and no changes in the windows that matter. Two months earlier a tidy-up of network rules, applied at the start of the evening peak, had stopped Dispatch for 25 minutes. Larder now makes about 200 changes a month, and 2027 adds a warehouse move store by store, two new cities and a picker app that ships every week. This volume follows Eli from January to June 2027: deciding which changes need a second look, when nothing may change, how to undo things, and keeping an ageing platform patched and supported.

Deciding about changes which changes need a second look, who looks, and when nothing changes

  1. 01Which Changes Need a Second Look?Sort changes into standard (pre-approved recipes), normal (reviewed) and emergency, so most go fast and a few get care.p. 01
  2. 02Do We Need a Change Board?Peer review by someone who knows the system beats a weekly board; keep a board only for the rare high-risk change.p. 02
  3. 03When Must We Not Change Anything?A change calendar with critical windows and freezes, enforced by the pipeline, with a clear way to make exceptions.p. 03
  4. 04Release or Deploy?Deploying puts code in production; releasing lets people use it. Separate them, and each gets safer.p. 04
  5. 05How Risky Is This Change?Five quick questions give a risk score that decides how much review, testing and timing a change needs.p. 05

Safety nets undo it, test it, handle the urgent one, and count what fails

  1. 06How Do We Undo It?Every change has a tested way back, or a written plan to go forward when there is no way back.p. 06
  2. 07Is It Really Ready?Test where it matters: in an environment that is honestly like production, and in production itself, carefully.p. 07
  3. 08The Urgent Fix at 17:00 on a FridayEmergency changes: fewer checks, never zero, two people, and the paperwork afterwards.p. 08
  4. 09Which Changes Fail?Four delivery numbers (frequency, lead time, failure rate, time to restore) show whether change is getting safer.p. 09

Moving big things store cutovers, new cities and a fleet of handhelds

  1. 10Cutting Over a StoreA cutover plan for moving a store to a new system: shadow run, go / no-go, a quiet hour, and a way back.p. 10
  2. 11A New City by ChecklistThe IT side of opening a city as a versioned runbook that gets shorter with every launch.p. 11
  3. 12Updating 120 HandheldsRoll out app versions to store devices in rings, outside store hours, with a way to stop and roll back.p. 12

Keeping it current who may touch production, patching and end-of-life

  1. 13Who May Touch Production?Few standing permissions, access on request for a time, and a break-glass account that alarms when used.p. 13
  2. 14Patching Without FearA patch routine: automatic for the small, a monthly window for the rest, and machines rebuilt rather than repaired.p. 14
  3. 15The Database Runs Out of SupportKeep a lifecycle register of versions and end-of-support dates, and upgrade on your schedule, not the vendor's.p. 15

★The nine operations ratings what a good operations practice is judged on

Every note scores the pattern from 1 (poor) to 5 (great) on the same nine things, comparing an app without the pattern to the same app with it.

User impact
Fewer customers, pickers and couriers hurt when something breaks.
Reliability
Things fail less, and fail smaller.
Recovery speed
Time from “it's broken” to “it works again”.
Change safety
Changes rarely cause incidents, and can be undone.
Visibility
Anyone on call can see what runs, what's wrong and what changed.
Toil reduction
Repetitive manual work goes down instead of up.
Cost awareness
Money and people's time are spent where they matter.
Sustainable on-call
The load is shared and bearable; nobody is the only one who knows.
Low overhead
Time and ceremony the practice takes. High = light.

first law

Most outages start with a change, and most changes are fine. Make the safe ones fast and boring, look twice at the risky few, and never make one you can't undo.
