The Database Runs Out of Support

TL;DR

Every layer you run, from database to framework to operating system to device, has an end-of-support date, after which security fixes stop or cost extra. Keep a lifecycle register of versions and dates, review it quarterly, and upgrade at least six months before the end, in planned, reversible steps. Otherwise the vendor's calendar decides your priorities, usually in a hurry.

1The situation Larder, February 2027

In February a letter arrives from the cloud provider. PostgreSQL 13 left community support in November 2025, and from 1 April 2027 extended support will cost extra, about €650 a month. Eli checks the rest. Larder Core runs Django 3.2 (support ended April 2024) on Python 3.8 (October 2024) on Ubuntu 20.04 (standard support ended May 2025). Every layer of Larder Core is already out of support, and nobody had a list.

  • Security fixes stopped arriving, silently.
  • Upgrades that could have been small steps are now big jumps.
  • The vendor's deadline now sets the plan.

2Real-life analogy the family's expiry dates

Passports, the boiler service, the car's inspection, the home insurance: each has an expiry date. A sensible family keeps one list with the dates and renews things a few months early, in a quiet month. Otherwise you find out at the airport the night before, when everything costs more and takes longer.

3How it solves it a register, six months' notice, and a catch-up plan

Eli starts ops/lifecycle.md in March: 23 components, each with its version, end-of-support date, owner and planned upgrade. The rule: upgrade at least six months before end of support, reviewed every quarter. Then comes the 2027 catch-up:

  • Postgres 13 → 16: a high-risk change (score 14). A restore is practised on 11 May. On Tuesday 18 May at 05:00, before the morning window, logical replication to a new server lets the switch take 4 minutes. The old server keeps replicating back for a week as the way back.
  • Ubuntu 20.04 → 24.04: the VMs are rebuilt from code in June, one per night.
  • Django 3.2 → 4.2 by July and → 5.2 by October, with Python along with it, as normal changes in the orders team's 20 % tech-debt time.

Larder pays extended support for April and May only (about €1,300), not forever. The register also covers the handhelds' Android versions, which matter in vol. 5.

  1. List every layer and its version

    Database, OS, language, framework, devices, SaaS plans.

  2. Add end-of-support dates and owners

    From vendors' published lifecycles.

  3. Set a notice rule

    Upgrade at least six months before the end.

  4. Plan catch-ups as reversible steps

    Risk score, way back, practised restore.

  5. Pay extended support only as a bridge

    With an end date.

  6. Review the register quarterly

    New components, new dates.

Vocabulary for the exam

End of support (EOL)
The date after which a vendor stops fixing a version.
Extended support
Paid fixes after the normal end, usually for a limited time.
Lifecycle register
The list of components, versions and end dates.
Major upgrade
A jump between major versions; often not reversible in place.
Logical replication
Copying data changes to another database server, even across versions.
Technical debt
Work postponed that costs more the longer it waits.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Waiting for the letter

    The vendor sets the plan. Fix → a register with dates.

  • Skipping versions for years

    Small steps become one big jump. Fix → upgrade regularly.

  • Extended support forever

    A cost that hides the debt. Fix → a bridge with an end date.

  • Forgetting devices and SaaS plans

    Handhelds stop getting updates. Fix → the register covers every layer.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Nobody knows which versions you run.
  • A vendor announces an end of support.
  • Before a security questionnaire.
  • Upgrades keep being postponed.

Skip it when…

  • Fully managed services that upgrade themselves: track their notices instead.

8Self-check quiz

What's in Larder's lifecycle register?

Every component's version, end-of-support date, owner and planned upgrade.

What's the notice rule?

Upgrade at least six months before end of support.

How was the Postgres 16 upgrade made reversible?

Logical replication to a new server; the old one kept replicating back for a week as the way back.

Why pay extended support for only two months?

As a bridge to the upgrade, not a way to postpone it forever.

What risk score did the Postgres upgrade get, and why?

14: everything depends on it, hard to undo in place, first time, a big jump.

what would you do?A SaaS tool you use announces it will retire your plan in nine months. Where does this go, and what happens next?

Into the lifecycle register with the date and an owner; the owner plans the move to a new plan or tool well before the six-month mark, as a normal change.

try it

List every layer of your most important service with its version. Look up each end-of-support date. How many are already past, and which one ends next? Put the upgrade six months before it.

remember!!

Every layer has an expiry date. Keep the list, upgrade six months early, and don't let the vendor's letter set your plan.
