Do We Need a Change Board?

TL;DR

A weekly change advisory board feels safe, but research and experience agree it slows change without making it safer: reviewers far from the work can't judge it, and changes pile into bigger, riskier batches. Better: peer review by someone who knows that system, in the pull request, for normal changes, and a short board only for the rare high-risk change. Give leaders numbers, not meetings.

1The situation Larder, 14 January – 4 February 2027

After the Christmas outage Marco proposes a change board: every non-deploy change approved at a weekly meeting. Eli agrees to a three-week trial. Six people, one hour each Thursday, 41 changes. The median wait for a non-deploy change goes from 1 day to 6. Most reviewers can't judge database or network changes, and approve them anyway. Changes pile up for Thursday, so the 4 February batch is large, and one of its changes fails in production regardless.

  • Approval by people who can't judge the change is a ritual, not a check.
  • Waiting a week makes batches bigger, and bigger batches are riskier.
  • The people who can judge (Eli, Bram, Ben) spend an hour a week approving others' recipes.

2Real-life analogy the newspaper's editors

A newspaper once sent every article to the editor-in-chief. The paper came out late, and it was no better: the chief couldn't check a football report and a court report equally well. Now each article is checked by an editor who knows the subject, the chief sees only the few that could get the paper sued, and corrections go out quickly.

3How it solves it knowing reviewers for normal changes, a short board for the risky few

On Monday 8 February the board is replaced, with Marco's agreement, by three things:

  • Normal changes are reviewed in the pull request by one person who knows that system, from a short list per system (network and database: Eli, Bram; Dispatch: Ben; payments: Cho; devices: Dee). The risk score and the way back go in the description.
  • High-risk changes (score 12 or more, about two a month) get a 20-minute review with Eli, the system's owner and whoever is affected. It can be done in writing.
  • Marco gets numbers, not meetings: each month, the number of changes, the change failure rate and the list of high-risk changes with their outcome.

The median wait for a normal change drops below one day. In February–June the change failure rate falls from 6 % to 3 % (this volume). Eli tells Marco that the board's best effect was making everyone write down how to undo their change, and that habit stays.

  1. Ask what the board actually catches

    Count approvals, waits and failures.

  2. Name reviewers per system

    People who can really judge the change.

  3. Review in the pull request

    Risk score and way back in the description.

  4. Keep a short board for high risk

    A score threshold, 20 minutes, can be in writing.

  5. Give leaders numbers

    Changes, failure rate, high-risk list monthly.

  6. Keep what the board taught

    Written undo plans.

Vocabulary for the exam

Change advisory board (CAB)
A group that reviews and advises on changes before they are made.
Change authority
The person or group allowed to approve a kind of change.
Peer review
Review by a colleague who knows the system, close to the work.
Batch size
How many changes go out together; bigger batches fail bigger.
Lead time for changes
Time from a change being ready to it being live.
Approval theatre
Approval by people who can't judge what they approve.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Approval as reassurance

    The board feels safe and checks nothing. Fix → count what it catches.

  • Reviewers who can't judge

    Approved because the author seems confident. Fix → named reviewers per system.

  • Weekly batches

    Changes wait and pile up. Fix → review when ready, in the pull request.

  • No board at all for the truly risky

    A major database change gets one tired reviewer. Fix → a short high-risk review.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Someone proposes approving every change after an outage.
  • Changes wait days for a meeting.
  • Reviewers approve what they can't judge.
  • Auditors ask how changes are authorised (show the log).

Skip it when…

  • Regulations that require a named board: keep it, but feed it pre-reviewed, scored changes.

8Self-check quiz

What did the three-week board trial show?

Waits went from 1 day to 6, reviewers approved what they couldn't judge, batches grew, and a change still failed.

Who reviews a normal change now?

One person who knows that system, in the pull request.

When does a change still get a board review?

When its risk score is 12 or more: about two a month, 20 minutes, can be in writing.

What does Marco get instead of meetings?

A monthly line: number of changes, change failure rate, and the high-risk list with outcomes.

Why do bigger batches matter?

More changes at once fail bigger and are harder to undo or diagnose.

what would you do?After an outage, the CEO says “from now on, I want to approve every change”. What do you offer instead?

A monthly page: number of changes, change failure rate, high-risk changes and their outcome, plus the promise that high-risk ones get a short review with the right people. Explain that approval by someone who can't judge slows change without making it safer.

try it

For a month, note every change approval in your team: who approved it, could they really judge it, and how long did the change wait? Which approvals would a knowing peer have done better and faster?

remember!!

Approval is only a check if the approver can judge the change. Review close to the work, and keep a board for the risky few.
