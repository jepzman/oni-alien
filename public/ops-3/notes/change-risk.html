How Risky Is This Change?

TL;DR

A risk score replaces gut feeling with the same few questions every time: how much breaks if it goes wrong, how fast can we undo it, where was it tested, when will it happen, and have we done it before? Score each 1–3 and add them up. The total decides how much review, testing, timing and watching the change gets. It takes a minute, and it catches the change that “feels like a three-minute job”.

1The situation Larder, late January 2027

With normal changes now reviewed, the queue in late January holds a database setting, a network rule, a new Dispatch version, a label printer driver and the picker app's weekly release. Each reviewer asks the same question, “how careful should we be?”, and answers it by feel. The 29 October network tidy-up had felt like a three-minute job too.

  • Gut feeling depends on who's reviewing, and how tired they are.
  • Two similar changes get very different care.
  • Nobody can explain afterwards why a change was treated lightly.

2Real-life analogy the mountain hut's avalanche checklist

Mountain huts give walkers a short avalanche checklist: how steep is the slope, how much new snow, is it warm today, how big is the group, have you done this route before. Each answer adds points. Low: go. Middle: go with care, and turn back early. High: not today, or only with a guide. Experienced walkers use it too, because experience is exactly what makes a dangerous slope look easy.

3How it solves it five questions, 1–3 each, and the total decides the path

From 1 February every normal change carries a score in its pull request. There are five questions, each scored 1–3:

  • Blast radius: one store (1), one city (2), everything (3).
  • Way back: a flag or instant undo (1), a redeploy (2), hard or impossible (3).
  • Tested: somewhere honestly like production (1), staging only (2), not at all (3).
  • Timing: quiet hours (1), normal hours (2), near a window or freeze (3).
  • New?: done many times (1), once or twice (2), first time (3).

A total of 5–7 is low (a recipe, or one reviewer). 8–11 is medium (a knowing reviewer and a tested way back). 12–15 is high (a high-risk review, a planned time, someone watching for 30 minutes after). The picker app's weekly release scores 8, and the Postgres major upgrade (this volume) 14. Re-scored, 29 October's tidy-up gets 13: everything, a redeploy, untested, 18:10, first time. The score doesn't make changes safe. It makes people look at them the same way.

  1. Pick five questions

    Blast radius, way back, tested, timing, new.

  2. Score 1–3 each, honestly

    In the pull request, by the author.

  3. The reviewer checks the score

    Disagreeing is part of the review.

  4. Let the total choose the path

    Low, medium, high: different care.

  5. Lower the score by design

    A flag or a smaller step often moves a change down.

  6. Compare scores with failures

    Adjust the questions after a few months.

Vocabulary for the exam

Risk score
A quick, consistent estimate of how dangerous a change is.
Blast radius
How much breaks if the change goes wrong.
Reversibility
How fast and how completely a change can be undone.
Change impact
What a change affects: services, people, data.
Watcher
A named person who watches signals for 30 minutes after a risky change.
Risk reduction by design
Changing how a change is made (smaller, flagged) to lower its score.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Scoring to get through

    Everything scores 7. Fix → the reviewer checks the score; scores are compared with failures.

  • Too many questions

    A form nobody fills in. Fix → five, 1–3 each.

  • Score as permission

    “It's low, so it's safe.” Fix → the score sets care, not certainty.

  • Never adjusting

    Questions that don't predict failures. Fix → compare with real failures every quarter.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Reviewers judge risk by feel.
  • Similar changes get different treatment.
  • You need to explain to auditors how risky changes are handled.
  • After a “small” change caused an incident.

Skip it when…

  • Standard changes: the recipe already is the risk decision.

8Self-check quiz

What are the five questions?

Blast radius, way back, tested where, timing, and how new it is.

What happens at 12 or more?

A high-risk review, a planned time, and a watcher for 30 minutes after.

What would the 29 October tidy-up have scored?

13: everything, a redeploy, untested, near the peak, first time. High risk.

How can a change lower its own score?

By design: a canary or a flag lowers blast radius and way back.

Does a low score mean the change is safe?

No: it sets how much care it gets, not whether it will fail.

what would you do?A change scores 11, but the author says “it's basically the same as last week's”. The reviewer disagrees with “new?”. What happens?

They talk it through in the review; if last week's was different in a way that matters, “new?” goes up and the change becomes high risk (12). The disagreement is the point of the score.

try it

Score your last five changes with the five questions. Did the ones that went wrong score higher? Which question would have changed how you handled them?

remember!!

Ask the same five questions every time: how much breaks, how fast back, tested where, when, done before? The total picks the care.
