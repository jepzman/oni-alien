Which Changes Need a Second Look?

TL;DR

Not every change deserves the same care. Sort them into three kinds. Standard changes are low-risk, repeated recipes that are pre-approved and just go. Normal changes get a risk score, a reviewer who knows the system and a tested way back. Emergency changes restore service during an incident, with fewer checks but never none. Most changes become fast and boring, and the risky few get real attention.

1The situation Larder, January 2027

After the Christmas failover Eli counts December's changes: 212. That's 160 deploys through the pipeline, 31 infrastructure and network changes, and 21 feature-flag changes. Each one was handled however its author thought best. From October to December, 9 incidents followed a change, and 7 of them were infrastructure or network changes, which are only 15 % of all changes. One was the 29 October network rule.

  • Risky changes get the same casual treatment as routine deploys.
  • Routine changes sometimes get stuck waiting for someone to look.
  • Nobody can say which changes need a second pair of eyes.

2Real-life analogy the hospital's medicine rules

On a hospital ward, a nurse gives paracetamol by a standing order, with no doctor needed. A new antibiotic needs a doctor's prescription and a pharmacist's check. In a cardiac arrest the crash team acts at once and writes it up afterwards. The path is chosen by risk, not by who's asking or how busy the ward is.

3How it solves it three paths, and most changes on the fast one

From Monday 18 January every change takes one of three paths:

  • Standard: one of 9 recipes, written and pre-approved. Examples: a deploy through the pipeline with a canary, a feature flag outside a critical window, enrolling a device, a new user through the joiners checklist, adding a DNS record. These go straight ahead, and the change feed logs them. A new recipe becomes standard after 10 clean runs.
  • Normal: anything that isn't a recipe, however small it feels. It needs a risk score (this volume), a reviewer who knows that system, and a tested way back.
  • Emergency: a change to restore service during an incident. The incident lead approves, two people do it, and it's written up within a day (this volume).

In February the split is 87 % standard, 12 % normal, 1 % emergency. Network and database changes are never recipes. If PR 233 came today, it would be a normal change.

  1. Count your changes by kind

    Deploys, infrastructure, network, flags, vendors.

  2. Find the incidents that followed changes

    Which kinds are risky in practice?

  3. Write recipes for the safe, repeated ones

    Pre-approved: they just go.

  4. Everything else is normal

    Risk score, a knowing reviewer, a tested way back.

  5. Define the emergency path

    Fewer checks, never zero, written up after.

  6. Promote recipes after clean runs

    And demote one that causes an incident.

Vocabulary for the exam

Change
Adding, modifying or removing anything that could affect a service.
Standard change
Low-risk, repeated, pre-approved; follows a recipe.
Normal change
Needs assessment and authorisation before it's made.
Emergency change
Must be made as soon as possible, usually to restore service.
Change authority
Whoever may approve a kind of change: at Larder, a knowing reviewer.
Recipe
The written steps for a standard change.

4The maturity ladder where are we, and what is the next rung?

1By hand

everyone decides alone

  • No kinds of change.
  • Risky and routine treated the same.

2Written down Larder now

three paths, written recipes

  • Most changes standard.
  • Normal ones reviewed by someone who knows.

3Scripted

the pipeline knows the path

  • Recipes enforced in the pipeline.
  • Normal changes can't merge without score and reviewer.

4Automatic

most change is standard

  • Risky changes split into safe recipes.
  • Normal changes rare.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Treating all changes the same

    Either everything waits, or nothing is checked. Fix → three paths by risk.

  • “It's small, so it's standard”

    Small network changes stop cities. Fix → only written recipes are standard.

  • Recipes that never change

    A recipe that caused an incident stays pre-approved. Fix → demote it to normal.

  • No emergency path

    People skip the process entirely under pressure. Fix → a light path, written in advance.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Incidents follow changes.
  • Some changes wait for days, others aren't checked at all.
  • After an outage, when people want to “approve everything”.
  • Before adding a change board.

Skip it when…

  • A tiny team deploying one app: a pipeline with tests and a canary may be all you need.

8Self-check quiz

What makes a change standard?

It's low-risk and repeated, follows a written recipe, and is pre-approved.

What does a normal change need at Larder?

A risk score, a reviewer who knows that system, and a tested way back.

When is a change an emergency change?

When it must be made at once, usually to restore service during an incident.

Why are network changes never standard?

They caused most change-related incidents, including 29 October.

How does a recipe become standard?

After 10 clean runs; and it's demoted again if it causes an incident.

what would you do?Dee wants to change a database setting “just slightly” on a Tuesday afternoon. It isn't a recipe. Which path?

Normal: a risk score, a reviewer who knows the database (Eli or Bram), and a tested way back, however small it feels.

try it

Count last month's changes by kind and mark which ones were followed by an incident. Write three recipes for your safest, most repeated changes. Which kind of change must never be standard?

remember!!

Make the routine fast and boring, and give the risky few a real second look. The path depends on risk, not on how small it feels.
