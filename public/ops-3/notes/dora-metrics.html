Which Changes Fail?

TL;DR

Four numbers show whether change is getting faster and safer: deployment frequency, lead time for changes (commit to production), change failure rate (share of changes that need a fix, a rollback or cause an incident), and time to restore after a failed change. Research (DORA) shows the best teams are good at all four: speed and stability go together. Compute them from your pipeline and incident list, look at them by kind of change, and use them for learning, never for ranking people.

1The situation Larder, February 2027

After the change board trial, Marco asks: “Are we getting safer or not?” Eli has a change feed listing every change since October, and an incident list. Nothing connects them. He can tell the stories of 29 October and of the change board, but he can't say how many changes fail, which kinds fail most, or whether things are getting better.

  • Without numbers, every debate about process is opinions.
  • “Slow down to be safe” and “speed up” both sound reasonable.
  • Nobody can see which kind of change is actually risky.

2Real-life analogy the bakery's four numbers

A good bakery keeps four numbers on the wall: batches baked a day, time from mixing to shelf, batches thrown away, and time to bake a replacement. The surprising thing is that faster and fewer thrown away go together: small, frequent batches are easier to get right and quicker to replace. A bakery that slows down to avoid mistakes usually makes bigger batches, and bigger mistakes.

3How it solves it four numbers, by kind of change, monthly

Bram joins the change feed and the incident list in March. A change counts as failed if it needed a fix, a rollback, or caused an incident within 24 hours. Each month Marco and tech leads get four numbers:

  • Deployment frequency: about 8 a day.
  • Lead time for changes: median 2 h 10 from commit to production.
  • Change failure rate: 6 % in Q4 2026, 4 % in Q1 2027, 3 % in Q2.
  • Time to restore after a failed change: median 14 minutes.

The most useful view is by kind of change. In Q2, deploys fail 2 % of the time and flags 1 %, but infrastructure 8 %, network 11 % and database 9 %. That feeds straight back into the risk score and the reviewer list. Deploys didn't slow down while failures fell, which is exactly what the research predicts. Eli adds one rule: the numbers are for learning, never for ranking people or teams.

  1. Define a failed change

    Needed a fix, a rollback, or caused an incident within 24 h.

  2. Join the change feed and incident list

    Each incident links to its change, if any.

  3. Compute the four numbers monthly

    From data, not memory.

  4. Split by kind of change

    That's where the lessons are.

  5. Show the trend with one sentence

    What changed, and why.

  6. Never rank people by them

    People game numbers that judge them.

Vocabulary for the exam

Deployment frequency
How often changes reach production.
Lead time for changes
Time from a commit to that commit running in production.
Change failure rate
The share of changes that cause a failure needing a fix or rollback.
Time to restore
How long it takes to recover from a failed change.
DORA
DevOps Research and Assessment: the research programme behind these four numbers.
Goodhart's law
A number that becomes a target stops being a good measure.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Numbers as targets

    Teams split changes to raise frequency. Fix → use them to learn, not to judge.

  • No definition of failure

    Everyone counts differently. Fix → write it down: fix, rollback or incident within 24 h.

  • One number alone

    Fast but failing, or safe but stuck. Fix → all four together.

  • Only the total

    The risky kinds hide in the average. Fix → split by kind of change.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Someone asks whether you're getting safer or faster.
  • Process changes are argued by opinion.
  • After introducing a new change practice.
  • Before deciding to slow down “for safety”.

Skip it when…

  • Very few changes a month: describe each one instead.

8Self-check quiz

What are the four key metrics?

Deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore.

How does Larder define a failed change?

One that needed a fix or rollback, or caused an incident within 24 hours.

What did splitting by kind show?

Network (11 %), database (9 %) and infrastructure (8 %) changes fail far more than deploys (2 %).

Do speed and stability trade off?

The research says no: the best teams are good at both, because small, frequent changes are safer.

Why never rank people with these numbers?

Numbers that judge people get gamed (Goodhart's law) and stop telling the truth.

what would you do?A manager suggests a bonus for the team with the lowest change failure rate. What do you say?

That it will be gamed: teams will avoid risky but necessary changes or stop recording failures. Use the numbers to choose improvements together, not to reward or rank.

try it

Take last month's changes and incidents. Mark which incidents followed a change within 24 hours. Compute your change failure rate overall and by kind of change. Which kind surprised you?

remember!!

Fast and safe go together. Count four numbers, split them by kind of change, and never use them to rank people.
