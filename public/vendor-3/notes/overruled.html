What If the Client Overrules Our Design?

TL;DR

The client owns its architecture, and sometimes it will overrule your design for reasons you don't fully share: standards, cost, people. Disagree in writing before the decision, with costs and risks. When it's made, record it (with your dissent and the risks accepted) and commit: make their choice work as if it were yours. Never say “I told you so”. A vendor that sulks gets replaced.

1The situation The Grid, design authority, 19 January 2028

At the design authority on Wednesday 19 January, Hana proposes log-based change capture for the nine core tables: about 40 seconds of delay, a licence of about £85k a year. Hollis says no.

  • MetroVolt has an integration bus, MetroBus, bought in 2014. Hollis's rule: no new integration licences while MetroBus can do the job.
  • MetroBus can poll MAVIS every 15 minutes with batch adapters. Slower, but it's MetroVolt's standard, and MetroVolt's team can support it after Wrenfield leaves.
  • Hana is furious. It was her idea since the bid, and it worked in the spike.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Hollis

Hollis has to support whatever Lodestar leaves behind, with a small team, for years. Every new tool is another licence, another skill, another thing that breaks at 3 a.m. after the vendor has gone. In a Horizon year, a new £85k licence is also a hard conversation upstairs.

“I'll be running this long after you've left.”

Wrenfield Ines and Hana

Wrenfield thinks log capture is better: fresher data, less load on MAVIS. But 15 minutes is enough for wave 1, MetroBus is a real option, and Hollis chairs the design authority. The question isn't whether Wrenfield is right; it's how to disagree well, then deliver.

“We can be right, or we can be useful. Ideally both, in that order.”

Most overruled designs aren't wrong decisions, just different weights: the client values long-term support more than the vendor does, because the client stays.

3Real-life analogy the homeowner's choice of boiler

A heating engineer recommends a modern heat pump. The homeowner chooses a boiler their local plumber can service. The good engineer writes down her advice and its reasons, then installs the boiler properly, the best boiler installation she's ever done. She doesn't fit it badly to prove a point, and she doesn't mention the heat pump every winter.

4How it works disagree in writing, record, commit

Disagree in writing, before. Hana's paper for the design authority already compared both options: delay, cost, load on MAVIS, who supports it. Ines adds one sentence: “Wrenfield recommends log capture; MetroBus is viable for wave 1 if a 15-minute delay is acceptable to Customer Operations.” Customer Ops confirms 15 minutes is fine.

Record the decision. Hollis decides MetroBus. It's written as ADR-0019: the decision, the options, the reasons (support, cost, standards), and Wrenfield's dissent with the risks MetroVolt accepts (15-minute delay; batch load on MAVIS, to be measured in the pilot). Hollis signs it. Decision log DL-028.

Commit. Hana builds the MetroBus adapters as if they were her idea, with monitoring of the delay and a load test in the pilot. It costs three weeks; it saves MetroVolt £85k a year and goes into CR-007's negotiation. Ines asks the team for one thing: nobody says “told you so”, ever, including if the pilot shows load problems.

Two weeks later, Hollis asks Hana to present the MetroBus adapters to his own team as an example of how to use it well.

  1. Compare options in writing beforehand

    Costs, risks, who supports it.

  2. Make your recommendation clear

    And say when the other option is viable.

  3. Let the owner decide

    Their architecture, their call.

  4. Record the decision and the dissent

    An ADR, signed, with risks accepted.

  5. Commit fully

    Build their choice as if it were yours.

  6. Never say “told you so”

    Measure, report facts, move on.

Vocabulary

Dissent
A recorded disagreement with a decision.

Enterprise standard
A technology the client has chosen to use everywhere.

How easily the client's own team can run and fix a system.

5What the contract says who decides architecture

Lodestar SOW · Schedule 7, §3 (design authority)v1.1 · Aug 2027
client's call

3.1 The Design Authority, chaired by MetroVolt's Head of IT Architecture, approves the architecture of the Solution.

Hollis chairs. Hollis decides. that's the deal we signed.

3.4 Where the Supplier disagrees with a decision, its recommendation and the risks MetroVolt accepts shall be recorded with the decision.

we added this in August. it's why the dissent is in ADR-0019, not in a grudge.

In plain words: the client usually owns architecture decisions. A clause that records the vendor's dissent and the risks accepted protects both sides: the client decides, and nobody can later claim the vendor didn't warn.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Fighting after the decision

    You look like you're protecting your idea. Fix → disagree before; commit after.

  • Building it badly to prove a point

    You'll own the failure. Fix → make their choice work.

  • Dissent not recorded

    Later, nobody remembers you warned. Fix → an ADR, signed.

  • “Told you so”

    The relationship never recovers. Fix → report facts, never the gloat.

  • Silent compliance with something unsafe

    Real harm. Fix → refuse in writing if it's unsafe.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • The client overrules your recommended design.
  • Standards or supportability outweigh technical preference.
  • You disagree, but the choice is safe.

Skip it when…

  • The decision is unsafe or unlawful: then disagree formally and don't build it.

11Self-check quiz

What does “disagree and commit” mean?

Argue your case before the decision, then fully support the decision made.

What went into ADR-0019 besides the decision?

The options, the reasons, Wrenfield's dissent, and the risks MetroVolt accepts.

Why did Hollis choose MetroBus?

Supportability after the vendor leaves, an existing standard, and no new licence.

What did the decision cost and save?

Three weeks of work; £85k a year of licence for MetroVolt.

When should you refuse instead of commit?

When the decision is unsafe or unlawful.

what would you do?In the pilot, MetroBus's batch load slows MAVIS's nightly run by 25 minutes. What do you report?

The fact, the measurement and options (tune the polling, move it outside the batch window, or revisit log capture for waves 2–4), referencing the accepted risk in ADR-0019. No told-you-so.

try it

Think of a decision that went against your recommendation. Write it as an ADR: options, decision, reasons, your dissent and the risks accepted. Then list three things you'd do to make the chosen option work well.

remember!!

Disagree before, in writing. Then make their choice the best version of itself.
