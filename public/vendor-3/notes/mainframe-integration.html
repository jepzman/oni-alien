How Do We Get Data Out of a Mainframe?

TL;DR

Getting data out of an old mainframe has two problems: knowing what changed and translating what you get. Test the change-capture options on real tables: reading the database log, trusting update timestamps, or comparing nightly snapshots; each table may need a different one. Then build a translation layer for copybooks, EBCDIC text and packed decimals, with a test for every field type.

1The situation The Grid, 21 October 2027

During the waves, some MetroVolt processes will still update MAVIS while Lodestar serves migrated customers. Lodestar needs to know what changed in MAVIS during the day. MAVIS can only produce a nightly extract.

  • Hana's favourite idea since the bid: read the database's change log (her “option 1 of 3, subject to discovery”).
  • Bilal's: use “last updated” timestamps. Simple, if they exist.
  • And everything MAVIS gives out comes in COBOL copybook layouts, EBCDIC text and packed decimal numbers. One wrong field definition corrupts money.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt MetroVolt's mainframe team

The mainframe team is four people who keep MAVIS running at night. Any option that touches the database log needs their time, a new licence and a change to a system they've kept stable for decades. Their first answer to new tools is no.

“Don't break the thing that never breaks.”

Wrenfield Hana

Hana needs change capture that's fast enough for the waves, cheap enough for the budget, and acceptable to the people who run the mainframe. Her favourite design may be right, but only a test on real tables will show it.

“Test all three. Let the tables choose.”

The best integration with an old system is often a mix: the fancy option where it matters, the boring one everywhere else.

3Real-life analogy news from a village with no phone

You need to know what happens in a village with no phone. You could put someone at the post office reading every letter as it's posted (fast, but they need permission), ask each house to write the date on their door when something changes (if they remember), or photograph the whole street every night and compare (slow, but it never misses anything). And everything is written in the local dialect, which you'll need to translate.

4How it works test the options on real tables, then mix

Hana runs a two-week spike with the mainframe team's blessing, read-only, in the test region.

  • Log capture on the nine core customer tables: changes arrive in about 40 seconds. It needs a commercial tool (about £85k a year) and the mainframe team to configure it.
  • Timestamps: 18 % of the tables have no reliable “last updated” column; some programs update rows without touching it.
  • Snapshot compare: works for every table, with a 24-hour delay and a large nightly comparison.

The proposal: log capture for the nine core tables, where minutes matter; snapshots for everything else. In parallel, Lottie and Bert build the translation layer: 84 copybooks turned into field definitions, EBCDIC to Unicode, packed decimals to numbers, each field type with a test against the golden master. A pound sign in EBCDIC is a different byte; the test catches it on day two.

  1. List what needs to be fresh, and how fresh

    Per table, not per system.

  2. Spike every option on real tables

    Read-only, with the owners' blessing.

  3. Measure delay, cost and effort

    Including the client's people and licences.

  4. Mix options by table

    Fancy where it matters, boring elsewhere.

  5. Build the translation layer early

    Copybooks, encodings, number formats.

  6. A test for every field type

    Money fields first.

Vocabulary

Change data capture (CDC)
Detecting and delivering changes made to a database.
Log-based capture
Reading changes from the database's own transaction log.
Snapshot compare
Comparing two full copies to find what changed.
Copybook
A COBOL definition of a record's layout.
EBCDIC
The character encoding used on IBM mainframes.
Packed decimal (COMP-3)
A compact way of storing numbers, two digits per byte.

5What the contract says what the design schedule left open

Lodestar SOW · Schedule 2, §3.3v1.0 · Sep 2027
subject to discovery

3.3 Change detection between MAVIS and the new platform: mechanism to be confirmed in discovery (options: log capture, timestamps, snapshot compare).

we left it open in the bid on purpose. good thing.

Sch. 5 Third-party licences required by the Solution are excluded from the Charges unless listed in Schedule 5.

£85k a year isn't in Schedule 5. it's in the change request.

In plain words: leaving the mechanism “subject to discovery” let the spike decide without a fight. A licence the design now needs isn't in the price, so it goes to the client openly as part of the discovery findings.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • One mechanism for every table

    Overkill for most, too slow for some. Fix → decide per table.

  • Trusting timestamps

    Some programs don't update them. Fix → test them on real tables.

  • Ignoring the mainframe team

    Their no stops everything. Fix → spike with their blessing.

  • Translating by hand

    One wrong copybook field corrupts money. Fix → generated definitions and a test per type.

  • Hiding the licence cost

    It appears at go-live. Fix → price it in the findings.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Integrating with a mainframe or any system without change events.
  • When freshness needs differ by table.
  • Before committing to a CDC tool.

Skip it when…

  • The old system already publishes reliable change events.

11Self-check quiz

What were the three change-capture options?

Database log capture, update timestamps, and nightly snapshot compare.

Why weren't timestamps enough?

18 % of tables had no reliable “last updated” column.

What was proposed?

Log capture for nine core tables, snapshots for the other 403.

What is a copybook?

A COBOL definition of a record's layout.

Why a test per field type?

Encodings and packed decimals are easy to get wrong, and money fields can't be.
