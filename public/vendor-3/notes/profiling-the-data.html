How Bad Is the Data, Really?

TL;DR

A sample tells you about the sample. Before you migrate, profile all the data you'll move, on four dimensions: validity (does it pass the rules?), uniqueness (duplicates), consistency (does it agree with other systems?) and completeness. Break every number down by segment (area, age, type), because averages hide where the problem lives. Then check how the sample was drawn.

1The situation The Grid, 19 November 2027

Assumption A1-07 said no more than 5 % of customer records would fail the address check. MetroVolt's sample in April showed 7.8 %. On 19 November Bilal's full profile of all 380,000 wave 1 accounts finishes running.

  • 31 % fail the address check: 117,800 records.
  • The 10,000-record sample was drawn from accounts opened after 2015. Young accounts, clean addresses.
  • A1-07 prices £9,500 for each 1 % above 5 %. Twenty-six points above.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Customer Operations

The data is Customer Ops' data. Priya's teams have lived with bad addresses for years (two in ten complaint calls), but nobody had a number. 31 % feels like an accusation, and it arrives in a cost-cutting year.

“We knew it was bad. We didn't know it was that bad.”

Wrenfield Bilal and Ines

For Wrenfield, 31 % is the biggest finding of discovery: it's the work, the risk and the money. It must be beyond dispute before anyone sees it, and it must explain why the sample said 7.8 %, without making MetroVolt look as if it cheated.

“Every record, every number by segment, and how the sample was drawn.”

Nobody chose the biased sample. Someone pulled the most convenient 10,000 records. That's what samples are, unless someone designs them.

3Real-life analogy the restaurant inspection

A restaurant inspector who only looks at the clean front counter will give it five stars. A thorough one opens every fridge, checks each against the same rules, and notes which fridge the problems are in. “The back fridge, everything older than a week” is far more useful than “30 % of food is out of date”.

4How it works all the data, four dimensions, every number by segment

Bilal profiles every wave 1 account against agreed rules, on four dimensions:

  • Validity: addresses checked against the national address file: 31 % fail.
  • Uniqueness: probable duplicate customers (same name, date of birth and nearby address): 4.2 %.
  • Consistency: meter numbers that don't match Gareth's meter register: 2.9 %.
  • Completeness: accounts with signs of vulnerability but no Priority Services flag: 0.6 %, small and the most important.

Then every number is broken down. By account age: opened before 2005, 48 % fail; after 2015, 6 %. By area: streets renamed since 2011 fail almost entirely (ADDRFIX's old table). The breakdown explains the sample: it was drawn from recent accounts, so it couldn't see the problem.

Bilal runs the profile twice, on two days, and Bert checks thirty failing records by hand. All thirty are really wrong. The number is beyond dispute.

  1. Profile everything you'll move

    Not a sample, if you can run it.

  2. Agree the rules first

    With the data owner, in writing.

  3. Four dimensions

    Validity, uniqueness, consistency, completeness.

  4. Break down every number

    By segment: age, area, type.

  5. Check how any sample was drawn

    It explains the gap.

  6. Verify by hand

    The expert checks a handful of failures.

Vocabulary

Data profiling
Measuring a dataset's content and quality systematically.
Validity
Whether values pass the rules (e.g. a real address).
Uniqueness
Whether each real thing appears once.
Consistency
Whether data agrees across systems.
Completeness
Whether required values are present.
Sampling bias
A sample that differs systematically from the whole.

31 %. 26 points. £247,000, by the contract's own formula.

3.2 If an Assumption proves materially incorrect, either Party may raise a Change Request under clause 7.

this is why we fought for “either party may raise” instead of “absorb”.

In plain words: a priced assumption turns a disaster into arithmetic. The contract already says what 31 % costs; the argument is about what to do, not who pays.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Trusting a sample

    It was drawn from the clean part. Fix → profile everything, or design the sample.

  • One average

    31 % hides where the problem lives. Fix → break down by segment.

  • Rules agreed afterwards

    The number gets argued away. Fix → agree rules first, in writing.

  • Blaming whoever drew the sample

    It was convenience, not deceit. Fix → explain it neutrally.

  • Validity only

    Duplicates and mismatches bite later. Fix → all four dimensions.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Before any data migration.
  • When an assumption depends on data quality.
  • When a sample looks better than people's experience.

Skip it when…

  • Tiny datasets you can inspect by eye.

11Self-check quiz

What are the four dimensions of the profile?

Validity, uniqueness, consistency and completeness.

Why did the sample show 7.8 % instead of 31 %?

It was drawn from accounts opened after 2015, which have cleaner addresses.

Why break numbers down by segment?

Averages hide where the problem lives; old accounts fail far more.

What does A1-07 say 31 % costs?

£9,500 for each 1 % above 5 %: 26 points, about £247,000.

Why did Bert hand-check thirty records?

To make sure the rule wasn't wrong and the number is beyond dispute.

what would you do?Priya's deputy says “the check is too strict; flats without numbers are fine”. What do you do?

Agree the rule together and re-run: if flats without numbers are acceptable to Customer Ops, the failure rate changes and so does the price. The rules belong to the data owner.

try it

Pick a dataset you rely on. Choose one rule for each of the four dimensions, run them on all of it, and break the worst number down by one segment. Where does the problem live?

remember!!

A sample tells you about the sample. Profile everything you'll move, and break it down.
