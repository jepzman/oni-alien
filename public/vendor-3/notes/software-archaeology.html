What Is Actually in There?

TL;DR

A legacy system's documents describe what someone intended, years ago. To learn what it does, dig in the running system: scheduler logs, execution records, the call graph and table access. Colour every program by how often it runs, find the parts that touch your problem, and only then ask the expert to explain them. Most old systems are smaller in use than on disk.

  • Family: inside MAVIS
  • Lives in: discovery/mavis-heat-map.xlsx

1The situation The Grid, October 2027

With read access to MAVIS since 16 August, discovery can start properly. MetroVolt's documents are a 2006 architecture diagram with nine boxes and a wiki last edited in 2014. The system itself: 4.1 million lines of COBOL, 1,214 programs, 386 batch jobs and 412 database tables.

  • Hana wants to start reading code at program one. At her speed that's about nine years.
  • Bert knows more than the documents, but he's one person, three days a week, retiring in 2029.
  • Discovery ends on 10 December. Ten weeks to understand thirty years.

MetroVolt Hollis

MetroVolt's own architects stopped mapping MAVIS years ago. Hollis knows the diagram is wrong and is a little embarrassed about it. He wants Wrenfield to find out what's there without making MetroVolt look as if it lost control of its own system.

“Tell me what's in there, kindly.”

Wrenfield Ines and Hana

Wrenfield needs to know which parts of MAVIS Lodestar must replace, which it can ignore, and where the surprises are, in ten weeks. Reading everything is impossible; trusting the diagram is dangerous.

“Let the running system tell us what matters.”

Every old system has more code than anyone uses. The job is to find the part that's alive.

3Real-life analogy the archaeological dig

An archaeologist arriving at an old town doesn't trust the town's old maps; they were drawn for other reasons. She marks out a grid and digs, recording what's found in each square and how deep. The layers in the ground say what people actually did there. The maps are useful afterwards, to explain what she found.

4How it works evidence from the running system, coloured by use

Hana and Lottie collect four kinds of evidence from the running system, with help from MetroVolt's mainframe team: twelve months of scheduler logs (which jobs ran, when, how long), execution records per program, a call graph built from the source (which program calls which), and table access statistics from the database.

Every program is coloured by use. 37 % never ran in twelve months. 41 % ran rarely (month-end, year-end, a regulatory report). 22 % run every night. Then they filter by the problem: 61 programs read or write customer addresses. That's where discovery starts.

Only then do they take the map to Bert, who corrects it in an afternoon: two “dead” programs run once a year for the regulator, and one “nightly” job is a copy nobody switched off in 2016.

  1. List everything from the system

    Programs, jobs, tables, files: from catalogues, not documents.

  2. Gather twelve months of runtime evidence

    Scheduler logs, execution records, table access.

  3. Build the call graph

    Which program calls which; which job runs which.

  4. Colour by use

    Never, rarely, nightly.

  5. Filter by your problem

    What touches customers, addresses, billing.

  6. Check the map with the expert

    Afterwards, with the evidence in front of you.

Vocabulary

Software archaeology
Learning how an old system works from its artefacts and behaviour.
Call graph
A map of which programs call which.
Dead code
Code that never runs; safe to leave behind, carefully.
Heat map
Parts of a system coloured by how much they're used.
Batch job
A program or chain of programs run on a schedule, often at night.
Runtime evidence
Logs and statistics from the live system.

discovery

“with evidence of use”. Hollis wrote that. good instinct.

D4 A discovery report setting out findings, their effect on the Assumptions, and options.

this map is the first page of D4.

In plain words: discovery is a deliverable with defined outputs. Writing “with evidence of use” into it means the inventory must come from the running system, not from old documents.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Reading from the top

    Years of reading for code that never runs. Fix → colour by use first.

  • Trusting the old diagram

    It shows intent, not reality. Fix → evidence from the running system.

  • Ignoring “rare” programs

    The yearly regulatory report breaks. Fix → ask the expert about every rare one.

  • Insulting the system

    The people who built it are in the room. Fix → numbers, not adjectives.

  • Mapping everything

    Discovery runs out of time. Fix → filter by your problem.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any legacy replacement or integration.
  • Documents are old or missing.
  • Time is short and the codebase large.

Skip it when…

  • The system is small and well documented, and the documents match the logs.

11Self-check quiz

Where does software archaeology get its evidence?

From the running system: scheduler logs, execution records, the call graph and table access.

What share of MAVIS programs never ran in twelve months?

37 %.

Why filter by the problem?

To spend limited time on the 61 programs that touch customer addresses.

Why check the map with Bert afterwards?

Runtime evidence misses things like yearly regulatory runs; the expert corrects it quickly with the evidence in front of him.

Why avoid calling the system a mess?

It ran the business for thirty years, and its builders are your colleagues now.

what would you do?Hollis asks for the full inventory of all 1,214 programs explained. What do you offer?

The full list with evidence of use, and explanations for the parts Lodestar touches. Explaining dead code costs time and changes no decision.

try it

Pick an old system you depend on. Find one source of runtime evidence (logs, schedules, usage stats). Colour its parts by use. What share hasn't run in a year?

remember!!
