How Do We Protect the Margin?

TL;DR

On a fixed price, margin pressure arrives late and pushes towards the cheapest cut: testing. Draw the line before the pressure: never cut testing, rehearsals, security or honesty. Then protect the margin with fair levers: bill agreed changes, plan roll-offs with the client, reuse what you've built, adjust timing rather than price. If you're still below the floor, say so to your own firm, in writing. That's a business fact, not a reason to make the night riskier.

1The situation Wrenfield, Leeds, 18 August 2028

Callum's monthly margin forecast, presented at Wrenfield's board in Leeds on Friday 18 August: the bid's P80 margin was 19 %; after CR-007 and the re-plan, 11 %; latest forecast, 9 %. Wrenfield's floor is 15 %.

  • Graham proposes two savings: drop rehearsal 3 (26 August) and halve the performance testing. About £60k.
  • Rehearsal 3 is the last full run before the night, and a go / no-go criterion.
  • Nobody at MetroVolt would know until 23 September.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt MetroVolt (who isn't in the room)

MetroVolt is paying a fixed price for a tested, rehearsed migration. Elaine has never asked about Wrenfield's margin, and shouldn't need to. What she'd care about is that the risk she's carrying on 23 September doesn't quietly go up to fix it.

“(not asked, and shouldn't have to be)”

Wrenfield Graham and Ines

Graham's worry is real: 9 % on Wrenfield's biggest contract is a bad year, and a 15 % floor exists for a reason. But the cut he proposes moves Wrenfield's financial risk onto 380,000 customers' accounts. Ines needs to protect the margin without touching the night.

“Protect the margin. Not by making the night more dangerous.”

The cheapest corner to cut is always the one that only shows up later. That's exactly why it has to be ruled out in advance.

3Real-life analogy the airline's fuel

An airline under cost pressure can sell more seats, renegotiate airport fees, fly older planes longer, schedule crews better. It doesn't carry less reserve fuel than the rules require. The fuel isn't where margin comes from; it's what lets the plane land when the weather turns.

4How it works the line first, then fair levers, then the truth to your own firm

The line, first. Ines writes it at the top of her response to Graham: “We won't cut testing, rehearsals, security, or anything we've told MetroVolt we're doing.” She points to Schedule 4: rehearsal 3 is a condition of wave 1 (clause 2.2); dropping it would be a contract breach as well as a risk.

Fair levers. Four: bill the agreed side-door work properly through the change pool (it had been under-invoiced: +£31k); roll two senior people off two weeks after cutover instead of six, agreed with Elaine as part of hypercare planning (+£38k); reuse wave 1's estimating model and tooling for the phase 2 bid instead of building new (saves bid cost later); and ask MetroVolt to pay M4 in two parts (cash flow, not margin). Forecast: 10 % (the two levers add about £69k on £5.7m of revenue).

The truth to your own firm. 10 % is still below the floor. Ines says so in writing, with why (CR-007's agreed price was £400k less than the work it covered, by choice, to keep MetroVolt's trust) and what it buys (a strong position for phase 2). Graham reads it twice and approves. Rehearsal 3 happens on 26 August.

  1. Write the line before the pressure

    What you will never cut.

  2. Check the contract

    Some cuts are breaches too.

  3. Bill what's agreed

    Change pool work, invoiced properly.

  4. Plan roll-offs with the client

    Fewer people after cutover, openly.

  5. Reuse what you've built

    For the next phase or bid.

  6. Tell your firm the truth

    Below the floor, why, and what it buys.

Vocabulary

Margin
Price minus cost, as a share of price.
Margin floor
The lowest margin a firm will accept.
Roll-off
Moving people off a project as their work ends.
Revenue leakage
Work done but not billed.
Quality cut
Saving cost by doing less testing or checking; risk moves to the client.
Forecast at completion
The expected final cost and margin, updated regularly.

no rehearsal 3, no wave 1. Graham hadn't read it.

Sch. 2, 5.3 Migrated customers' bills shall be calculated identically to MAVIS, except where MetroVolt instructs a change.

halving the performance test risks the 02:10 file. same problem.

In plain words: many cost cuts that look internal are actually commitments to the client. Check the contract before discussing them; it often settles the argument for you.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Cutting testing

    Risk moves to the client, unseen. Fix → a written line, before the pressure.

  • Hiding the margin problem

    Your firm finds out at year end. Fix → tell it, with why.

  • Leaking revenue

    Agreed work never invoiced. Fix → bill the pool properly.

  • Surprise roll-offs

    The client feels abandoned. Fix → plan them with the client.

  • Making it the client's problem

    Asking them to pay for your miss. Fix → fix it inside, or renegotiate openly.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Fixed-price work under cost pressure.
  • Late in delivery, when testing is the last big cost.
  • When someone proposes a cut the client wouldn't see.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip the line; skip the levers if the margin is healthy.

11Self-check quiz

What's on the line Ines wrote first?

Never cut testing, rehearsals, security, or anything MetroVolt has been told.

Why couldn't rehearsal 3 be dropped anyway?

Schedule 4 §2.2 makes it a condition of wave 1.

What were the four fair levers?

Bill change-pool work, plan earlier roll-offs, reuse tooling, adjust invoice timing.

What did the forecast become?

10 %, still below the 15 % floor.

Why tell Graham it was still below the floor?

It's a business fact; hiding it would surprise the firm later.

what would you do?Graham says: “Fine, keep rehearsal 3, but don't run the Welsh-language tests, nobody will notice.” What do you say?

No: the Welsh-language behaviour is part of Schedule 2 and the golden master; skipping it is the same kind of cut. Offer to look for another lever instead.

try it

Write your own line: three things you'd never cut on your current project, whatever the cost pressure. Then list three fair levers you could pull instead.

remember!!

Draw the line before the pressure. Margin comes from fair levers, never from the reserve fuel.
