What Happens When Steering Turns on Us?

TL;DR

When someone ambushes you at a steering committee, don't fight the slide in the room. Thank them, ask for the data behind the claim, promise a written answer in 48 hours, and ask the chair to defer any decision until then. Then answer with evidence, calmly, in writing. And change the rules so it can't happen again: every paper shared before every meeting, in both directions.

1The situation The Grid, room 27A, 28 June 2028

The pilot, Ashgrove, went live on Monday 5 June: 20,000 accounts, two billing cycles fine, balances reconciled. But 0.8 % of records were left in the exception queue; the criterion is 0.5 %. On 23 June MetroVolt rejected the milestone, correctly, naming the criterion.

  • At steering on Wednesday 28 June, Oliver Thane asks to “share a perspective”. His slide: “Lodestar: 9 weeks behind. Recommend Vexmoor-led recovery.”
  • Nobody at Wrenfield, and not Priya either, has seen it before.
  • Lord Varnish, attending for the first time since the kick-off: “I always said this would happen.”

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Priya

Priya is ambushed too: a slide about her programme, unseen, in front of Lord Varnish and Rupert's delegate. If Wrenfield argues in the room, she's chairing a fight between suppliers. If Wrenfield says nothing, the slide becomes the truth by default.

“Don't make me referee this today.”

Wrenfield Ines

Ines's instinct is to show the baseline on her laptop and prove the slide wrong on the spot. She might even succeed, and look exactly as defensive as Oliver hopes. The slide's power is surprise; time and evidence take it away.

“Don't win the room. Win Friday.”

Ambushes work by making you defend yourself badly in public. Refusing to be rushed is most of the defence.

3Real-life analogy the cross-examination

A witness under hostile cross-examination who's shown an unexpected document doesn't guess at it. A good one says: “I'd need to see where that figure comes from before I can comment.” The judge allows time. Later, with the source in hand, the witness explains calmly that the figure compared this year with the wrong year.

4How it works in the room: thank, ask, 48 hours; after: evidence; then: new rules

In the room, Ines says four sentences: “Thank you, Oliver. We take any view of the date seriously. Could we see the data behind the nine weeks? We'll answer in writing by Friday, and I'd ask that no decision is taken until then.” Priya agrees immediately; it gets her out of refereeing. Lord Varnish says nothing more.

After, Bea sends the spreadsheet that afternoon (to her credit, promptly). The nine weeks compare today's progress with baseline v1.0, from before CR-007. Against baseline v2.0, agreed in February, the slip is two weeks: the pilot's acceptance, rejected on 23 June for 0.8 % exceptions, being fixed for resubmission.

Friday, in writing: two pages to Priya, copied to the steering: the two baselines side by side, the pilot criterion and its fix, the wave 1 date unchanged with nine days of buffer left. No adjectives about Vexmoor. Priya forwards it to Rupert without comment.

New rules, proposed by Ines and made by Priya: every steering paper, from anyone, is shared two days before. Ines meets Oliver before every steering from then on. He shares his papers too.

  1. Stay calm and thank them

    The room is watching you, not the slide.

  2. Ask for the data

    One question, no counter-argument.

  3. Promise a written answer with a date

    48 hours is usually enough.

  4. Ask the chair to defer decisions

    It helps the chair too.

  5. Answer with evidence, in writing

    Side by side, no adjectives.

  6. Change the rules

    Papers shared before every meeting, both ways.

Vocabulary

Ambush
Surprising someone with criticism in a meeting they can't prepare for.
Pre-wiring
Sharing positions and papers before a meeting.
Deferral
Postponing a decision until information is available.
Baseline confusion
Comparing progress with an out-of-date plan.
Right of reply
A chance to answer a claim before decisions are made.
No-surprises rule
Agreeing nobody brings unseen papers to a meeting.

5What the contract says the governance rule that stops ambushes

Lodestar SOW · Schedule 7, §2.7 (as amended)v1.3 · 5 Jul 2028
no surprises

2.7 Papers for the Steering Committee, from any attendee, shall be circulated to all members at least two Working Days before the meeting. Papers not circulated may be noted but not decided upon.

Priya's rule, after 28 June.

2.6 A representative of Horizon Assurance may attend the Steering Committee and receive its papers, without a vote.

receive papers. now also share them.

In plain words: a governance rule that undecided papers can be noted but not decided on removes the power of a surprise slide, for everyone, including you.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Fighting in the room

    You look defensive; the slide wins. Fix → thank, ask, 48 hours.

  • Silence

    The slide becomes true. Fix → ask for the data and a deferral.

  • Attacking the ambusher

    You become the story. Fix → evidence only, no adjectives.

  • A verbal answer

    Forgotten by Monday. Fix → two pages, in writing.

  • Not changing the rules

    It happens again. Fix → papers shared before, both ways.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Someone surprises you with a damaging claim in a meeting.
  • A rival has influence over the client.
  • Governance lets unseen papers be decided on.

Skip it when…

  • The claim is right and you knew it: then say so in the room, and bring the plan.
