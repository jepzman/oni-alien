Is Anyone Reading the RAID Log?

TL;DR

A RAID log people don't read is a filing cabinet. Keep a short page of the items that matter now (a dozen), each with an owner, a next step and a date; move the rest to a backlog you review monthly. Read the page aloud for fifteen minutes every week, with the client. An item without an owner or a date isn't managed; it's decoration.

1The situation The Grid, 3 April 2028

Lodestar's RAID log has grown since the bid: 214 rows of risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies, maintained by Callum, read by nobody.

  • 61 rows have no owner. 88 haven't been updated since January.
  • Row 1, the top risk by its score, is still the 2027 laptop delay.
  • The real top risk (the rounding rule missing from the back-feed, found in rehearsal 1) is row 197.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Elaine

Elaine is copied on the RAID log every week and has stopped opening it. She wants to know what could hurt the pilot and who's doing what about it, in a minute, not in a spreadsheet.

“Tell me the five things that worry you, and who's on them.”

Wrenfield Callum

Callum keeps the log because the contract asks for one, and because deleting a row feels like hiding a risk. So it grows, and the important rows sink. He's protecting himself, and the log protects nobody.

“If I delete it and it happens, it's my fault.”

A long risk log feels safe for the person who writes it. A short one is safe for the project.

3Real-life analogy the pilot's checklist and the manual

A plane has a manual of thousands of pages and a checklist of a dozen lines, read aloud before take-off. Nobody reads the manual every flight. Everyone reads the checklist, every flight, and each line has someone who answers “checked”.

4How it works a short page, owners and dates, read weekly

On 3 April Ines and Callum go through all 214 rows in two hours. Twelve go on the page: the ones that could stop the pilot or wave 1 in the next three months. Each gets an owner (a person, not a team), a next step and a date. The rest go to a backlog, reviewed on the first Monday of each month. Forty-one rows are closed, each with a line saying what closed it (the laptops arrived in July 2027).

The page is read aloud every Monday at 09:45, fifteen minutes, standing, with Elaine. For each item, the owner says one sentence: done, next, still on date or not. New items come in only with an owner and a date.

Callum's fear is answered by the backlog: nothing is deleted, only sorted. In the first month the page changes eleven times. The rounding-rule risk (row 197) is item 1 on the page, and fixed by 28 April.

  1. Sort the whole log once

    Page, backlog or closed.

  2. Keep the page short

    A dozen items that matter in the next three months.

  3. Owner, next step, date

    For every item on the page.

  4. Read it aloud weekly

    Fifteen minutes, with the client.

  5. Review the backlog monthly

    Promote what's getting closer.

  6. Close with a reason

    What closed it, in one line.

Vocabulary

RAID log
A list of risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies.
Risk

Issue
Something that is happening now.
Risk owner
The one person responsible for watching and acting on a risk.
Next step
The very next action, specific enough to check.
Risk backlog
Items being watched but not actively managed this week.

5What the contract says what the contract asks for

Lodestar SOW · Schedule 7, §5.3v1.1 · Aug 2027
read weekly

5.3 The Supplier shall maintain a register of risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies and review it with MetroVolt's Programme Director at least monthly.

monthly is the minimum. weekly is how it gets read.

6.1 Each Party shall notify the other promptly of any matter which could increase the Charges, delay a Milestone or impair performance.

a risk at row 197 isn't “notified”.

In plain words: contracts ask for a risk register and a review. Meeting the clause with 214 rows nobody reads doesn't meet its purpose, or the early-warning clause next to it.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Everything on one list

    The important sinks. Fix → a page and a backlog.

  • Teams as owners

    Nobody acts. Fix → one person per item.

  • No dates

    Items sit forever. Fix → a next step with a date.

  • Emailed, not read

    Nobody opens it. Fix → read aloud, weekly.

  • Deleting to tidy

    History lost. Fix → close with a reason, keep the backlog.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any project with a risk register.
  • When nobody reads the log.
  • Before a key milestone.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip it; on a tiny project the page may have three items.

11Self-check quiz

What goes on the RAID page?

The dozen items that could hurt in the next three months, each with an owner, next step and date.

What happens to the rest?

They go to a backlog reviewed monthly; nothing is deleted.

Why read it aloud?

Owners commit in front of the client; nobody reads an emailed spreadsheet.

What's the rule for new items?

No owner or no date means it isn't on the page.

What was the real top risk in April?

The rounding rule R7 missing from the back-feed (row 197 of the old log).

what would you do?Hollis is the owner of three items and never has an update on Mondays. What do you do?

Talk to him privately: ask whether he's the right owner or needs a named person in his team, and agree one sentence he can give each week. Don't shame him in the review.

try it

Open your project's risk list. Pick the twelve items that matter in the next three months. Give each one person, a next step and a date. Read the page aloud to your team on Monday.

remember!!

A risk log is read, not kept. Twelve items, an owner and a date each, fifteen minutes a week.
