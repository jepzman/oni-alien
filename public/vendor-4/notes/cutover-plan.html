What Happens on the Night?

TL;DR

A cutover night is a performance, not an improvisation. Write a runbook where every step has an owner, a start time, a duration, a check and what to do if the check fails. Name the roles (lead, decision maker, communications, scribe), set checkpoints where you decide to go on or go back, and agree the point of no return in advance. Then rehearse the runbook itself until the timing is boring.

  • Family: the night
  • Lives in: migration/cutover-runbook-v3.md (312 steps)
  • Pairs with: migration in waves (vol. 3), go / no-go

1The situation The Grid, rehearsal 3, 26 August 2028

Wave 1's window: Saturday 23 September, 18:00 to Sunday 08:00. Fourteen hours to freeze MAVIS for 380,000 accounts, migrate them, reconcile, switch the router, and run the 02:10 job with both MAVIS's and Lodestar's feeds to Halvorsen.

  • Callum's first runbook, in June, was a two-page checklist: “migrate accounts”, “check”, “switch router”.
  • Rehearsal 2 in July ran over by 1 h 40, mostly waiting for people who didn't know it was their turn.
  • Nobody had decided who could say “roll back”, or until when.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Elaine and Customer Ops

For MetroVolt, the night decides whether 380,000 customers notice anything on Monday. Elaine wants to know when she'll be asked to decide, on what facts, and what happens if she says no. Customer Ops wants to know when their exception-queue staff start.

“When do I decide, and what am I deciding?”

Wrenfield Ines and Callum

Wrenfield needs fourteen hours of work by about forty people from four organisations to run in order, on time, without anyone improvising. Every minute lost waiting for someone eats into the time left for problems.

“Every step owned, every check written, every decision pre-agreed.”

The runbook isn't for when things go well. It's so that, when something goes wrong at 03:00, nobody has to think about anything except that one thing.

3Real-life analogy the theatre's prompt book

A stage manager's prompt book lists every cue: who moves, when, what light changes, what happens if an actor misses an entrance. During the show, she calls each cue in order. Nobody wonders whose turn it is. If the set jams, the book says what happens next, and who decides whether the show goes on.

4How it works every step owned, checkpoints, a point of no return, rehearsed

Every step. Runbook v3 has 312 steps. Each has an owner (a person, with a deputy), a planned start, a duration, a check (how we know it worked) and an if it fails line (retry, skip with approval, or escalate to the checkpoint).

Roles. Cutover lead: Ines, who calls each phase. Decision maker: Elaine, for MetroVolt. Communications lead: Callum, who updates the bridge channel every 30 minutes and Priya at each checkpoint. Scribe: Lottie, who logs every step's real time.

Checkpoints, each a go / roll-back decision by Elaine on agreed facts: 23:00 (migration half done, exceptions under 0.5 %), 02:10 (the batch runs with both feeds), 04:30 (reconciliation complete), 07:00 (go live).

Point of no return: 04:30. Before it, rolling back means flipping 380,000 flags back and unfreezing MAVIS (tested: 1 h 20). After it, MAVIS has run its Sunday processes without these accounts, and the plan is to fix forward.

Rehearse the runbook itself. Rehearsal 3 on 26 August runs every step with the real people, timed: 10 h 40 against an 11-hour target. Twenty-one steps are re-ordered afterwards.

  1. Write every step

    Owner, deputy, start, duration, check, if it fails.

  2. Name the roles

    Lead, decision maker, communications, scribe.

  3. Set checkpoints with facts

    What must be true to go on.

  4. Agree the point of no return

    Before it, roll back; after it, fix forward.

  5. Rehearse with the real people

    Timed, the whole runbook.

  6. Re-order after each rehearsal

    Remove waiting.

Vocabulary

Cutover
Switching from the old system to the new one.
Runbook
A step-by-step script for an operation, with owners and checks.
Checkpoint
A planned moment to decide whether to continue.
Point of no return
The moment after which rolling back is no longer the plan.
Fix forward
Solving problems in the new state rather than going back.
Bridge
A shared call or channel where everyone involved follows the night.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A short checklist

    People wait and improvise. Fix → every step owned and timed.

  • Unclear decision rights

    Arguments at 04:00. Fix → name the decision maker, in the contract.

  • No point of no return

    Rolling back too late. Fix → agree it in advance.

  • Untested rollback

    It doesn't work when needed. Fix → rehearse it.

  • Rehearsing the data, not the people

    Hand-offs fail. Fix → rehearse with the real people.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Any cutover or go-live with several teams.
  • When rolling back is expensive.
  • Night or weekend operations.

Skip it when…

  • A small, easily reversed change: a short checklist will do.

11Self-check quiz

What does each runbook step have?

An owner and deputy, a start time, a duration, a check and what to do if it fails.

What are the four roles?

Cutover lead, decision maker, communications lead and scribe.

When is the point of no return, and why then?

04:30: after it, MAVIS has run Sunday processes without the migrated accounts, so the plan is to fix forward.

Who decides at checkpoints?

Elaine, on the facts in the runbook, as the contract says.

How long did rehearsal 3 take?

10 hours 40 minutes against an 11-hour target.

what would you do?At rehearsal 3, step 212 is owned by a MetroVolt engineer who isn't working on 23 September. What do you do?

Name a deputy now and rehearse them on the step before the night; a step without a present owner is a step that won't happen.

try it

Pick an operation you'll run soon (a release, a migration, a move). Write the first ten steps with owner, start, duration, check and if-it-fails. Where's your point of no return?

remember!!

Make the night boring: every step owned, every check written, every decision agreed in advance.
