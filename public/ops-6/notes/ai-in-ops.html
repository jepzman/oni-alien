AI in Operations: Where It Helps, Where It Lies

TL;DR

AI assistants are useful in operations for drafts, summaries and explanations: incident timelines, review write-ups, long logs, unfamiliar configuration. They're also confidently wrong sometimes, and they don't know your system. So: an approved tool; every fact checked; the person who sends it owns it; say when AI drafted it. Never let it act on production or decide during an incident, and never paste customer data or secrets into it.

1The situation Larder, July 2028

Eli tries an approved AI assistant for operations work in June and July, under Lena's rules from Sanne's trial. Some uses are excellent: three review write-ups drafted in an hour each instead of three. Then, on Tuesday 25 July at 17:20, during a SEV3 about database connections, Yusuf, three weeks into the job, asks it for help. It answers confidently: restart the database primary to clear the connections. In Larder's setup that would trigger a failover inside the 17:00–20:00 window. Yusuf checks the runbook first, and the runbook says the opposite.

  • Fluent answers sound like knowledge of your system.
  • New people are the most likely to trust them under pressure.
  • Without rules, it's a matter of luck who checks.
meanwhile, in Sanne’s notebook

2Real-life analogy the very fast intern

Picture a new intern who is very fast and has read everything. Wonderful for first drafts, summaries and “where would I find…?”. But the intern has never been in this building, sometimes guesses with total confidence, and is never allowed to touch the boiler controls. Every draft is checked by someone who knows the building.

3How it solves it useful for drafts; never for production; every fact checked

From August Larder's operations rules sit on top of Lena's four rules (an approved tool, every fact checked, the sender owns it, say when AI drafted it):

  • Good uses: drafting an incident timeline from the chat (about 90 % right, and the scribe corrects the rest); drafting a review write-up (3 hours → 1); summarising a long log; explaining an unfamiliar configuration file; suggesting a runbook from incident notes.
  • Never: run or approve anything in production; decide during an incident (the incident lead decides); paste customer personal data or secrets into a prompt.
  • Suggestions about production are checked against the runbook and tested on staging or in a game day before they become a runbook step.
  • AI-drafted documents say so at the top, so readers know to check.

Yusuf's 25 July check becomes a story in the onboarding pack: the runbook beat the confident answer, and checking first was exactly right.

  1. Use an approved tool only

    With the data rules your legal team set.

  2. Use it for drafts and summaries

    Where a mistake is cheap and checked.

  3. Check every fact

    Against runbooks, logs and people who know.

  4. Never let it act on production

    Or decide in an incident.

  5. Keep customer data and secrets out

    Of every prompt.

  6. Label AI drafts

    So readers know to check.

Vocabulary for the exam

AI assistant
A language-model tool that drafts, summarises and answers questions.
Hallucination
A fluent, confident answer that is wrong.
Human in the loop
A person checks and decides before anything happens.
Approved tool
A tool legal and security have agreed for company data.
Grounding
Checking AI output against real sources (runbooks, logs).
Automation bias
Trusting a machine's answer more than you should.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Trusting fluency

    Confident isn't correct. Fix → check every fact against your sources.

  • Letting it act

    A wrong command in production. Fix → never on production.

  • Pasting customer data

    A privacy breach. Fix → approved tool, no personal data.

  • Unlabelled drafts

    Readers assume a person checked everything. Fix → label AI drafts.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Write-ups and summaries take hours.
  • People already use AI informally.
  • Onboarding new engineers to unfamiliar systems.
  • Searching large logs.

Skip it when…

  • Any decision or action on production during an incident.

8Self-check quiz

What are Lena's four rules?

Approved tool, every fact checked, the sender owns it, say when AI drafted it.

What may the assistant never do?

Act on or approve anything in production, decide during an incident, or receive customer data or secrets.

What happened on 25 July?

The assistant suggested restarting the primary, which would cause a failover in the peak window; Yusuf checked the runbook first.

What's it good for?

Timeline and review drafts, log summaries, explaining config, suggesting runbooks (then tested).

What is automation bias?

Trusting a machine's answer more than you should, especially under pressure.

what would you do?A colleague wants to give the assistant access to run read-only queries on production to speed up investigations. What do you ask?

Which data it could read (customer data?), whether the tool is approved for that, and who checks its conclusions. Read-only still exposes data; start with logs that have personal data masked, with Lena's agreement.

try it

List three operations tasks where a draft would save time and a mistake is cheap to catch. Write your “never” list. Try one draft task this week and note every fact you had to correct.

remember!!

Use AI for drafts, never decisions. Check every fact, keep customer data out, and never let it touch production.
