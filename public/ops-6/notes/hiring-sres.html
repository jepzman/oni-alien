Hiring the First SREs

TL;DR

Hire operations engineers for what the job really is: staying calm in an incident, reading unfamiliar systems, writing code that removes handwork, and explaining clearly. A long list of tools filters out good people and tests nothing. Write a one-page role card, use a work sample that looks like the job (a real incident to talk through, a broken service to investigate), and write the scoring sheet before the first interview.

1The situation Larder, April 2028

Eli's first draft of the job advert lists twenty-two technologies, asks for “24/7 availability”, uses the word “rockstar”, and plans three whiteboard rounds about algorithms. Ana reads it and asks: would you have got this job in 2025? Would Bram? Nothing in it tests what the job really is: staying calm in an incident, reading an unfamiliar system, automating handwork, and explaining things to a store lead at 07:00.

  • Tool lists filter out people who learn fast.
  • “24/7” scares off exactly the people who'd make on-call sustainable.
  • Whiteboard puzzles measure interview skill, not incident skill.
meanwhile, in Sanne’s notebook
  • Who Do We Hire? (Sanne, vol. 6): Sanne's hiring of Nadia (Nov–Dec 2027): a role card, 34 applicants, work samples from Antwerp's real status and issue log, a scoring sheet written first.

2Real-life analogy hiring a chef

A restaurant can ask a cook to list every dish they know, or it can ask them to cook a short service: two hours in the real kitchen, a busy moment, a missing ingredient, a dish sent back. The second shows calm, skill and taste. The first only shows memory.

3How it solves it a role card, a realistic work sample, a scoring sheet first

Eli rewrites the process in May, borrowing the shape of Sanne's hiring:

  • A one-page role card: the job (run tier 1 with others, automate toil, lead incidents, teach the product teams), what matters (calm, curiosity, writing code to remove handwork, explaining clearly), on-call one week in four, paid, with rest, and what doesn't matter: any particular tool.
  • A work sample that looks like the job: talk through the timeline of a real past incident (the 29 October 2026 network rule, names removed), then investigate a broken staging service for 90 minutes with Bram, thinking aloud.
  • A scoring sheet written before the first interview, with Eli, Bram and Ana as the panel.

61 applicants, 12 screening calls, 5 work samples. Two are hired. Lotte (she/her), from a logistics company's platform team, found the broken service's cause in 40 minutes and explained it as if to a store lead. She starts on Monday 3 July. Yusuf (he/him), from a bank's operations team, asked the best questions about the incident timeline, and starts on Tuesday 1 August.

  1. Write what the job really is

    Day to day, and at 3 a.m.

  2. Name what matters, and what doesn't

    Qualities over tools.

  3. Be honest about on-call

    Frequency, pay, rest.

  4. Design a work sample like the job

    A real incident; a broken service.

  5. Write the scoring sheet first

    Before meeting anyone.

  6. Panel of people they'll work with

    Including someone outside the team.

Vocabulary for the exam

Role card
A one-page description of a job, its purpose and what matters.
Work sample
A task from the real job, done as part of the interview.
Scoring sheet
Criteria and scales, agreed before interviews, to judge fairly.
Structured interview
The same questions and criteria for every candidate.
Culture add
Hiring people who bring something the team lacks, not more of the same.
Onboarding
Helping a new person become effective (next notes).

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Tool lists

    Good learners filtered out. Fix → qualities over tools.

  • Hiding on-call

    New hires leave after their first rota. Fix → be honest: frequency, pay, rest.

  • Puzzles instead of the job

    You hire interview experts. Fix → a work sample like the job.

  • Scoring after the interview

    Gut feeling wins. Fix → the sheet comes first.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • You're hiring for operations or SRE.
  • Job adverts list tools, not the job.
  • Interviews don't predict who does well.
  • A new team is forming.

Skip it when…

  • Short contracts for a narrow, well-defined task.

8Self-check quiz

What was wrong with the first advert?

22 technologies, “24/7”, “rockstar” and whiteboard puzzles: nothing about what the job really is.

What's in the SRE role card?

The job, what matters (calm, curiosity, automation, explaining), honest on-call terms, and what isn't required.

What was the work sample?

Talking through a real past incident's timeline, and investigating a broken staging service for 90 minutes with Bram.

When was the scoring sheet written?

Before the first interview (9 May).

Who was hired, and when do they start?

Lotte (she/her) on 3 July and Yusuf (he/him) on 1 August.

what would you do?A candidate knows every tool on your stack but blames others when talking through the incident timeline. Another knows half the tools but asks what made each decision make sense. Who scores higher?

The second, on the criteria that matter: curiosity and blameless thinking. Tools can be learned in weeks; how someone thinks about incidents is much harder to teach.

try it

Write a one-page role card for an operations role: what the job is, what matters, the honest on-call terms, and what isn't required. Then design a 90-minute work sample from a real incident of yours.

remember!!

Hire for calm, curiosity and clear explanation, and test it with a sample that looks like the job.
