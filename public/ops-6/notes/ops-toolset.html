Which Tools Do We Really Need?

TL;DR

Operations tools multiply: every team buys the one it needs, and nobody switches anything off. Keep one tool per job, each with a one-sentence job, an owner, a cost and its users. Choose between overlaps with the people who use them, consolidate, and archive the rest. Fewer tools means fewer places to look in an incident, fewer logins, less cost, and one way of doing things.

1The situation Larder, July 2028

Eli counts the operations tools in July: 17. There are two paging tools (one from 2026, and one a team bought in 2027), three tools for metrics and logs, two status pages, tickets in both the helpdesk and the engineering tracker, a wiki nobody updates, and two uptime checkers. Together they cost €11,200 a month. During an incident, on-call has to look in four places.

  • Each tool made sense to someone when it was bought.
  • Overlaps mean two truths, and two places to forget.
  • New people learn 17 logins before their first page.

2Real-life analogy the kitchen drawer

A kitchen drawer fills up with three tin openers, two broken peelers and a gadget nobody remembers buying. A good cook keeps one of each tool she uses, sharp, in the same place, and gives the rest away. In a hurry she finds what she needs without thinking.

3How it solves it one tool per job, each with a sentence, an owner and a cost

In July and August, Eli and the reliability team give each tool a one-sentence job, an owner, a cost and a user count. Where two tools do the same job, the people who use them run a short trial and choose. The result:

  • Paging: one tool.
  • Metrics, logs and traces: one platform, plus the separate security log store.
  • Status page: one, for customers and business clients.
  • Tickets: the helpdesk for requests and incidents from people, the tracker for engineering work, linked to each other.
  • Knowledge: the repository; the old wiki is archived.
  • Uptime: the outside checks inside the metrics platform.

17 tools become 9. The cost falls from €11,200 to €6,900 a month. On-call looks in two places. Retiring the eight uses the decommissioning checklist. The rule from now on: a new tool needs a job no current tool does.

Vocabulary for the exam

Toolset
The set of tools a team uses to do its work.
Tool sprawl
Many overlapping tools, bought separately.
Consolidation
Reducing overlapping tools to one per job.
Total cost
Licences plus the time to learn, maintain and switch between tools.
Tool owner
The person who keeps a tool configured, current and paid for.
Context switching
Moving between tools, costly during incidents.

4The maturity ladder where are we, and what is the next rung?

a drawer of tools

  • Overlaps, logins, costs.
  • Four places to look.

2Written down Larder now

  • Consolidated, archived.
  • A door policy.

  • Tools linked: alert → incident → ticket → review.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Choosing by managers' preference

    The people using it hate it. Fix → trial with users.

  • Keeping both just in case

    Sprawl returns. Fix → retire properly.

  • Ignoring time costs

    A free tool that costs hours. Fix → count learning and switching.

  • No door policy

    Back to 17 in two years. Fix → a new tool needs a new job.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Several tools do the same job.
  • On-call looks in many places during incidents.
  • Tool costs are unknown or growing.
  • A new team is forming and needs a standard set.

Skip it when…

  • When each tool truly has a separate job and everyone knows it.

8Self-check quiz

What does every tool need?

A one-sentence job, an owner, a cost and its users.

How were overlaps resolved?

By a short trial with the people who use them.

What was the result?

17 tools → 9; €11,200 → €6,900 a month; two places to look in an incident.

What's the rule for new tools?

A new tool needs a job no current tool does.

Why count time, not just licences?

Learning, maintaining and switching between tools costs hours, especially in incidents.

what would you do?Dee's team wants a new error-tracking tool for the apps. The observability platform has a basic version. What happens?

Ask what job the new tool does that the platform can't. If it's real (mobile crash grouping, say), it gets a sentence, an owner and a cost, and joins the list; if not, improve how the platform is used.

try it

List every tool your team uses for operations, with its cost. Write each one's job in a sentence. Where do two tools share a sentence? Which one would your on-call person keep?

remember!!

One tool per job, each with an owner and a cost, and a door policy: a new tool needs a new job.
