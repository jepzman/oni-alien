Is This Service Ready for Production?

TL;DR

Before a team takes on a service, walk through it together with a checklist: SLOs set and met, alerts with tested runbooks, dashboards, capacity, tested failover and restore, mapped dependencies, rollbacks, known errors, training. “It's been in production for years” isn't readiness. Turn every gap into an action with a date, and don't take the pager until they're closed. Reuse the checklist for every service after.

1The situation Larder, August 2028

The reliability team is to take Ordering's pager on 1 October. Ben's team says Ordering is ready: “it's been in production for four years”. Lotte, five weeks into the job, asks simple questions. Which alerts page, and does each have a runbook? What's the restore time? What breaks if the address lookup vendor fails? Some of the answers are “Ben knows”.

  • Age in production isn't readiness; it often hides tacit knowledge.
  • A team taking over inherits every gap at 3 a.m.
  • Without a checklist, “ready” is an argument.

2Real-life analogy the building handover

When a builder hands a building to the facilities team, they walk every floor with a checklist before the keys change hands: fire alarms tested, plans of the pipes and wires, manuals for the lift, spare parts, a list of known snags with dates. “It's been standing for years” isn't on the list.

3How it solves it 31 checks, walked through together, gaps closed before the pager moves

On Monday 28 August, Bram and Lotte hold the readiness review with Ben's team. It covers 31 checks in seven areas:

  • SLOs set and met for three months.
  • Alerts symptom-based, each with a tested runbook.
  • Dashboards per the standard.
  • Capacity headroom and the last load test.
  • Failover and restore tested within a month.
  • Dependencies mapped, with vendor plans.
  • Rollback for every kind of deploy, known errors written down, on-call training done.

The result is 25 met, 6 gaps: two alerts without runbooks, the address lookup vendor's failure never tested, one dashboard missing, a slow-query fix that lived only in Ben's head, and a restore test older than a month. All six are fixed by Monday 11 September. The pager doesn't move until they are. The checklist becomes the template for Dispatch in 2029, and for any team that wants a review of its own service.

  1. Write a checklist of what “ready” means

    SLOs, alerts, runbooks, capacity, recovery, dependencies.

  2. Walk through it together

    Both teams, the real service.

  3. Answer with evidence, not memory

    “Ben knows” is a gap.

  4. Turn gaps into dated actions

    Owned by the building team.

  5. Don't move the pager until they're closed

    Or agree the exceptions in writing.

  6. Reuse the checklist

    For every service after.

Vocabulary for the exam

Production readiness review (PRR)
A structured check that a service can be run reliably by its future operators.
Acceptance criteria
Conditions a service must meet before it's taken over.
Tacit knowledge
What someone knows but hasn't written down.
Gap
A check not met, with an owner and a date.
Warranty period
A time after handover when the building team still helps (next notes).
Template
The reusable checklist for the next service.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Age as readiness

    Old services hide the most tacit knowledge. Fix → walk the checklist.

  • Answers from memory

    “Ben knows” at 3 a.m. is no answer. Fix → evidence for every check.

  • Taking the pager with open gaps

    The new team inherits them. Fix → close them first, or agree in writing.

  • A checklist used once

    Each review starts from scratch. Fix → reuse and improve it.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • A team is taking over a service.
  • A service goes live for the first time.
  • Before a major launch.
  • When a team asks “are we ready?”

Skip it when…

  • Tiny internal tools with no on-call.

8Self-check quiz

What does a readiness review check?

SLOs met, alerts with tested runbooks, dashboards, capacity, failover and restore, dependencies, rollbacks, known errors, training.

Why isn't “four years in production” readiness?

Old services often hide tacit knowledge and untested failures.

What did Ordering's review find?

25 of 31 met; 6 gaps, including a fix only in Ben's head and an untested vendor failure.

When does the pager move?

Only after the gaps are closed (by 11 September), then game days, then 1 October.

What happens to the checklist?

It's reused for Dispatch in 2029 and any team that wants it.

what would you do?Two days before the pager handover, one runbook is still missing for a rare alert. Ben says “it never fires”. What do you do?

Either Ben writes it now (an hour), or both sides agree in writing that it's an accepted gap with a date and Ben's team stays first line for that alert until it's done. “Never fires” is what people say about the alert that fires on the first weekend.

try it

Pick a service someone else might one day run. Walk through it with the seven areas and count the checks you could answer with evidence, not memory. What's in one person's head?

remember!!

Ready is a checklist walked together, not years in production. Close the gaps before the pager moves.
