Where Are We Going?

TL;DR

An operations strategy is one page: a purpose tied to the business plan, three goals for the next year or two, a few principles that guide everyday choices, and a quarterly roadmap. It turns a pile of registers and requests into a direction, so decisions are checked against the page instead of fought one at a time. Get it signed by leadership and review it every quarter.

1The situation Larder, July 2028

The board's plan of 20 March 2028 says eight cities and 60,000 orders a day by the end of 2028, and more after. Operations has an improvement register, a risk register, a maturity check and a new team. What it doesn't have is a page saying where operations is going, and why. So every request (another tool, another city, another team wanting help with on-call) arrives as a separate negotiation.

  • Lists of work aren't a direction.
  • Leadership can't support what it hasn't seen on a page.
  • The team can't say no without a reason everyone shares.

2Real-life analogy the voyage plan

A sailing club planning a long voyage writes one page: the destination, the three things that must be true to get there (a sound boat, a trained crew, a safe route), and the legs of the journey with dates. Every decision on board, from what to stow to which harbour to skip, is checked against that page.

3How it solves it purpose, three goals, principles, a quarterly roadmap, signed

Eli writes the operations strategy on one page in July, and Femke signs it on Monday 24 July:

  • Purpose: Larder keeps its promises at 60,000 orders a day, without heroics.
  • Three goals for 18 months: tier 1 meets its SLOs at twice today's volume; a new city's IT in 5 days, self-service; no single person critical to operations.
  • Principles: build it and run it, with help; automate the whole job; measure what users feel; practise failure.
  • Roadmap: Q3 2028, the reliability team and Ordering's handover; Q4, Dispatch's readiness review and capacity forecasting; Q1 2029, Dispatch's handover and a payments review; Q2 2029, a decision on a second region against ADR 0026's triggers.

It's reviewed every quarter with the maturity check and the risk register. When Dee's team asks the reliability team to run the apps' on-call, the answer comes from the page: not tier 1, so build it and run it, with help. And the help comes the next week.

  1. Start from the business plan

    What must operations make possible?

  2. Write a purpose in one sentence

    In business words.

  3. Choose three goals

    Measurable, 12–18 months.

  4. Write a few principles

    For everyday choices.

  5. Lay out a quarterly roadmap

    Tied to the goals and the top risks.

  6. Get it signed and review quarterly

    And use it to answer requests.

Vocabulary for the exam

Strategy
Where you're going, why, and how you'll choose what to do.
Purpose
Why the team exists, in the business's words.
Goal
A measurable result to reach in a set time.
Principle
A rule of thumb for everyday choices.
Roadmap
The order of big pieces of work over time.
Charter
A team's agreed purpose and scope, signed by leadership.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A long document

    Nobody reads it. Fix → one page.

  • Goals in IT words

    Leadership can't connect. Fix → business words and numbers.

  • Unsigned

    Ignored at the first conflict. Fix → signed by leadership.

  • Never used

    Requests still fought one by one. Fix → check every request against the page.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Operations has lots of work and no stated direction.
  • A business plan changes the scale.
  • A new team is forming.
  • Requests keep turning into negotiations.

Skip it when…

  • A tiny team with one obvious job.
