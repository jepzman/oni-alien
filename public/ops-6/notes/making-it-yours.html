Making It Yours

TL;DR

This notebook is ninety practices, and nobody needs all of them at once. Start where you are: find the rung you're on for the few practices that hurt most, and climb one rung at a time. Eli's order, if he started again: know what you run, share the pager safely, handle incidents together, change safely, promise what users feel, practise the worst, and build the team that doesn't need you. The goal was never to be the one who keeps it running.

1The situation Larder, Monday 30 October 2028

Two and a half years after his first page, Eli reads his notebook from the start. In March 2026: one person on call all year, 410 pages a month with 9 that mattered, nothing written down, the domain renewing on an expired card, and Eli at 160 %. In October 2028: a reliability team of four, product teams running their own services, 12 night pages a month across Larder, every major incident reviewed within a week, restores in 22 minutes, and the first incident on the most critical service handled without him. The job was never to be the one who keeps it running.

  • Ninety practices is too many to start with.
  • Every organisation starts on a different rung.
  • The order matters more than the completeness.
meanwhile, in Sanne’s notebook
  • What Was It All For? (Sanne, vol. 6): Sanne's closing note, 25 September 2028: the six principles revisited, eight cities, and one line for her first day: “the plan is the team's, the truth is yours…”.

2Real-life analogy the gardener's overgrown plot

A gardener who took on one overgrown plot didn't plant everything at once. First she cleared one bed, then laid paths, then put in a water butt, then planted, season by season. Now there's a garden others tend with her, and her notebook says what she'd do first if she started again: the path to the shed, so everyone else could find the tools.

3How it solves it seven steps, in order, one rung at a time

Eli writes the order he'd follow if he started again, one rung at a time:

  1. Know what you run: catalogue, dependencies, assets, owners (vol. 1).
  2. Share the pager safely: a rota, alerts worth waking for, runbooks, one front door for problems (vol. 1).
  3. Handle incidents together and learn without blame: severity, roles, communication, reviews, problems (vol. 2).
  4. Change safely: kinds of change, windows, risk, a way back, keeping current (vol. 3).
  5. Promise and measure what users feel: SLIs, SLOs, error budgets, an SLA you can keep (vol. 4).
  6. Practise the worst: restores, paper fallback, game days, security, vendors (vol. 5).
  7. Build the team that doesn't need you: who runs what, hiring, humane on-call, handover, growth (vol. 6).

The numbers that moved: useful pages from 2 % to about 80 %; change failures from 6 % to 3 %; a full restore from 3 h 40 to 22 minutes; toil from 41 % to 19 %; and Eli's own night pages in October 2028: none. Most of them came from small rungs, climbed one at a time, by more and more people.

  1. Pick the three practices that hurt most

    Not all ninety.

  2. Find your rung on each

    Honestly, with evidence.

  3. Climb one rung

    The next one, not the top.

  4. Write it down as you go

    Your own notebook.

  5. Bring others onto the rota and the work

    Early.

  6. Look back every six months

    What moved, what's next.

Vocabulary for the exam

Start where you are
Build on what exists; don't start from scratch.
Rung
One step on a practice's ladder, from by hand to automatic.
Iterative improvement
Small steps, each checked, rather than a big redesign.
Heroics
Reliability that depends on individuals' extra effort.
Sustainable operations
Reliability that lasts without heroics.
Your notebook
Your own record of what you did and why.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Trying to do everything at once

    Nothing finishes; people burn out. Fix → three practices, one rung each.

  • Copying someone else's top rung

    Automation before anything is written down. Fix → start where you are.

  • Doing it alone

    A new kind of hero. Fix → bring others in early.

  • Not writing it down

    The next person starts from scratch. Fix → keep your own notebook.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • You've inherited “everything technical”.
  • You're the person who keeps it running.
  • You've read this notebook and wonder where to start.
  • Every six months, to look back.

Skip it when…

  • Never.

8Self-check quiz

What's Eli's first step if he started again?

Know what you run and who owns it.

What's the last step?

Build the team that doesn't need you.

Why one rung at a time?

Small steps finish and stick; doing everything at once finishes nothing.

Which numbers moved most?

Useful pages 2 % → ≈ 80 %, restore 3 h 40 → 22 min, toil 41 % → 19 %, Eli's night pages to none.

What was the job, in the end?

Not to be the one who keeps it running, but to build something that keeps running without one person.

what would you do?A friend has just become the only operations person at a 40-person company. They ask where to start. What do you say?

Write down what you run and who owns it this week; then get at least three more people onto a rota with alerts worth waking for and a runbook for each. Everything else can wait for the next rung.

try it

Write your own first page: what your customers buy, what you keep running, and what a bad hour costs. Then pick the three practices from this notebook that hurt most today, find your rung on each, and climb one.

remember!!

Start where you are, one rung at a time, and remember the goal: a service that keeps running without heroes, including you.
