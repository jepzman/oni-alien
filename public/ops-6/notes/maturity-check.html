Where Are We Weak?

TL;DR

A maturity check asks, practice by practice, how well each one is done today. Score each on a simple ladder (by hand → written → scripted → automatic), together with the people who depend on operations, and with evidence, not from one person's view. Then choose three weak spots to work on, not twenty, and check again in six months. It turns “how are we doing?” into a short, honest list.

1The situation Larder, June 2028

Before the board in June, Femke asks Eli for “the state of operations”. He could list everything built since 2026, and it would sound good. But he doesn't know what's still weak, and his own view is the least objective in the building. Every practice in this notebook ends with a ladder from “by hand” to “automatic”, and nobody has ever looked at all the ladders together.

  • Lists of achievements hide the gaps.
  • The builder's view of their own work is always kind.
  • Without a picture, priorities are whatever's loudest.

2Real-life analogy the team's end-of-season review

At the end of a season a football team rates each part of its game (defending set pieces, fitness, passing under pressure) together, coaches and players, against what a good team does. Then the coach picks three things for pre-season, not twenty. The honest 2 out of 4 on set pieces is worth more than any highlight reel.

3How it solves it every practice on its ladder, together, with evidence; pick three

On Monday 19 June, Eli runs a half-day maturity check with the reliability team, Ben, Cho, Dee, Sam, Joost's operations lead and Mo. Every practice in the notebook is scored on its own ladder, from 1 (by hand) to 4 (automatic), together and with evidence: a link, a number, or a recent incident.

  • Strongest: incidents and reviews (3), change (3), on-call (3), monitoring (3).
  • Weakest: capacity forecasting (2), knowledge (2), store self-service (2), vendor management (2), cost (2). The people risk is named again: two people still hold too much.

Three are chosen for the next six months: knowledge (write up the last six undocumented systems), capacity forecasting from Ana's growth plans, and the reliability team itself. Femke gets a one-page picture: the strong, the weak, the three, and the date of the next check in December.

  2. Use a simple ladder for each

    By hand, written, scripted, automatic.

  3. Score together, with evidence

    People who depend on operations included.

  4. Name the people risks too

    Not just processes.

  5. Choose three

    Not twenty.

  6. Re-check in six months

    Same people, same ladders.

Vocabulary for the exam

How well and how consistently a practice is done.

Levels from ad hoc to automatic, with what each looks like.
Assessment

Evidence
A link, number or recent example showing the level.
Baseline
The first score, to compare later ones with.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Scoring alone

    Kind to your own work. Fix → together, with those who depend on it.

  • No evidence

    Scores are opinions. Fix → a link, number or example for each.

  • Twenty focus areas

    Nothing improves. Fix → three.

  • Chasing level 4 everywhere

    Automation where it isn't worth it. Fix → the level a practice needs, not the top.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Someone asks “how are we doing?”
  • Planning the next half-year.
  • After a period of building.
  • Before a board or a big change.

Skip it when…

  • In the middle of a crisis: fix it first.

8Self-check quiz

How was each practice scored?

On its own ladder, 1 (by hand) to 4 (automatic), together, with evidence.

Who took part?

The reliability team with Ben, Cho, Dee, Sam, Joost's operations lead and Mo.

What were the weakest practices?

Capacity forecasting, knowledge, store self-service, vendor management and cost (all 2), plus the people risk.

Which three were chosen?

Knowledge, capacity forecasting, and the reliability team itself.

Should every practice aim for 4?

No: each needs the level that's worth it.
