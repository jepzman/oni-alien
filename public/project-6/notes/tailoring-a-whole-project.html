a.k.a. “tailoring a whole project”: choosing and sizing the whole method for one project

How Light Can We Go?

TL;DR

Vol. 1 tailored one process at a time. Tailoring a whole project means setting the method for all of it, on one page, before it starts: what to keep in full (the parts where this project can go wrong), what to shorten, what to drop, which meetings to hold, and a date to check the fit. Set it from a few dials (novelty, size, regulation, the team's experience, the cost of a slip), not from the last project's habits. The fourth time you do something, most of the first time's process is waste; the checks where it went wrong are not.

1The situation Larder, Monday 27 September 2027

Eindhoven must open on Monday 22 November, eight weeks away. Antwerp, the launch that slipped, is still three weeks from its own opening, and Sanne runs both. Rotterdam's project file had 58 pages and the launch had four standing meetings. Copy that, and Sanne spends Eindhoven's eight weeks writing documents. But Eindhoven is the fourth Dutch city. Most of what Rotterdam had to invent (the workstreams, the gates, the money rules, the people sums) now exists in the playbook.

  • The first project's process is the default, because it's there.
  • Dropping things feels like cutting corners.
  • Nobody has said which checks still matter.
meanwhile, in Ana’s notebook
  • Just-Enough Up-Front Design (Ana, vol. 6): Ana's just-enough up-front design: decide early only what is hard to change later, and leave the rest.

2Real-life analogy the fourth time a recipe is cooked

The first time a cook makes a family recipe she weighs everything, reads every line, sets three timers. By the fourth time she weighs only the flour and the salt, the two things that ruin the dish if they're wrong, and keeps one timer, for the oven. Same dish, a quarter of the effort. She didn't get careless. She learned where it goes wrong.

3How it solves it one page: keep, shorten, drop, meetings, check the fit

Sanne writes Eindhoven's tailoring sheet on one page. Keep in full: the five gates, the risk register with a Larder owner on every row (Antwerp's lesson), the store IT line from week one (Rotterdam's), the go / no-go. Shorten: the charter to half a page, the plan to the playbook's timeline with Eindhoven's dates filled in, status to five lines, the budget to one table. Drop: the communications plan (one language, people who know each other), the separate quality review, earned value, the Monday decision log (Ana's log is enough). Meetings: one standing meeting, Thursday 09:30 for thirty minutes; the steering group gets a written note every other Friday instead of a meeting. Check the fit at week four, Monday 25 October: Joost asks to put one thing back, a ten-minute call on the Friday before go / no-go, and nothing else is missed. The file ends at 20 pages; Eindhoven opens on 22 November, eight weeks from the sheet.

  1. Set the dials first

    Novelty, size, regulation, team experience, cost of a slip.

  2. Keep in full what has gone wrong before

    The checks that caught real problems.

  3. Shorten what still helps

    Half a page is still a charter.

  4. Drop what was there to learn

    Say why, in one line each.

  5. Choose the fewest meetings that work

    Written notes where talk adds nothing.

  6. Check the fit on a date

    Put back only what was missed.

＋Going deeper the dials, what can't be dropped, tailoring down and up, and checking the fit

Vol. 1 introduced tailoring as four steps applied to processes one at a time. After six launches Sanne tailors a whole project in an afternoon. This chapter shows the dials she sets first, the few things that never get tailored away, why tailoring sometimes means adding process, and how to check the fit without arguing about it.

1Set the dials before choosing anything

Process should follow the project, so describe the project first. Sanne uses five dials, each from light to heavy:

  • Novelty: done this before, here? Eindhoven light; Antwerp heavy.
  • Size and money: how much is at stake?
  • Regulation: permits, inspections, contracts with teeth? Antwerp's permit made it heavy.
  • Team experience: has this team done it together?
  • Cost of a slip: what a week late costs (≈ €17k for a city, since playbook v3).

Each dial pushes a different part: novelty and regulation push risk and stakeholder work, size pushes money control, team experience pushes planning detail, cost of a slip pushes schedule control.

2What never gets tailored away

Some things are cheap and catch disasters, so they stay however light the project: a sponsor who decides; a written “done means”; a risk list with owners inside the organisation; a go / no-go before anything irreversible; a status the sponsor can trust. Everything else is a choice. Eindhoven's twenty pages included all five.

3Tailoring can mean adding

Tailoring isn't a diet. Antwerp, tailored honestly, needed more than Rotterdam: a stakeholder register of thirty-one, a communications plan in three languages, a simulation. The mistake there was tailoring by habit (Rotterdam's light touch on permits), not tailoring too much. Turn every dial, and let some of them say “heavier”.

4Checking the fit without an argument

Set the review date when you write the sheet, and ask two questions only: what did we miss because we dropped it, and what did we keep that nobody used? Put back only what was actually missed. At Eindhoven's week-four check Joost missed one thing, a short call before go / no-go, and nobody missed the communications plan. Record both answers in the playbook, so the next sheet starts from evidence.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

  • No dials.

Standard

Eindhoven

  • One page from five dials.
  • Keep, shorten, drop, meetings.
  • Fit checked at week four.

Heavy

regulated or first-of-a-kind

  • A tailoring record with reasons.
  • Approved by governance.
  • Reviewed at every gate.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Copying the first project

    Its learning costs, again. Fix → set the dials for this one.

  • Cutting the cheap safety checks

    Small saving, big risk. Fix → keep the five that never go.

  • Tailoring only down

    Some projects need more. Fix → let dials say heavier.

  • No fit check

    Gaps found at go / no-go. Fix → set the date when you write the sheet.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • At the start of every project.
  • When a project is a repeat of earlier ones.
  • When a project is new in a way the last wasn't.
  • At each gate, briefly.

Skip it when…

  • Never; on tiny work it's three lines.
