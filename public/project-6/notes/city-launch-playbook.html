a.k.a. “the city launch playbook”: turning repeated projects into a method

Can a Launch Be a Recipe?

TL;DR

When a kind of project repeats, write down how it's done in a playbook: what is fixed in every instance (the parts that make it work, and the checks where it went wrong), what is decided locally, the templates, and the timeline. Version it: after each project, turn lessons that held in more than one place into changes, and remove what nobody used. A playbook is only real when someone who didn't write it can run a launch from it.

  • Lives in: one versioned document plus a kit of templates

1The situation Larder, December 2027

Six cities are open. Each launch taught something, and the lessons are in four places: Grace's playbook (the apps), Sanne's lessons pages, the project file, and people's heads, mostly Sanne's, Joost's and Yara's. Ghent and Haarlem open in 2028, and a second project manager will run them, starting in February. Asked “how do we launch a city?”, three people would give three answers, all partly right.

  • Lessons were captured, but in different places.
  • Nothing says which parts are fixed and which are local.
  • Old advice was never removed, so the documents only grew.
meanwhile, in Grace’s notebook
  • Launching a New City (Grace, vol. 6): Grace's city playbook for the product side: configuration vs local, a launch rhythm, five daily numbers; v1 Utrecht, v2 The Hague, v3 after Antwerp.

2Real-life analogy the chain's recipe card

A restaurant chain's recipe card fixes what makes the dish the dish: the sauce, the weights, the cooking time. It leaves room for what each kitchen must decide: the local supplier of tomatoes, the plate. When a kitchen finds a better way, the head chef changes the card and dates it, and takes off the line nobody follows any more. A card that only grows becomes a book nobody reads during service.

3How it solves it one versioned playbook: fixed, local, kit, changes

On Monday 13 December 2027 Sanne and Grace merge their two documents into playbook v4: one timeline, a product chapter and a project chapter, each with one owner. Each version has changed the recipe. v1 (Utrecht, April 2027) fixed the eight workstreams, the five milestones and the people and money sums: a city in eight weeks. v2 (The Hague, June) made a city configuration, not code. v3 (Antwerp, October) added Antwerp's four lines. v4 (after Eindhoven) adds the tailoring sheet, the launch kit of 18 templates from the artefact review, and benefit checks at +4 and +12 weeks with a named owner (see “Did we get what we paid for?”). Two sections are fixed in every city and decided locally, and every page says which. v4 is also 11 pages shorter than v3 with Grace's added: lines nobody had used in two launches were taken out. The test comes in February: Nadia, the new project manager, plans Ghent from it in her second week, and her questions become v4.1.

  1. Gather the lessons from every launch

    And the lessons pages, and people's heads.

  2. Keep what held in more than one place

    One-off lessons stay on the lessons page.

  3. Mark each part fixed or local

    On the page itself.

  4. Attach the kit

    The templates that were actually used.

  5. Take out what nobody used

    Date every version.

  6. Test it on someone who didn't write it

    Their questions are the next version.

＋Going deeper what goes in, how lessons become lines, fixed vs local, and keeping it short

Most playbooks fail in one of two ways: nobody writes them, or nobody can read them. Larder's went through four versions in nine months. This chapter shows what a playbook needs, how a lesson earns a line, how to decide fixed and local, and the habit that kept v4 shorter than v3.

1What a playbook needs

  • A timeline with milestones and gates, in weeks from the start.
  • What's fixed in every instance, and why (each fixed line points to the launch that taught it).
  • What's decided locally, and who decides.
  • A tailoring sheet to fill in first.
  • A kit: the templates, already shortened.
  • A version history and an owner.

What it doesn't need: everything that was ever written about launches. That's what the lessons pages are for.

2How a lesson earns a line

A lesson goes into the playbook only after four steps: it's written in a closing report; it's a pattern (it happened, or nearly happened, in more than one launch); it becomes a specific line; and it's checked on the next launch. Rotterdam's 41 lessons gave 12 changes; Antwerp's 33 gave only 4, because most were already covered or one-off.

3Fixed or local?

Make a part fixed when getting it wrong was expensive and the right answer is the same everywhere: gates, store IT in week one, owned risks. Make it local when the right answer depends on the place: the builder, the permit route, the zones. When in doubt, fix the question and leave the answer local: “list every permit with a local expert” is fixed; which permits is local.

4Keeping it short

Every review asks one more question than “what should we add?”: what didn't anyone use? A line unused in two launches comes out, into the history. v4 lost eleven pages that way. The test of length is simple: Nadia read v4 in an afternoon and planned Ghent in her second week. A playbook that takes a week to read gets skimmed, and then it's back in people's heads.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a task repeated twice

  • A checklist.
  • Updated when it fails.
  • One owner.

Standard

city launches

  • A versioned playbook: fixed, local, kit.
  • Changed after every launch.
  • Tested on a new reader.

Heavy

franchises, regulated roll-outs

  • A controlled method with change approval.
  • Training and certification on it.
  • Audits of use.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Never written

    It leaves with the people. Fix → write it after the second launch.

  • Only grows

    Nobody reads it. Fix → remove what nobody used.

  • All fixed

    Local sense is overruled. Fix → mark fixed and local.

  • Tested by its author

    Gaps stay invisible. Fix → test it on a new reader.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When a kind of project repeats.
  • Before a new person runs one.
  • After every instance.
  • When lessons are in many places.

Skip it when…

  • One-off projects: keep the lessons page instead.
