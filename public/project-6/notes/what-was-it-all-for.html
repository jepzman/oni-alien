a.k.a. the six principles revisited: the book's closing note

What Was It All For?

TL;DR

The guide's six principles read like sentences anyone would agree with: see the whole, focus on value, build quality in, be accountable, think long-term, let the team decide. They only mean something when you can name the day each one changed a decision. At the end of a project, or a book, go back to the principles and write that day next to each. The ones without a day are the ones you haven't learned yet.

1The situation Larder, Monday 25 September 2028

Three days before the board of 28 September, eight cities are open: Amsterdam, Rotterdam, Utrecht, The Hague, Antwerp, Eindhoven, Ghent and Haarlem, with about 51,000 orders a day in September, on course for the board's 60,000 by December. Sanne rereads the first pages of this notebook, from January 2026: a lease date fifteen weeks away, Joost's spreadsheet with 31 rows without an owner, and the six principles she copied out on her second day. She agreed with all six then. She couldn't have said what any of them would cost or change.

  • Principles are easy to agree with and hard to use.
  • Each one only became real on a particular day.
  • That day is worth writing down.

2Real-life analogy looking back down the path

At the top of a long hike you can look back down the path and see which turns mattered: the stream where you nearly turned back, the ridge you skipped because of the wind. On the way up, each one looked like any other bend. The view from the top doesn't change the route. It tells you which bends to watch for next time.

3How it solves it each principle, and the day it changed a decision

Adopt a holistic view: Antwerp's permit, the thing outside the plan and outside the room, which set the date for a whole city. Focus on value: the benefits check of January 2028, which found Rotterdam's on-time target met two months late, and that nobody had looked. Embed quality: store IT in week one of every launch since Rotterdam's 4G router, a check that costs an hour and has never been needed since. Be an accountable leader: Ghent amber before the investors on 25 May 2028, and a sponsor who presented it. Integrate sustainability: Ghent's cooling plant changed while it was still a drawing. Build an empowered culture: the first and the last: on 4 February 2026 the team threw away Sanne's 96-task plan and planned its own work, and on 17 July 2028 Nadia moved Haarlem's date without asking her. Two years, eight cities, ninety-odd pages. If Sanne could leave one line on the first page for the person she was on 12 January 2026, it would be: the plan is the team's, the truth is yours, and the thing that sets your date is usually outside the room.

Vocabulary for the exam

Principle
A general rule that guides decisions in every situation.
Holistic view
Seeing the project as one system inside a bigger one.
Value
The benefit the project exists for.
Accountability
Owning the outcome and the truth about it.
Stewardship
Looking after what you're trusted with, for the long term.
Empowerment
Giving people the room, facts and trust to decide.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

end of a small project

  • Reread the principles.
  • One line on the one you used most.
  • Done.

Standard

end of a programme or year

  • Each principle with its day.
  • The ones without a day named.
  • A line for the next person.

Heavy

end of a career stage

  • A written reflection.
  • Shared with those you coach.
  • Into the organisation's method.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Agreeing, not using

    Principles as wallpaper. Fix → find the day each changed a decision.

  • Only successes

    The lessons are in the slips. Fix → include Antwerp.

  • Kept private

    The next person starts from zero. Fix → put it on the playbook's first page.

  • Stopping the notebook

    The next two years go unrecorded. Fix → start the next one.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • At the end of a project, programme or year.
  • Before handing over.
  • When coaching someone new.
  • When principles feel like slogans.

Skip it when…

  • Never.
