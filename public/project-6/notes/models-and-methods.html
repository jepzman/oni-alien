a.k.a. models, methods and artifacts: choosing the right tool for the question

Which Tools, When?

TL;DR

The PMBOK Guide lists dozens of models (ways of thinking: situational leadership, Tuckman's team stages, change models), methods (ways of doing: critical path, earned value, Monte Carlo, Scrum, Kanban, RACI) and artefacts (the documents they produce). None is required. Choose by the question you need answered on this project, the way a mechanic starts from the symptom: what decides the date? is the money buying progress? who's affected? A toolbox page sorted by question is worth more than knowing every tool by name.

  • Family: models, methods, artefacts
  • Lives in: one page sorted by question, in the playbook
  • Pairs with: tailoring, the playbook, every earlier note

1The situation Larder, Monday 14 February 2028

Nadia joined on 7 February as Larder's second project manager. In her second week she has read the playbook and Sanne's notebooks: critical path, earned value, Monte Carlo, RACI, MoSCoW, Scrum, Kanban, Tuckman, wideband Delphi, engagement matrices. “Which of all these do I use on Ghent?” Sanne realises she has never written down how she chooses. She just knows, from six launches, and knowing isn't something Nadia can read.

  • The tools are known by name, not by the question they answer.
  • Using everything is as wrong as using nothing.
  • The choice lives in one person's experience.

2Real-life analogy the mechanic's wall

A mechanic doesn't use every tool on every car. She starts from the symptom (a noise from the front left wheel), turns it into a question (bearing or brake?) and then picks the tool: the jack, a stethoscope, the torque wrench. Her wall is sorted by job, not alphabetically, so an apprentice can find the right one.

3How it solves it a page sorted by question, and three kinds of tool

Sanne writes the toolbox page with Nadia that afternoon, sorted by question. What decides the date? The network and critical path; three-point estimates for how sure; Monte Carlo when two chains meet or a yes-or-no risk sits on the path (Antwerp's permit). Is the money buying progress? Earned value, on long or fixed-price work; not on an eight-week Dutch launch. What's in and out? The scope statement, MoSCoW, the work breakdown. Who does what, who's affected? RACI, the register, power and interest. What could happen? The register, EMV, responses. Which way to run the work? Predictive for the fit-out; adaptive (Scrum for a team building in sprints, Kanban for a flow of requests) for the apps; hybrid for a launch. And three models for people: Tuckman's stages (a new team storms; plan for it), situational leadership (direct a beginner, delegate to an expert; she uses it on Nadia), and a change model (people must know, be able, and keep doing it: Ghent's pickers on the new handhelds). For Ghent, Nadia picks nine of the tools. Each line on the page points to the note that shows the tool on a real launch.

  1. Write the questions this project must answer

    Date, money, scope, people, risk.

  2. Pick the lightest tool that answers each

    Not every tool you know.

  3. Sort your toolbox by question

    So others can use it.

  4. Use models for people, methods for work

    Know which you're reaching for.

  5. Name what you're not using, and why

    On the tailoring sheet.

  6. Point each tool to a worked example

    A tool without an example is a word.

Vocabulary for the exam

Model
A way of thinking about a situation: Tuckman's stages, situational leadership.
Method
A way of doing something: critical path, earned value, Scrum.
Artefact
A document or other product of the work: a register, a plan, a burn-up.
Situational leadership
Leading differently by how able and willing a person is: direct, coach, support, delegate.
Tuckman's stages
Forming, storming, norming, performing (and adjourning).
Change model
Steps people go through to take up a change: aware, willing, able, sustained.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • Three questions, three tools.
  • No page.
  • Ask a colleague.

Standard

a city launch

  • A toolbox page sorted by question.
  • Chosen tools on the tailoring sheet.
  • Each pointing to an example.

Heavy

an organisation's method

  • A method library with guidance.
  • Training per tool.
  • Tool choice reviewed at gates.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Every tool, every time

    Overhead, no extra insight. Fix → one tool per question.

  • The favourite tool

    Every problem looks like a nail. Fix → start from the question.

  • Models as rules

    People aren't stages. Fix → use models to think, not to label.

  • Tools without examples

    Words, not skills. Fix → point each to a worked case.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When planning a project's method.
  • When a new project manager starts.
  • When a question has no tool.
  • When a tool is used out of habit.

Skip it when…

  • Never; even a small job has questions.

8Self-check quiz

Model or method: earned value?

A method. Tuckman's stages is a model.

When does Sanne reach for Monte Carlo?

When chains meet or a yes-or-no risk sits on the path, and the date matters.

Why not earned value on Ghent?

A fixed-price builder and a short launch: the gates and status answer the money question.

Ghent's new pickers resist the handhelds. Which tool?

A change model: are they aware why, able to use them, and is it kept up? Then train, not order.

A colleague uses Scrum for the fit-out. What do you ask?

What question it answers there; a builder's sequence needs a network and dates, Scrum suits a team building in sprints.
