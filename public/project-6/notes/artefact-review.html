Which Documents Earn Their Keep?

TL;DR

Every project produces artefacts: plans, registers, logs, reports. Their value is in being used, not written. After a project (or a few), review each one with one question: was it used after it was written, and by whom? Keep what was used, shorten what was used in part, merge what did the same job twice, and drop what nobody read. What's left becomes a small template kit. The register you read every week is worth ten plans nobody opened.

1The situation Larder, Monday 6 December 2027

The project file has 73 pages: 58 from Rotterdam, 15 from Antwerp. Eindhoven, tailored, used 20. Grace, merging the playbooks, asks which pages the playbook should point to. Nobody knows which pages were read after they were written. Some were opened every Friday for three months; some were written, filed and never seen again. Both kinds look the same in the file.

  • Documents were judged by being complete, not by being used.
  • Some did the same job twice (three schedule views).
  • A new project manager can't tell which to copy.

2Real-life analogy clearing the kitchen drawer

Clearing a kitchen drawer, you take everything out. Used this month: back in. Used this year: the cupboard. Two things that do the same job (three bottle openers): keep the best one. Not used in a year: out. The drawer closes again, and you can find the scissors.

3How it solves it one question per page, four piles, one kit

Sanne and Grace go through all 73 pages on Monday 6 December, asking of each: was it used after it was written, and by whom? The evidence: who opened it, what it was quoted in, whether Eindhoven copied it. Used as is: 21, among them the risk register, the issue log, the money rules, the gate criteria and the change log, all opened weekly and quoted in decisions. Used shortened: 14: the charter (to half a page), the status (to five lines), the plan (to the playbook's timeline). Merged: 12, such as the three schedule views into one milestone chart, or the lessons page into the closing report. Not used: 26, among them the earned-value sheets (the right tool once, in Rotterdam, but not again), the long RACI and the extras list after the baseline. The result is the launch kit: 18 templates, each a page or less, in playbook v4. Nothing is deleted. The full file stays as the worked example, and the 26 unused pages are still there for the project that needs them.

  1. Put every artefact on the table

    The whole file.

  2. Ask of each: used after it was written?

    By whom, how often, for what decision?

  3. Sort into four piles

    As is, shortened, merged, not used.

  4. Build a small kit from the first three

    One page each where possible.

  5. Keep the rest as examples

    Not as defaults.

  6. Repeat after the next few projects

    Usage changes.

Vocabulary for the exam

Artefact
A document, template or other product of project work.
Log
A running record: issues, changes, decisions.
Register
A list of items with their attributes: risks, stakeholders.
Baseline
An approved version used for comparison.
Template
A blank artefact to start from.
Template kit
The small set of templates a method says to use.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

small work

  • After the job, ask what you'd write again.
  • Keep that.
  • Bin the rest.

Standard

a repeated kind of project

  • Review every artefact for use.
  • A small template kit.
  • Repeat every few projects.

Heavy

regulated work

  • Required artefacts kept by rule.
  • A records policy.
  • Kit reviewed by the PMO.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Judged by completeness

    Long, unread documents. Fix → judge by use.

  • Deleting the examples

    The next unusual project needs them. Fix → keep them as examples.

  • Duplicates kept

    Two versions of the truth. Fix → merge what does one job.

  • One review forever

    Usage changes. Fix → repeat every few projects.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • After a few projects of the same kind.
  • Before building a playbook or kit.
  • When a new PM asks what to copy.
  • When documents multiply.

Skip it when…

  • A one-off project: the lessons page is enough.
