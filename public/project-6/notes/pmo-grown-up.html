a.k.a. the PMO, grown up: a launch office that serves project managers

Who Helps the Project Managers Now?

TL;DR

A project management office (PMO) is whatever helps projects run well across an organisation. It can be supportive (templates, lessons, coaching), controlling (required methods, reviews) or directive (it runs the projects). Grow it only as far as the work needs: start supportive, make mandatory only the few things that protect the organisation, and judge it by what it makes easier (days to an agreed plan, templates actually used), not by forms filled. If its knowledge lives in one person, it isn't an office yet.

1The situation Larder, February 2028

Vol. 1's launch desk was two hours a week of Sanne's time: templates, a lessons page, a quarterly sheet of projects for Femke, agreed “until Utrecht”. Two years later there are two project managers, a programme, playbook v4, an 18-template kit, a booking sheet for shared people and Femke's portfolio review. Nadia's first questions are all the same shape: where does this live, and who keeps it current? Every answer is “ask Sanne”. A support function that is one person's memory stops the day that person is on holiday.

  • The desk grew without anyone deciding what it was.
  • Nothing said what was optional and what wasn't.
  • Nobody measured whether it helped.

2Real-life analogy the nursing station

A hospital's nursing station doesn't treat patients. It keeps the charts current, the drug cupboard stocked, the rota clear and the handover sheet done every shift, so every nurse can get on with treating. A few rules there are absolute (how drugs are signed out). If the station starts telling surgeons how to operate, it has become something else, and usually something worse.

Translating it to the job
In real lifeOn the project
The chartsThe playbook and the 18-template kit
The rotaThe programme's booking of shared people
The handover sheetThe lessons page after every launch
Signing out drugsThe five gates and go / no-go: mandatory
Telling surgeons how to operateWriting PMs' plans for them: not its job

3How it solves it what it owns, what it doesn't, one rule, two measures

From Tuesday 1 February 2028 the desk becomes the launch office, written down on one page. It owns the playbook and kit (versioned, with change requests from any PM), the programme's people booking, Femke's quarterly portfolio sheet and the lessons page after every launch. It runs a monthly peer review: Sanne and Nadia read each other's status and risk register and ask the three review questions from Vol. 5. It is supportive by default, with one mandatory rule: the five gates and go / no-go, because Antwerp and Rotterdam both showed what they catch. It doesn't write project plans, approve every document, or own any launch. And it's measured by what it makes easier: days from kick-off to an agreed plan (Rotterdam about 35; Ghent 14) and templates actually used (Ghent: 16 of 18; the two unused go to the next review). Nadia keeps the kit from April. The office's knowledge now lives in two people and a document.

  1. Write down what the office owns

    And what it doesn't.

  2. Start supportive

    Templates, lessons, coaching, booking.

  3. Make only a few things mandatory

    The ones that protect the organisation.

  4. Run a peer review

    Project managers reading each other's work.

  5. Measure what it makes easier

    Days to a plan; templates used.

  6. Spread its knowledge

    Two people at least, and the document.

Vocabulary for the exam

PMO
Project management office: a function that supports or governs projects across an organisation.
Supportive PMO
Provides templates, lessons and coaching; low control.
Controlling PMO
Requires methods and reviews; medium control.
Directive PMO
Manages the projects directly; high control.
Peer review
Colleagues reviewing each other's work to improve it.
Bus factor
How many people could leave before knowledge is lost; here, one.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

one or two projects

  • A shared folder of templates.
  • Lessons after each project.
  • No office.

Standard

a launch programme

  • A supportive office with one mandatory rule.
  • Peer reviews.
  • Measured by days to a plan and templates used.

Heavy

large organisations

  • Controlling or enterprise PMO.
  • Portfolio governance, standards, audits.
  • Training and career paths for PMs.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Office as police

    PMs work around it. Fix → supportive by default.

  • Everything mandatory

    Ceremony replaces judgment. Fix → a few rules that protect.

  • Measured by forms

    Paperwork grows. Fix → measure what it makes easier.

  • One person's memory

    Stops on their holiday. Fix → two people and a document.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When several projects or PMs share methods.
  • When knowledge sits with one person.
  • When a new PM joins.
  • When the portfolio needs one view.

Skip it when…

  • A single project with one PM.
