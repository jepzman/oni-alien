One Project, or Many?

TL;DR

A project delivers a result. A programme runs related projects together for a benefit none of them gives alone: one roadmap, one place where shared people and shared risks are managed, one board. A portfolio is everything the organisation invests in, managed to choose what gets funded and in what order. When several projects share people, money and a goal, run them as a programme; when they only share a budget, a portfolio view is enough.

1The situation Larder, January 2028

Five pieces of work land on Sanne's desk in the same week: Ghent (Belgium again, June), Haarlem (September), a launch office to set up, the courier contract renewal, and Ana's station lockers pilot asking for space in the stores. Each has its own plan and its own status. They share Joost's operations lead, Eli and Lena, the same budget year, and the same promise: eight cities by the end of 2028. Run separately, they'll book the same people for the same weeks, and nobody will be watching the promise.

  • Five plans, no view of all of them.
  • Shared people are booked twice.
  • The benefit (eight cities, 60,000 orders a day) belongs to none of them.

2Real-life analogy the wedding weekend

One wedding ceremony is a project. The whole weekend, rehearsal dinner, ceremony, party, Sunday brunch, run by one planner so the guests get one good weekend, is a programme: the pieces share guests, caterers and a goal. The family's spending for the whole year, choosing between the wedding, a new car and the roof, is a portfolio. Different questions, different people asking them.

3How it solves it three layers, one benefit, one board

On Monday 10 January 2028 Femke makes Cities 2028 a programme, with Sanne as programme manager. Its benefit is the board's: eight cities and 60,000 orders a day by the end of 2028 (confirmed in the board plan of 20 March). Four projects sit inside it: Ghent, Haarlem, the launch office and the courier renewal. The lockers pilot stays outside, as Ana's project with one recorded dependency (store space), because it serves a different benefit. The programme adds three things the projects can't give themselves: one roadmap with every project's milestones; one risk list for what crosses projects (shared people, Belgian permits, the builder used in both cities); and a programme board every four weeks that books shared people and moves money between projects within the programme's total. Above it, Femke's quarterly sheet becomes a real portfolio review: cities against the ordering database, Larder Plus and the business portal, ranked for funding.

  1. Ask what benefit the projects share

    If none, they're a portfolio, not a programme.

  2. Group the ones that serve it

    Leave the rest outside, with dependencies.

  3. One roadmap

    Every project's milestones together.

  4. One list of cross-project risks

    And one booking of shared people.

  5. A programme board on a rhythm

    It can move money and people within the total.

  6. Report the benefit, not only the projects

    Cities open, orders a day.

Vocabulary for the exam

Project
Temporary work to deliver a result.
Programme
Related projects managed together for benefits none gives alone.
Portfolio
All the organisation's investments, managed to choose and balance them.
Programme roadmap
One timeline of all the programme's projects and benefits.
Dependency
Something one piece of work needs from another.
Benefit
The measurable improvement the work exists for.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

two related projects

  • One shared timeline.
  • A weekly call between the PMs.
  • No board.

Standard

Cities 2028

  • A programme with a benefit, a roadmap and cross-project risks.
  • A board every four weeks.
  • A portfolio sheet above it.

Heavy

large transformations

  • Programme management office.
  • Benefits register and tranches.
  • Portfolio governance with stage funding.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Programme of unrelated work

    A board with nothing to decide. Fix → group only what shares a benefit.

  • Programme as a big project

    Detail chokes it. Fix → manage the links; let projects run.

  • No benefit owner

    Projects finish; the promise doesn't. Fix → report the benefit.

  • Portfolio by habit

    Last year's priorities. Fix → rank investments each quarter.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Several projects sharing people, money and a goal.
  • When projects collide on people or dates.
  • When the benefit needs more than one project.
  • Quarterly, for the portfolio.

Skip it when…

  • A single project; a portfolio of unrelated small work.

8Self-check quiz

Project, programme or portfolio: Ghent's launch?

A project, inside the Cities 2028 programme.

Why is the lockers pilot outside the programme?

It serves a different benefit; it's Ana's project, with one dependency.

What can the programme board do that a project can't?

Move money and people between projects within the total; manage cross-project risks.

Two project managers both book Eli for the week of 12 June. Who decides?

The programme: shared people are booked at programme level; the board settles it.

Femke asks whether the business portal should join the programme. What do you ask?

Does it serve the programme's benefit (eight cities, 60,000 a day)? If not, it's a separate investment in the portfolio.

try it

List the projects that share people with yours. Do they serve one benefit? If yes, draw one roadmap of all of them and one list of the risks between them.
