a.k.a. handing over: letting someone else run it

Can Someone Else Run This?

TL;DR

Handing over work you've led means moving decision rights, not just documents: write down what moves, introduce the new lead to everyone who calls, let them shadow, then lead with you as back-up, then step out on a date. The hardest part is the last one: when someone phones you out of habit, send them on instead of deciding. Tell every outside party in writing who decides now. A handover is finished when the new lead makes a big decision without you.

1The situation Larder, week of 24 July 2028

This is Sanne's mistake for this collection. Haarlem was handed to Nadia on 3 July, with a plan. On Wednesday 26 July the builder's site manager phones Sanne, as he has for two launches, about moving the e-bike chargers to the other wall. It's a small, sensible change; Sanne agrees on the call. Nadia hears about it on Friday, from the builder. Now one supplier has two people who decide, and Nadia has learned that her authority holds until someone rings Sanne.

  • Suppliers call whoever they're used to.
  • Sanne answered out of habit, and helpfully.
  • The handover moved the documents, not the phone calls.

2Real-life analogy the baton

In a relay the baton changes hands in a passing zone. The incoming runner is already running alongside; the outgoing runner hands it over cleanly, and then lets go and stops running. If the old runner keeps a hand on the baton for a few more steps, out of care, both slow down and the team may be disqualified.

3How it solves it plan, shadow, lead, step out, and redirect the phone

The plan, written on 26 June: what moves (decision rights, open risks and issues, budget and contingency, the steering note), introductions (the builder, Hilde, Haarlem's landlord), and dates. Nadia shadows every Haarlem meeting in the last two weeks of June and leads from 3 July with Sanne as back-up. The handover's real test comes early: on 17 July Nadia and Ana decide to move Haarlem from 4 to 11 September so it opens on the new ordering database, and Nadia takes it to Femke herself. Then the slip. On Friday 28 July Sanne fixes the cause, not just the charger: a letter to every Haarlem supplier saying Nadia decides; Sanne's phone and email reply “ask Nadia”; the charger change goes back to Nadia, who keeps it. From 7 August Sanne steps out to the programme board only. Haarlem opens on Monday 11 September: Larder's eighth city, in time for the board of 28 September.

  1. Write what moves

    Decision rights first.

  2. Introduce them to everyone who calls

    In person where you can.

  3. Shadow, then lead with back-up

    With dates.

  4. Tell outside parties in writing

    Who decides now.

  5. Send calls on; don't decide

    Even small things.

  6. Step out on a date

    Done when they decide something big without you.

Vocabulary for the exam

Handover
Moving responsibility for work from one person to another.
Decision rights
Which decisions a role may take alone.
Shadowing
The new lead joins the work before taking it over.
Back-up
The outgoing lead, available but not deciding.
Step-out date
When the outgoing lead stops taking part.
Two voices
A supplier or team hearing decisions from two people.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small piece of work

  • A one-page note.
  • Introductions.
  • Stop answering for it.

Standard

a city launch

  • Plan, shadow, lead, step out, with dates.
  • Decision rights in writing.
  • Suppliers told; calls redirected.

Heavy

a programme or a regulated service

  • Formal transition plan and acceptance.
  • Knowledge transfer sessions.
  • A hypercare period with metrics.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Documents, not decisions

    Nothing really moves. Fix → move decision rights first.

  • Answering out of habit

    Two voices. Fix → send calls on.

  • No step-out date

    The old lead lingers. Fix → set it in the plan.

  • Outside parties not told

    They call who they know. Fix → write to them.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • When someone takes over work you lead.
  • When you move to a bigger role.
  • Before leave or leaving.
  • Whenever a supplier calls the wrong person.

Skip it when…

  • Never; on small work it's one note and a phone call.
