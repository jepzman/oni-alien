Can AI Help?

TL;DR

AI assistants are good at first drafts (status, minutes, risk lists), summaries, consistency checks across plans and translation drafts, and they save real time. They don't know what happened on site this morning, they can fill a gap with something that sounds right and isn't, and they must not see confidential data outside an approved tool. Use them a lot, under three rules: an approved tool only; a person checks every fact against its source; the sender owns every line. Decisions, judgment about people and relationships stay human.

1The situation Larder, Friday 11 February 2028

This is Sanne's mistake for this collection. In the second week of a six-week trial of Larder's approved AI assistant, she lets it draft the programme's Friday status from her Thursday notes and the issue log. It reads well. She skims it and sends it to Joost. One line says “Ghent lease signed.” Her notes said “agreed in principle”; it's signed on 2 March. Joost congratulates Femke in the leadership channel before Sanne notices. Corrected within the hour, no harm done, except to the thing a status report is for: being believed.

  • A fluent draft read as a checked one.
  • “Agreed in principle” became “signed”: the gap was filled with what usually comes next.
  • The sender was Sanne, whoever drafted it.

2Real-life analogy the fast new assistant

Imagine a new assistant who drafts a letter in seconds, summarises a fifty-page report over coffee and spots that two documents disagree on a date. They've never been to the site, don't know the client, sometimes fill a gap with something that sounds right, and mustn't see the files marked confidential. A good manager uses them a lot, and reads everything before it leaves the building.

3How it solves it six weeks, five uses, four rules

The trial runs from 1 February to 10 March 2028, on Larder's approved assistant (company contract, data not used for training), under Lena's rules: no personal data, no contract terms in any other tool. Five uses earn their place. Drafting the Friday status from notes and the issue log: 90 minutes → 30, with every line checked against its source after 11 February. Summarising meetings in English and Dutch. Suggesting risks for Ghent from the Rotterdam and Antwerp lessons pages: 23 suggested, 9 kept, 2 that nobody on the team had thought of, and 1 dangerously wrong: Ghent needs no environmental permit because Antwerp's was granted. (It does; a permit is per site. Sanne, after Antwerp, caught it at once; Nadia, in her first week, might not have.) Checking the plan: three dates that disagreed between the roadmap and Ghent's plan. Translation drafts for the Flemish writer to correct. The trial ends with four rules in the playbook: approved tool only; every fact checked against its source; the sender owns every line; say when a text was drafted with AI. Decisions, judgment about people and conversations stay with people.

  1. Use the organisation's approved tool

    With legal's rules on data.

  2. Give it the sources, not the conclusion

    Notes, logs, the plan.

  3. Use it to draft, summarise and check

    Not to decide.

  4. Check every fact against its source

    Especially the ones that read well.

  5. Own what you send

    Whoever drafted it.

  6. Say when AI drafted it

    So readers know what was checked.

Vocabulary for the exam

AI assistant
A tool that generates text, summaries or analysis from instructions and documents.
Hallucination
A confident output that isn't true or isn't supported by the sources.
Approved tool
An AI service the organisation has contracted and cleared for its data.
Human in the loop
A person reviews and decides before output is used.
Confidential data
Information that must not leave approved systems: personal data, contract terms.
Accountability
The person who sends or decides answers for the result.

＋Going deeper where it helps, where it doesn't, confidentiality, and checking habits

AI arrived in project work faster than any method in this book. The eighth edition of the guide treats it as a newer topic, not a process. This chapter is Sanne's practical view after six weeks: the jobs it does well, the jobs it shouldn't do, the confidentiality rules, and the checking habits that make the time it saves real.

1Where it helps

  • First drafts from your own sources: the Friday status from notes and logs; minutes from a transcript; a charter from the playbook's template.
  • Summaries of long material: a 40-page permit decision, a supplier's contract (inside the approved tool).
  • Consistency checks: dates across the roadmap and project plans; totals across the budget and the money table.
  • Prompts for thinking: risks from past lessons, questions for a pre-mortem. Treat them as suggestions from a well-read stranger.
  • Translation drafts and small formulas or scripts, checked by someone who knows.

2Where it doesn't

It doesn't know what happened today unless you tell it; it can't judge people (whether Nadia is ready to run Haarlem); it shouldn't make decisions, because someone must own them; it can't build trust with a neighbour, a regulator or a team. And it can be confidently wrong, in a way that's hardest to see exactly where you know least: the permit suggestion would have looked plausible to anyone who hadn't lived through Antwerp.

3Confidentiality

Prompts are documents. Anything pasted into a tool is sent to that tool's provider. Larder's rules, from Lena: use only the approved assistant; no personal data (names in an issue log are personal data); no contract terms outside it; the same Dutch and Belgian privacy rules apply as to any other system. When in doubt, describe the problem without the data.

4Checking habits that make it work

  • Give it the sources, and ask it to say which source each line comes from.
  • Check the lines that read best: the smooth sentences are where a gap was filled.
  • Check every number and date against the source, every time.
  • Own it: if you send it, it's yours. The status is Sanne's, not the assistant's.
  • Label it: “drafted with AI, checked by Sanne” tells readers what happened.

With those habits the 60 minutes saved on a status were real. Without them, they were borrowed from the next correction.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

personal use

  • Approved tool, no personal data.
  • Drafts and summaries.
  • Check before sending.

Standard

a programme

  • A trial with measured results.
  • Written rules with legal.
  • Every fact checked; sender owns it; labelled.

Heavy

regulated or sensitive work

  • AI policy and approved use cases.
  • Logs of AI-assisted outputs.
  • Independent review of high-stakes uses.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Skim and send

    Fluent and wrong goes out. Fix → check every fact against its source.

  • Unapproved tools

    Confidential data leaves. Fix → the approved assistant only.

  • AI as decider

    Nobody owns the decision. Fix → people decide.

  • Trusting it where you know least

    Errors look plausible there. Fix → check hardest where you're newest.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Drafts from your own sources.
  • Summaries and translations.
  • Consistency checks.
  • Prompts for risk and pre-mortem thinking.

Skip it when…

  • Judgments about people; anything confidential outside approved tools; decisions.
