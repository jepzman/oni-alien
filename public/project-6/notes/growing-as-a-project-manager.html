a.k.a. growing as a project manager, and what certification is for

How Do I Get Better at This?

TL;DR

Experience is what happened to you; growth is what you can now do that you couldn't. Turn the first into the second on purpose: keep a log (one page per hard question, written that week), ask for feedback from several people, pick one or two weak things to work on, take a stretch that exercises them, teach someone, and measure yourself against a standard now and then. A certification such as the PMP is good for a shared vocabulary and a structured review of what you learned by doing; it tests methods well and judgment barely.

1The situation Larder, Wednesday 12 January 2028

Sanne's two-year review with Joost. She arrived on 12 January 2026 from warehouse fit-outs, with a 96-task plan the team threw away on 4 February. Since then: Rotterdam, Utrecht, The Hague, Antwerp (five weeks late), Eindhoven, a playbook, and now a programme. Joost asks two questions: “What are you better at? And what next?” Sanne has a long list of what happened, and finds the list of what she can now do is much shorter, and harder to write.

  • Experience piled up faster than reflection.
  • Strengths and gaps were impressions, not evidence.
  • “What next” was a job title, not a skill.

  • Growing as a Product Owner (Grace, vol. 6): Grace's two-year review (8 November 2027): what she's better at, from evidence, and a plan for the next year.

2Real-life analogy the cyclist's training

A cyclist who wants to ride further doesn't just ride more. She keeps a log of every ride, asks a coach to watch her on a climb, works on her weak thing (hills), and enters one event a year that measures her against a standard. Riding is the experience. The log, the coach and the hills turn it into progress.

3How it solves it evidence, two weak things, three ways to grow, one habit

Before the review Sanne asks six people (Joost, Femke, Grace, Ana, Eli, Yara) two questions each: what do I do well, and what should I do differently? Better at, in their words: fitting process to the work, a status they believe, and teams that own their estimates. To work on: people outside Larder (Antwerp's permit office was her blind spot), letting go (Eli: “you still fix things you should hand back”), and thinking in programmes, not launches. Three ways to grow follow. A stretch: the Cities 2028 programme. A person: coaching Nadia, which turns “letting go” into a weekly practice (and a mistake, in February). A standard: the PMP exam, taken on 21 April 2028 and passed. She's clear what it's for: a shared vocabulary with partners and the next hires, and a structured pass through the guide to find what she'd learned by doing and what she'd never met. It doesn't prove judgment; Antwerp did that, the hard way. And one habit she's kept since her first week: this notebook. One page per hard question, written the week it happened. Ninety pages in two years, and the playbook was built from them.

  1. Keep a log

    One page per hard question, that week.

  2. Ask several people for feedback

    Two questions each.

  3. Pick one or two weak things

    From the evidence.

  4. Take a stretch that exercises them

    And someone to teach.

  5. Test yourself against a standard

    Know what it does and doesn't prove.

  6. Review once a year

    Better at, to work on, next.

＋Going deeper three kinds of skill, learning from projects, certifications, and mentoring both ways

Most project managers learn by doing, which is slow and expensive for the projects they learn on. This chapter covers what there is to get better at, how to learn faster from your own projects, what certifications are actually good for, and why the person you coach teaches you as much as you teach them.

1Three kinds of skill

PMI groups a project manager's skills into a talent triangle. Ways of working: the methods in this book, from critical path to Monte Carlo. Power skills: leading without authority, communicating across languages, coaching, telling the truth under pressure. Business acumen: knowing what the work is worth, what a week late costs, how investors see it.

Sanne's first year was mostly the first kind: she had to learn adaptive methods and Larder's money. Her second year was mostly the other two: the permit office, Femke's investor update, Nadia. That's the usual shape.

2Learning from your own projects

  • Write it down the same week: the situation, what you did, what you'd do next time. Memory edits.
  • Look for your own patterns: Sanne's three biggest mistakes (the internet line, the permit, the drawings) were all the same kind: something outside the room that nobody in the room knew.
  • Ask the people who were there: feedback from six people is evidence; one opinion is weather.
  • Turn a lesson into a habit, not a resolution: “read three low rows aloud at every review.”

3What certifications are for

A certification such as the PMP (PMI), PRINCE2 (a UK method), or an agile one gives three things: a shared vocabulary with other professionals, a structured review of a whole body of knowledge (Sanne found two areas she'd never met: procurement contract types beyond fixed price, and formal quality audits), and a signal to employers and partners. It doesn't show judgment, influence or courage. Take one when the vocabulary and the review are worth the study; don't mistake it for the experience.

4Mentoring, both ways

Coaching Nadia was on the plan to teach Nadia. It taught Sanne more: the 25 February status showed her “letting go” in one evening, and Nadia's questions in her second week (“which tools do I use?”) produced the toolbox page. Find someone to coach early. And keep someone who coaches you: for Sanne from 2028, Ana, who grows architects the same way.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Experience as growth

    Ten years, one year ten times. Fix → log, reflect, change a habit.

  • Feedback from one person

    Weather, not evidence. Fix → ask several.

  • Certificate as proof

    Judgment untested. Fix → know what it's for.

  • No one to coach

    A slower way to learn. Fix → teach someone early.
