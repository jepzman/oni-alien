a.k.a. ethics and professional conduct: telling the truth under pressure

What Would You Do?

TL;DR

A project manager's reports are only useful if they're always true, so pressure to shade them is an ethical question, not a presentational one. PMI's code names four values: responsibility, respect, fairness and honesty. In practice: don't report what you know is false, in either direction; follow your own written rules (the colour rules exist for this moment); offer the person under pressure a true version that helps them: the facts, the recovery and a decision date. Say no to the request and yes to the person.

1The situation Larder, Monday 22 May 2028

Ghent's fit-out gate, due Friday 2 June, is forecast for Thursday 8 June: the builder is six days behind after a late steel delivery. The opening on 19 June still holds, with less time to stock. By the colour rules written in Rotterdam, that's amber. Femke has an investor update on Thursday 25 May. “Show Ghent green. It'll be fine by June, and investors only read colours.” She isn't asking for anything dramatic: one colour, for one week, for a good reason. Nadia, who runs Ghent, is in the room.

  • A small untruth, to a powerful audience, for a likely-true outcome.
  • The colour rules were written for exactly this.
  • A new colleague is learning what's normal here.

2Real-life analogy the inspection sheet

A friend selling his car asks his mechanic to write “no faults” on the inspection sheet: the small oil leak “is nothing”. The mechanic writes “small oil leak, cheap to fix” and offers to fix it before the sale. The friend is annoyed for a day. The buyer trusts the sheet, and every sheet that mechanic signs afterwards is worth something.

3How it solves it no to the request, yes to the person: a true version that helps

Sanne says no, and in the same breath offers something. Green would be untrue and would break the colour rules; if June went wrong, every later status would be doubted, including Nadia's. Red would be untrue the other way: the opening holds. She offers amber with the facts and the recovery: the fit-out six days late; the opening on 19 June holds; stocking compressed from twelve days to eight with a second shift; a decision point on Friday 2 June, when Femke gets the next update. And one sentence for the investors, written with her: “Ghent is on track to open on 19 June; the fit-out is six days behind and we've planned the recovery.” Behind it are PMI's four values: responsibility (own the report), respect (for Femke's pressure, and her audience), fairness (investors decide on true information), honesty. Femke presents amber on 25 May. The investors ask one question. The gate passes on Wednesday 7 June; Ghent opens on 19 June. Later Nadia asks Sanne what she'd have done if Femke had insisted. “Told her I couldn't send it, and that she could. Then written down that I'd said so.”

  1. Recognise the question as ethical

    Not presentational.

  2. Check your own written rules

    They exist for this moment.

  3. Say no to the untruth, both ways

    Not green, not red.

  4. Offer a true version that helps

    Facts, recovery, decision date.

  5. Write it with them

    One sentence they can present.

  6. If overruled, don't send it yourself

    And record that you said so.

Vocabulary for the exam

Code of ethics
A profession's statement of the conduct it expects; PMI's has four values.
Responsibility
Owning your decisions and their consequences, including reports.
Respect
Treating others, their views and their pressures with consideration.
Fairness
Deciding impartially; letting others decide on true information.
Honesty
Understanding the truth and acting truthfully, in what you say and imply.
Watermelon status
Green outside, red inside: reports that hide trouble.

tools: the colour rules, the facts, a recovery, a decision date, and one sentence written together.

4Light, standard, heavy how much of this do you need?

Light

a small internal report

  • Report it true.
  • Say what you're doing about it.
  • Done.

Standard

a programme with a board

  • Written colour rules followed.
  • A true version that helps the person under pressure.
  • Record if overruled.

Heavy

regulated or public reporting

  • Independent assurance of reports.
  • Whistle-blowing routes.
  • Ethics training and audits.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Shading “just this once”

    Every later report is doubted. Fix → always true, both ways.

  • Saying no and nothing else

    The person is left with their pressure. Fix → offer a true version that helps.

  • Moralising

    Pressure becomes a fight. Fix → respect their position; give facts.

  • Silent compliance

    You own the untruth. Fix → don't send it; record that you said so.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Whenever a report is asked to say less than the truth.
  • Before audiences with power.
  • When a colleague watches what's normal.
  • When rules feel inconvenient.

Skip it when…

  • Never.

8Self-check quiz

Name PMI's four values.

Responsibility, respect, fairness, honesty.

Why not red, to be safe?

Untrue the other way: the opening held; overstating is also misreporting.

What did Sanne give Femke instead of green?

Amber with facts, the recovery, a decision date, and one sentence for investors.

Femke insists on green. What then?

Sanne doesn't send it herself; Femke can present her own view; Sanne records that she said so.

A supplier offers you a weekend away “for being a great client” mid-tender. What's at stake?

Fairness and honesty: decline, tell your manager, keep the tender clean.

try it

Find your project's colour rules. If none are written, write them before you need them. Then think of the last time you shaded a report, and write the true version that would have helped.

remember!!

Write “small leak, cheap to fix”, then offer to fix it.
