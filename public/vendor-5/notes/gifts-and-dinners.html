Can We Take the Client to the Football?

TL;DR

Hospitality can be a genuine thank-you or something that looks like a bribe, and the difference is often timing, value and who's deciding what. Know both companies' policies, keep a register, and during any procurement, offer nothing to the people involved beyond coffee. Thank people with words, in writing, copied to their boss. If a gift needs explaining, it isn't a gift.

1The situation Wrenfield and MetroVolt, December 2028

Vivian wants to say thank you for wave 1. Her plan: a hospitality box at a football match in December for Elaine, Desmond and Hollis, about £450 a head, with Graham and Ines.

  • MetroVolt's phase 2 tender opens in March. Desmond runs it; Elaine and Hollis will be on the panel.
  • Vivian: “It's just a thank-you. Everyone does it.”
  • Vexmoor's assurance team sits on the same floor and notices everything.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Desmond

Desmond would have to declare a £450 box, then explain to auditors why he accepted hospitality from a bidder three months before evaluating its bid. Saying yes would put him in a terrible position; saying no would feel rude. He'd rather not be asked.

“Please don't put me in that position.”

Wrenfield Ines and Vivian

Vivian means it kindly. But a box for the tender panel from a bidder, months before the tender, is exactly what anti-bribery rules are written about. It could disqualify Wrenfield and embarrass three people who've been good to it.

“A thank-you shouldn't need explaining to an auditor.”

The question isn't “is it a bribe?” but “would it look like one to someone who doesn't know us?”

3Real-life analogy the referee and the club dinner

A football club wants to thank a referee for a well-run season. It doesn't invite him to the directors' box the week before he referees their cup final. It sends a letter, copied to the referees' association, saying what he did well. The referee can frame it, and nobody has to wonder about the final.

4How it works both policies, a register, nothing during procurement

Check both policies. MetroVolt's: gifts and hospitality over £50 must be declared and approved; nothing may be accepted from a supplier taking part in a live or planned procurement. Wrenfield's own policy (written after the UK Bribery Act guidance): register everything offered or received; no hospitality to people involved in a procurement Wrenfield is bidding in.

Decide. Phase 2 is a planned procurement; the three guests are on its panel. The box is declined, by Ines, with Graham's agreement, before anyone at MetroVolt is asked.

Thank people properly instead. Ines writes thank-you letters: to Customer Ops' exception desk (who worked through the night and Sunday), to MetroVolt IT's on-call team, to Bert. Each is specific, and copied to the person's director. Wrenfield pays for its own team's lunch. Elaine gets nothing but a card. She pins it to her screen.

Keep the register. Everything offered or received goes in it, including the box that wasn't, with the reason it wasn't.

  1. Read both companies' policies

    Thresholds, approvals, procurement rules.

  2. Check timing

    Is a procurement live or planned?

  3. Check who decides what

    Are guests on a panel?

  4. When in doubt, don't

    Decide before asking the client.

  5. Thank with words

    Specific, written, copied to their boss.

  6. Register everything

    Offered, received, declined.

A period when contact and hospitality with bidders are restricted.

Whether hospitality is reasonable in size and purpose.

5What the contract says what the contract and the law expect

Lodestar SOW · §15 (anti-bribery) · MetroVolt supplier codesigned 30 June 2027
declined

15.1 The Supplier shall comply with applicable anti-bribery laws and MetroVolt's Supplier Code of Conduct, including its gifts and hospitality rules.

the code says: nothing from a bidder during a planned procurement.

15.3 Breach of this clause entitles MetroVolt to terminate the Contract and exclude the Supplier from future procurements.

a £450 box against a £9m phase 2. and Desmond's reputation.

In plain words: anti-bribery clauses and supplier codes usually cover hospitality explicitly, with tougher rules during procurements. Breaching them can end the current contract and the next one.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • “Everyone does it”

    The rules disagree. Fix → read both policies.

  • Ignoring timing

    Hospitality before a tender. Fix → check for planned procurements.

  • Asking the client to decide

    You put them in a bad position. Fix → decide first.

  • Unregistered gifts

    Hard to explain later. Fix → register everything.

  • No thanks at all

    People feel unappreciated. Fix → specific letters, copied to their boss.
