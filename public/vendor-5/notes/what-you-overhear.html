What Do We Do with What We Overhear?

TL;DR

On a client's site you will overhear things: restructures, budgets, people's futures. Treat it as confidential: don't repeat it (not even inside your firm), don't trade on it (in bids, prices or negotiations), and don't act as if you know. If it creates a real legal or ethical duty, get advice. Otherwise, wait for the announcement. Overheard news is often wrong, and it is never yours.

1The situation The Grid, the lift, 14 December 2028

At 08:52 on Thursday 14 December, two MetroVolt directors get into the lift on floor 2. They don't notice Ines behind them. They talk about Ascend.

  • Customer Operations will lose 40 roles in the spring.
  • Phase 2's budget “may have to give” to fund other Ascend work.
  • Callum and Vivian are finalising Wrenfield's phase 2 pricing strategy that evening. A smaller budget would change everything about it.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt MetroVolt

Restructures are discussed for weeks before announcements; sometimes carelessly. The people affected (including Asif's colleagues) should hear it from their employer, properly, not from a rumour that started with a supplier.

“That wasn't meant for you.”

Wrenfield Ines

The information could help Wrenfield price phase 2. It could also be wrong, it isn't Wrenfield's, using it would breach confidentiality, and repeating it would hurt people Ines works with every day. The only question is how completely to forget it.

“I heard it. It isn't mine.”

Information you weren't meant to have is a test of character with no witnesses. That's what makes it a real one.

3Real-life analogy the overheard phone call on the train

On a train you overhear someone telling a colleague, loudly, that their company will make cuts next month. You happen to own shares in that company. You don't sell, and you don't tell your friends to sell: it would be using information you had no right to, and in some cases a crime. You put your headphones on.

4How it works don't repeat, don't trade, don't act, get advice if needed, wait

Don't repeat it. Not to Callum, Vivian or Graham, not even as “I heard something”. Inside Wrenfield, a rumour becomes a planning assumption within a day.

Don't trade on it. That evening, Callum asks whether there's anything that should change the phase 2 pricing strategy. Ines says: “Nothing I can share. Price it on the evidence, as agreed.” The strategy doesn't change.

Don't act as if you know. When Asif mentions Ascend rumours a week later, Ines says what she said in October: she doesn't know anything she can share, and the right people to ask are at MetroVolt. (True: what she overheard isn't knowledge she can share.)

Get advice if it matters legally. If she'd overheard something unlawful, or something that put people at risk, she would talk to Wrenfield's lawyer and, if needed, MetroVolt's whistleblowing route. This was neither.

Wait. In February, Ascend is announced: 36 roles in Customer Operations, not 40, and phase 2's budget unchanged. A bid priced on the lift would have been wrong.

  1. Don't repeat it

    To anyone, inside or outside your firm.

  2. Don't use it

    In bids, prices, negotiations or plans.

  3. Don't act as if you know

    Behave exactly as before.

  4. Ask: does it create a legal duty?

    If yes, get advice.

  5. Reduce exposure

    Stairs, headphones, step away.

  6. Wait for the announcement

    It's often different.

5What the contract says the duty that applies even to accidents

Lodestar SOW · §16 (confidentiality)signed 30 June 2027
confidential

16.1 The Supplier shall keep confidential all information about MetroVolt which it obtains in connection with the Services, however obtained, and use it only for the Services.

“however obtained”. a lift counts.

Phase 2 ITT (draft), 7.3 MetroVolt may exclude a bidder that has obtained confidential information that could give it an undue advantage.

a bid priced on the lift could be excluded, if anyone ever knew.

In plain words: confidentiality clauses usually cover information however you came by it, including by accident. Using overheard information in a bid can breach the contract and the procurement rules, even if nobody finds out.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • “Just between us”

    It's everyone's by Friday. Fix → don't repeat it at all.

  • Using it in a bid

    Breach, exclusion, and often wrong. Fix → price on evidence.

  • Acting as if you know

    People guess where you heard it. Fix → behave as before.

  • Ignoring a real legal duty

    Harm continues. Fix → get advice.

  • Staying where you'll hear more

    Temptation repeats. Fix → take the stairs.

11Self-check quiz

What are the three don'ts?

Don't repeat it, don't trade on it, don't act as if you know.

Why not tell your own bid team?

A rumour becomes a planning assumption; using it can breach confidentiality and procurement rules.

When should you get advice?

If what you overheard creates a legal or ethical duty, such as wrongdoing or risk to people.

What did the February announcement say?

36 roles, not 40, and phase 2's budget unchanged.

What does “however obtained” in clause 16.1 mean?

Confidentiality covers information you came by accidentally too.

what would you do?Vivian says she heard a rumour that phase 2's budget will be cut, and asks Ines to confirm. What does Ines say?

That she can't confirm or discuss rumours about MetroVolt's budgets, and that the bid should be priced on the evidence and the tender documents only.
