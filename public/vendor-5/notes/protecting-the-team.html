How Do We Protect Our Team from the Client?

TL;DR

Your team is lent to the client, not given. When someone on the client side mistreats them (shouting, contempt, late-night demands), step in at once, calmly; raise it the same day with the right person, about the behaviour; set rules that stop it recurring (one front door, working hours); and look after the person privately. The client relationship matters. Your people matter more, and they're watching what you do.

1The situation The Grid, December 2028

One week in December, after hypercare ends:

  • Monday: in a meter-data working group, Gareth's deputy shouts at Lottie about a field name, in front of eight people. Lottie says nothing and leaves early.
  • Monday to Wednesday: Rupert's office emails Hana at 23:00 three nights running with “urgent” questions about phase 2 estimates. She answers each by midnight.
  • Wednesday: a MetroVolt manager tells Oskar his testers are “lazy” in the corridor.
  • Hana hasn't had a weekend off since August.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt MetroVolt people

Under Ascend pressure, MetroVolt's managers are stretched and short-tempered. Most don't think of supplier staff as people they need to look after: they're “the vendor”, paid to be available. Nobody means harm; the harm happens anyway.

“They're the supplier. That's what they're paid for.”

Wrenfield Ines

Ines's team has watched her be diplomatic with MetroVolt for eighteen months. Now they're watching whether she'll be equally firm for them. If she isn't, they'll conclude that the client always comes first, and the best of them will leave.

“My team is watching what I do this week.”

A vendor lead's first duty to the client is good work. Their first duty to their team is not to let them be harmed doing it.

3Real-life analogy the agency nurse and the ward

An agency sends nurses to a hospital. If a consultant shouts at one of them, the agency manager doesn't say “they're the client”. She speaks to the ward manager the same day, agrees how requests will come in future, and checks the nurse is all right. The hospital usually thanks her: it doesn't want its consultants behaving like that either.

4How it works step in, raise it, make a rule, look after the person

In the moment. Ines wasn't in Monday's working group; Bilal was, and froze. Ines tells the team: next time, whoever is senior steps in at once, calmly: “Let's take this one offline.” In Wednesday's corridor, Ines is there; she says it.

The same day. Ines speaks to Elaine about all three incidents on Wednesday evening, and to Gareth (whom she now knows well) about his deputy. She describes the behaviour, not the person: “shouting at a junior engineer in a meeting”. Gareth apologises to Lottie himself on Thursday.

A rule. The team agreement v2, signed by Elaine on 13 December: urgent requests to Wrenfield go through Ines or Callum, not to individual engineers; no requests outside 08:00–19:00 except severity 1; disagreements about work go to the working group lead, not to the person.

The person. Ines checks in privately with each. Lottie is fine, mostly; she wanted to know Ines would have stepped in. Oskar laughs it off. Hana says she's fine, and then cries in the stairwell. That conversation starts the next volume.

  1. Step in at once

    Calmly: “let's take this offline”.

  2. Raise it the same day

    With the client lead and the person's manager.

  3. Describe behaviour, not character

    What happened, where, to whom.

  4. Make a rule

    One front door, working hours, in writing.

  5. Check in privately

    With each person affected.

  6. Tell the team what you did

    They need to know you'll do it again.

Vocabulary

Front door
The single route for requests to a team.
Duty of care

Bystander
Someone who witnesses mistreatment; their action or silence matters.
Out-of-hours contact
Requests outside agreed working times.

5What the contract says what protects the team

signed

18.1 Each Party shall ensure its personnel treat the other's personnel with courtesy and respect, and comply with the other Party's dignity at work policy while on its premises.

MetroVolt's own policy applies to its managers too.

TA v2, 5.2 No requests to Wrenfield staff outside 08:00–19:00 except severity 1 incidents.

Hana's 23:00 emails, ended by a sentence.

In plain words: many contracts include a mutual conduct clause and the client's dignity-at-work policy. Pointing to them makes protecting your team a matter of agreed rules, not a personal complaint.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Saying nothing

    Your team learns the client always wins. Fix → step in, raise it.

  • Raising it weeks later

    Nobody remembers; nothing changes. Fix → the same day.

  • Attacking the person

    They defend themselves. Fix → describe the behaviour.

  • No rule

    It happens again next week. Fix → front door, working hours.

  • Forgetting the person

    They feel unsupported. Fix → check in privately.

11Self-check quiz

What do you say in the moment?

Something calm like “let's take this one offline”.

Who do you raise it with, and when?

The client lead and the person's manager, the same day.

What do you describe?

The behaviour, not the person's character.

What rules went into the team agreement?

One front door for urgent requests; no requests outside 08:00–19:00 except severity 1; disagreements to the working-group lead.

Why check in privately?

People say they're fine in public; private check-ins find out.
