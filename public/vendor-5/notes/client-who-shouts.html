What Do We Do When the Client Shouts?

TL;DR

When a client shouts at you in a meeting, the room is watching how you respond, not who's right. Stay calm and lower your voice. Acknowledge the worry (not the accusation). Answer with one clear fact and where to find the proof, and offer to go through it afterwards. Follow up in writing the same day. Talk about the shouting itself later, privately. Never shout back, and never apologise for facts.

1The situation The Grid, room 27A, 21 November 2028

At the hypercare closing review on Tuesday 21 November, Rupert, two weeks into sponsoring Lodestar, reads from a one-line Vexmoor summary: “1,206 exceptions at cutover.”

  • He believes that means 1,206 customers with wrong bills.
  • He raises his voice, in front of twelve people: “This is exactly what I was warned about.”
  • In fact all 1,206 were handled by hand within a day by Customer Ops' exception desk, and no customer received a wrong bill. It's on page 4 of the pack he hasn't opened.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Rupert

Rupert is new to Lodestar, under pressure from Ascend's targets, and has been briefed mostly by Vexmoor. Wrong bills would be a board-level problem. His anger is mostly fear, expressed badly, in public.

“I won't be the sponsor who let this happen.”

Wrenfield Ines

Ines knows the facts are on her side. The danger is the room: twelve people watching whether Wrenfield is defensive, rattled or calm. Winning the argument loudly would lose the relationship with the new sponsor.

“The facts will win. My job is to let them, calmly.”

Anger in meetings is usually fear in public. Answer the fear; deal with the manner privately.

3Real-life analogy the airline gate agent

A passenger shouts at a gate agent that the flight is cancelled. The good agent doesn't shout back or argue. She lowers her voice, says “I understand you're worried about making your connection”, gives one fact (“the flight is delayed 40 minutes, not cancelled, and your connection is protected”), and shows him the screen. Most passengers calm down in a sentence.

4How it works calm, acknowledge, one fact, an offer; the manner later

In the room, Ines waits a second, lowers her voice and says three things:

  • Acknowledge the worry: “Wrong bills would be serious, and you're right to ask.”
  • One fact, and the proof: “All 1,206 were handled by hand within a day by Customer Ops' exception desk; no customer received a wrong bill. The reconciliation is on page 4.”
  • An offer: “I'd be glad to go through it with you after the meeting.”

Elaine confirms the fact from the exception desk's report. Rupert says “fine” and moves on. The meeting continues.

The same day, in writing: a five-line email to Rupert, through Elaine, with the exception figures, the reconciliation and the desk's report attached.

Later, privately: on Thursday, in a short one-to-one Rupert asked for, Ines says: “On Tuesday, I'd have preferred that question before the meeting. If something worries you, call me directly; I'll always give you the facts.” Rupert says Vexmoor's summary had alarmed him. He doesn't apologise. He does call directly, twice, in December.

  1. Pause and lower your voice

    Calm is contagious too.

  2. Acknowledge the worry

    Not the accusation.

  3. One fact, and where the proof is

    Short, checkable.

  4. Offer to go through it

    After the meeting.

  5. Follow up in writing the same day

    Facts, attachments.

  6. Address the manner privately

    Later, briefly, without blame.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Shouting back

    Two angry people, one loser: you. Fix → lower your voice.

  • Apologising for facts

    You've accepted a false claim. Fix → acknowledge the worry, not the claim.

  • A lecture

    The room loses patience. Fix → one fact.

  • No follow-up

    The false number survives. Fix → in writing, same day.

  • Never addressing the manner

    It becomes normal. Fix → privately, later, briefly.
