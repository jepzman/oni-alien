What If an Executive Wants a Slide?

TL;DR

Senior people need slides that make them look good to their board. Give them one, built from facts, with credit to the people who did the work, and let them choose the title. Don't fight over who gets praised; do refuse anything untrue. A slide that's accurate and generous survives the board, and the people named on it remember.

1The situation The Grid, floor 27, 23 November 2028

On 20 November Lord Varnish's assistant asks Ines for a slide for MetroVolt's board on 30 November, with a title already chosen: “Lodestar: the Orion foundation delivers.”

  • Lodestar didn't use Orion's hub. The design authority decided that in 2027.
  • The draft names nobody: not Customer Ops' exception desk, not Bert, not MetroVolt IT.
  • Hana's view: “Tell him Orion failed.” Lord Varnish founded Orion, and sits on the board.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Lord Varnish

Lord Varnish launched Orion in 2024 and watched it fail. A successful Lodestar is a chance to tell the board the investment wasn't wasted. That's human. It's also partly true: Lodestar learned a lot from Orion's mistakes.

“Let the board see Orion wasn't for nothing.”

Wrenfield Ines

Wrenfield doesn't need credit on the slide; it needs the slide to be true. A board paper claiming Lodestar was built on Orion's hub would be wrong, and could matter in phase 2. And the people who did the work deserve to be named.

“His title. Our facts. Their names.”

Credit is cheap to give and expensive to fight for. Accuracy is the one thing you can't give away.

3Real-life analogy the speech at the wedding

The bride's uncle wants to say in his speech that he introduced the couple. He didn't, quite: he invited them both to a party where they met. A wise best man doesn't argue. He gives the uncle a true version (“it all started at Uncle Simon's party”), thanks the people who organised the wedding by name, and lets the uncle enjoy his moment.

4How it works facts, credit, a true history line, and his title

Ines doesn't argue about the title. She sends a slide through Elaine on 23 November with three parts:

  • Facts: 379,997 customers moved on 24 September; 0.32 % exceptions, cleared within a day; no billing complaints; MAVIS's batch load down 16 %.
  • Credits: Customer Operations (the exception desk), MetroVolt IT, Network Assets, Halvorsen, Wrenfield. People first, supplier last.
  • A true history line: “Built on lessons from earlier programmes, including Orion.” True, generous, and not a claim about the hub.

The covering note says: “Title as you prefer; we've kept the facts to what the board can verify.” Lord Varnish's office changes the title to “Lodestar: lessons from Orion deliver”. It keeps every fact and every credit. Hana says that's still not true. Ines says it's close enough to be fair, and the facts underneath are exact.

  1. Don't argue about the title

    It's theirs.

  2. Build the body from verifiable facts

    Numbers the board can check.

  3. Credit people before suppliers

    By team, by name if possible.

  4. Offer a true version of their story

    Generous, not false.

  5. Say what you kept to, politely

    “Facts the board can verify.”

  6. Refuse only what's untrue

    Not what's flattering.

Vocabulary

Board paper
A document presented to a company's board of directors.
Credit politics
Competition over who is recognised for success.
Verifiable fact
A statement someone else can check.
Narrative
The story told around facts; flexible within truth.
Attribution
Naming who did or contributed to something.
Executive sponsor
A senior person whose reputation is attached to a programme.

5What the contract says what the supplier may and may not publish

Lodestar SOW · §14 (publicity)signed 30 June 2027
MetroVolt's slide

14.1 The Supplier shall not make public statements about the Services without MetroVolt's prior written consent.

the board slide is MetroVolt's. we provide facts; they present.

14.3 Each Party shall ensure information it provides for the other's governance is accurate.

the one line that lets us say no to a false claim.

In plain words: the client owns its board papers; you supply facts. An accuracy clause gives you a firm, polite reason to keep the facts right while leaving the story to them.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Fighting for credit

    You lose the executive and gain nothing. Fix → credit people, let them have the title.

  • Sending what was asked

    A false claim in a board paper. Fix → facts the board can verify.

  • Naming only the supplier

    Client teams feel used. Fix → people first, supplier last.

  • Mocking the old programme

    The founder sits on the board. Fix → a generous, true history line.

  • Going around your counterpart

    Politics you can't win. Fix → through Elaine.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Senior people ask for success slides.
  • A programme's history is politically sensitive.
  • Credit could go to the wrong people.

Skip it when…

  • The client already writes its own accurate summaries: just check the facts.
