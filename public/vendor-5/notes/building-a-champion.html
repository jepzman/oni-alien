How Do We Build a Champion?

TL;DR

A champion is someone on the client side who will speak for you, from their own experience, when you're not in the room. You can't buy, ask or flatter one into existence. You make them over months with evidence they can repeat, credit they can share and promises they've watched you keep. And you protect them: never ask a champion to lobby, leak, or bend a procurement. If they speak for you, it must be because it's true.

  • Lives in: the eighteen months before it

1The situation The Grid, January 2029

Phase 2's tender opens in March. The panel will be Rupert (decider), Desmond (running it), Hollis, Nkem, Gareth and Elaine. Vexmoor has advised Rupert for two years.

  • Vivian asks Ines to “have a quiet word” with Elaine about putting in a good word with Rupert.
  • Graham asks whether Gareth could be “brought onside” before the tender.
  • Both requests would put good people in a terrible position.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Elaine

Elaine has worked beside Wrenfield for eighteen months and trusts it more than any supplier she's known. She'll be on the panel. She must score fairly, declare any conflict, and be able to look Desmond in the eye. A “quiet word” request would force her to choose between her integrity and a supplier she likes.

“I'll say what's true when asked. Don't ask me for more.”

Wrenfield Ines

Ines knows Wrenfield has champions: Elaine, Rosa, and (surprisingly) Gareth. They became champions because of evidence, credit and kept promises. Asking them to lobby would turn that into something cheaper, and might get Wrenfield excluded.

“Champions are made by the work. Then you leave them alone.”

A champion who lobbies for you is a liability. A champion who simply tells the truth about you is the strongest asset a vendor can have.

3Real-life analogy the teacher's reference

A student doesn't ask her teacher to “put in a good word” with a university. She works hard for two years, and when the university asks the teacher for a reference, the teacher writes, from her own experience, what the student did. A reference the student had pushed for would be worth less, and the teacher would resent it.

4How it works evidence, credit, kept promises, and never asking for more

Looking back over eighteen months, Ines writes down what made Wrenfield's champions:

  • Evidence they can repeat: stories that are short, true and checkable. Elaine tells the 31 % story herself now: “they told us before we found out”. Rosa tells the defect triage story. Gareth tells the meter data service story.
  • Credit they can share: every success named their teams first: the exception desk on the board slide, Network Assets on the meter service, Rosa's testers in the go / no-go minutes.
  • Promises they've watched kept: the access tracker, the promises checklist, “no told-you-so” about MetroBus.

And she writes down what never to do. She declines Vivian's and Graham's requests the same day: “We don't ask anyone at MetroVolt to speak for us. If they're asked, they'll say what's true.” In February Desmond asks every panel member to declare relationships with bidders; Elaine declares eighteen months of working with Wrenfield, and Desmond records that she'll score with a second marker. That's how it should work.

  1. Do work worth talking about

    There's no shortcut.

  2. Give people short, true stories

    With numbers they can check.

  3. Share the credit

    Their teams first, always.

  4. Keep promises where they can see

    Lists, trackers, dates.

  5. Never ask a champion to lobby

    Or leak, or bend a procurement.

  6. Let procurement rules protect them

    Declarations, second markers.

Vocabulary

Champion
Someone on the client side who speaks for you from their own experience.
Advocacy
Speaking up for someone; worthless if requested.
Declaration of interest
Stating a relationship that might affect a decision.
Second marker
Another evaluator who checks a panel member's scores.
Lobbying
Trying to influence a decision outside its proper process.
Reputation
What people say about you when you're not there.

5What the contract says the rule that protects champions

Phase 2 ITT (draft) · §6.7 and §8.2to be published 5 Mar 2029
hands off

6.7 Bidders may not contact MetroVolt staff about this procurement except through the clarification process.

from 5 March, a “quiet word” isn't just unwise. it's exclusion.

8.2 Evaluators shall declare any relationship with a bidder; MetroVolt may assign a second marker.

Elaine's declaration protects her and us.

In plain words: procurement rules exist partly to protect the client's staff from suppliers who'd ask them for favours. Respecting them is how you keep your champions.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Asking for a good word

    Puts your champion in a terrible position. Fix → never ask.

  • Flattery

    Seen through, and resented. Fix → evidence and credit.

  • Taking the credit

    Nobody repeats your stories. Fix → their teams first.

  • Contact during procurement

    Exclusion. Fix → the clarification process only.

  • Champions of one

    One move and they're gone. Fix → several, at different levels.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Before any decision you'll be judged on.
  • Any long engagement.
  • When your firm wants to “work the relationships”.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip the work; always skip the asking.
