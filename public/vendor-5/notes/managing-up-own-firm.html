How Do We Manage Up in Our Own Firm?

TL;DR

A vendor's architect has two clients: the customer, and their own firm's leadership, who see the account through spreadsheets and phone calls. Give your own boss the same honesty you give the client: a weekly note, the numbers, the risks, and options with consequences for decisions only they can make. Be clear which decisions are theirs and which are yours, and never let pressure from home change how you behave on the client's floor.

  • Family: people, theirs and ours

  • Pairs with: protecting margin (vol. 4), saying no

1The situation Wrenfield, Leeds, 27 October 2028

At Wrenfield's monthly review in Leeds on Friday 27 October, Graham has three demands about Lodestar:

  • Cut the team from eight to three for the warranty, “to get the margin back”.
  • Have Vivian sell phase 2 to Rupert directly, “before Vexmoor does”.
  • Tell MetroVolt Wrenfield will be cheaper than Vexmoor on phase 2.

Graham is worried: margin at 10 %, phase 2 worth more than the whole of 2027's revenue. Each demand, done literally, would hurt the account he's trying to protect.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt MetroVolt (not in the room)

MetroVolt expects warranty support at the level promised, a fair phase 2 procurement, and bids built on facts. It has no view on Wrenfield's internal arguments, and shouldn't have to.

“(not asked, and shouldn't have to be)”

Wrenfield Ines and Graham

Graham owns the firm's risk: cash, margin, the next contract. Ines owns the account's integrity: what's promised, how Wrenfield behaves, the design. Both are legitimate. The friction comes when Graham's pressure arrives without the facts Ines sees every day.

“He sees spreadsheets. I see Elaine. He needs both.”

Most tension between a firm's leadership and its people on site comes from information gaps, not different values. A weekly note closes most of the gap.

3Real-life analogy the ship's captain and the owners

A ship's owners, on shore, see fuel costs and delivery dates. The captain sees the weather. A good captain sends regular, honest reports, gives the owners the decisions that are theirs (which port, which cargo) with options and costs, and keeps the decisions that are hers (how to sail through a storm). She doesn't follow an order that would sink the ship; she explains why, with the chart.

4How it works a Friday note, options with consequences, clear decision rights

A Friday note, from 27 October: the same shape as the client's status. Done this week, the numbers (margin, hours, cash), risks, and decisions Graham needs to make. Half a page. Graham reads it on Saturday mornings.

Options with consequences for each demand:

  • Team size: three would breach the warranty and hypercare service levels in Schedule 9; five after hypercare ends (19 November) meets them and saves about £14k a month. Graham chooses five.
  • Selling to Rupert: phase 2 is a planned procurement; direct selling now could get Wrenfield excluded (as with the football box). Instead: deliver warranty perfectly and let the readiness pack and the results speak. Graham agrees, reluctantly.
  • “Cheaper than Vexmoor”: an unpriceable promise. Instead: price phase 2 from wave 1's real velocity and data, at a margin above the floor. Graham agrees, if the bid / no-bid includes his floor.

Decision rights, written down. Graham decides team size, bid / no-bid and price floor. Ines decides design, client conduct and what Wrenfield promises on site. Both on one page, signed by both.

  1. Send your boss a weekly note

    Done, numbers, risks, decisions needed.

  2. Answer demands with options

    Each with its consequence.

  3. Use the contract as a fact

    Service levels, procurement rules.

  4. Agree who decides what

    Written, signed.

  5. Bring problems before they're crises

    No surprises, upwards too.

  6. Never act on pressure you disagree with on site

    Escalate inside your firm.

5What the contract says the client contract as a fact in an internal argument

Lodestar SOW · Schedule 9 (hypercare and warranty)baseline v2.0
internal

Sch. 9, 4.1 During warranty, the Supplier shall respond to Severity 1 defects within 1 hour and resolve within 1 Working Day, with named engineers familiar with the Solution.

three people can't do this. five can.

Phase 2 ITT (draft), 6.7 Bidders may not contact MetroVolt staff about this procurement except through the clarification process.

it won't be published until March. the spirit applies now.

In plain words: the client's contract is the most persuasive fact in an argument with your own leadership. It turns “I disagree” into “we've promised this”.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Silence upwards

    Your boss decides on spreadsheets alone. Fix → a weekly note.

  • Arguing without options

    It sounds like refusal. Fix → options with consequences.

  • Following pressure on site

    The client sees the firm's panic. Fix → escalate inside, behave the same outside.

  • Unclear decision rights

    The same argument every month. Fix → write them down.

  • Hiding bad numbers

    A worse surprise later. Fix → numbers every week.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Your firm's leadership is far from the client.
  • Pressure from home conflicts with the client's interests.
  • Before a big bid or commercial decision.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip it with a fixed-price account.

11Self-check quiz

What's in the Friday note?

Done, numbers, risks, and decisions Graham needs to make.

Why five people, not three, after hypercare?

Three would breach Schedule 9's warranty service levels; five meets them.

Why no direct selling to Rupert?

Phase 2 is a planned procurement; direct selling risks exclusion.

Why not promise to be cheaper than Vexmoor?

It can't be priced; price from evidence instead.

Whose decisions are whose?

Graham: team size, bid / no-bid, price floor. Ines: design, client conduct, on-site promises.

what would you do?Graham phones Rupert himself “just to say hello”. Rupert mentions it to Elaine. What does Ines do?

Tell Graham, privately and quickly, why it's risky before a procurement, and tell Elaine (no surprises) that it was a courtesy call and won't be repeated.

try it

Write a half-page Friday note to your own boss: done, numbers, risks, and the decisions only they can make, each with options and consequences.

remember!!

Give your own boss what you give the client: a weekly note, the numbers, and their decisions to make.

Three demands. Three counter-offers. Five people, not three. No selling to Rupert. No “cheaper than Vexmoor”.

Graham signed the decision-rights page. He said it was the first time anyone had told him which decisions were his.

Friday notes from now on. I should have started in 2027.
