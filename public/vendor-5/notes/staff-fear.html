What If Client Staff Fear We'll Take Their Jobs?

TL;DR

Replacing a system often threatens the jobs of the people who ran it, and they'll ask you, quietly, what it means for them. Don't promise what you can't control. Say what you know, plainly. Involve them in what comes next (they know things you need). And send job questions to their employer, who must answer them. Never speculate about restructures you've only heard about.

1The situation The Grid, 18 October 2028

MetroVolt's MAVIS support team is seven people. Wave 1 retired 16 % of MAVIS's nightly batch. Rumours about Ascend talk of “20 % fewer people”.

  • On 18 October, Asif, who's paired with Bilal since 2027, asks Ines at the coffee machine: “When MAVIS goes, do we go too?”
  • Ines doesn't know. MetroVolt hasn't said. It isn't Wrenfield's decision.
  • Two of the seven have stopped coming to the Lodestar stand-up. Bert retires in March; his knowledge is in their heads now.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Asif and the MAVIS team

They've kept MAVIS running for years, and Lodestar is visibly shrinking it. In a year of cost cuts they're scared, and Wrenfield is the most visible sign of the change. They ask the vendor because the vendor is at the next desk; their own management hasn't said anything.

“Are you here to switch us off along with MAVIS?”

Wrenfield Ines

Ines likes Asif and needs the MAVIS team's knowledge for waves 2–4. She also knows she has no right to promise anything about MetroVolt jobs, and that a wrong word at a coffee machine becomes a rumour by lunchtime.

“I can't answer his question. I can answer the ones that are mine.”

People don't need the vendor to promise their jobs are safe. They need honesty about what the vendor knows, and a way to be part of what comes next.

3Real-life analogy the builders and the tenants

Builders renovating a block of flats are asked by tenants: “Will we be able to stay?” A good site manager doesn't guess. He says what he knows (“we're doing two floors at a time, about thirty months”), asks the tenants what they know about the building, and says: “That question is for the housing association; I'll ask them to hold a meeting.” Then he makes sure they do.

4How it works don't promise, say what you know, involve them, send it to the client

Don't promise. Ines tells Asif honestly: “I don't know, and it isn't Wrenfield's decision. I won't guess, because a guess from me would sound like a fact.”

Say what you know. “MAVIS shrinks wave by wave, over about thirty months. Lodestar will need people who understand customer data to run it. That's not a promise about anyone; it's the plan.”

Involve them. Ines proposes to Elaine that the MAVIS team pair with Wrenfield on wave 1 support during hypercare, and that MetroVolt consider them for the Lodestar run team. By January three of the seven (Asif, Marta and one more) have joined it. Their MAVIS knowledge goes into Bert's book and the runbooks.

Send job questions to MetroVolt. Ines asks Elaine, the same afternoon, whether MetroVolt could talk to the team. Elaine and HR hold a session on 1 November: no promises either, but a timeline and a named person to ask.

Never speculate about Ascend. Not to Asif, not to Callum, not to anyone.

  1. Don't promise anything about jobs

    It isn't yours to promise.

  2. Say what you know, plainly

    Timelines, skills needed.

  3. Don't guess

    Guesses become facts by lunchtime.

  4. Involve them in what's next

    Their knowledge is valuable.

  5. Take the question to the client

    Same day, to your counterpart.

  6. Follow up

    Make sure someone answered them.

5What the contract says whose people they are

Lodestar SOW · §11.2 and §17signed 30 June 2027
their employer

11.2 The Supplier shall transfer knowledge to MetroVolt staff throughout the Services, so that MetroVolt can operate the solution.

the run team is §11.2 in practice. their knowledge moves both ways.

17.3 The Supplier shall not make representations to MetroVolt staff about their employment.

this clause exists for exactly the coffee-machine moment.

In plain words: contracts often forbid suppliers from making statements to client staff about their employment. That's protection for everyone; it's also a reason to send those questions straight to the client.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • False reassurance

    “You'll be fine” becomes a broken promise. Fix → don't promise.

  • Guessing

    Rumours with your name on them. Fix → say only what you know.

  • Avoiding them

    Fear turns to resentment. Fix → involve them.

  • Answering for the client

    Breaches §17.3 and trust. Fix → send it to the employer.

  • Ignoring absences

    Quiet withdrawal, lost knowledge. Fix → notice who stops coming.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Your project replaces or shrinks a team's work.
  • Staff ask you about their future.
  • A restructure is rumoured.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip honesty; the involvement depends on the client.

11Self-check quiz

What must a vendor never do when asked about jobs?

Promise, guess, or answer for the client.

what would you do?Asif asks Ines whether Wrenfield is hiring. What does she say?

That Wrenfield can't recruit MetroVolt staff involved in Lodestar during the contract (§17) and that his question about his future is best answered by MetroVolt; she'll make sure he gets an answer.

try it

Think of the people whose work your project changes. What do you honestly know about what happens to them? Who should answer what you don't know? How could they be involved in what comes next?
