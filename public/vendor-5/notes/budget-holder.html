What About the Stakeholder Who Holds the Budget?

TL;DR

When someone who opposed your project gains control of money it needs, don't avoid them and don't go around them. Meet them early, alone, and ask what they need. Look for a shared win that gives them ownership of something real. Give credit in public, and put the arrangement in writing. An opponent with a stake in your success is the strongest supporter you'll have.

1The situation The Grid, floor 20, 16 November 2028

Under Ascend, Gareth Pound's Network Assets becomes the budget holder for “customer data services”, including the meter data and the meter feed (COPYMTR's replacement) that phase 2 depends on.

  • Gareth wanted Lodestar in his own programme in 2027. In August he emailed Rupert that Lodestar was “refusing essential data”.
  • Phase 2 can't be designed without his budget, and Rupert listens to him.
  • Callum's suggestion: “keep our heads down and go through Elaine”. That's how an opponent stays an opponent.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Gareth

Gareth has spent years building Network Assets and believes the meter data is the heart of MetroVolt's future (he's not wrong). He resents a supplier from Leeds being the face of “customer data”. Credit and control matter to him more than any particular design.

“My team built the meter data. Lodestar gets the headlines.”

Wrenfield Ines

Wrenfield needs Gareth's budget for phase 2 and his team's cooperation for the meter feed. It can't buy his support and mustn't flatter him. It can offer something he genuinely wants: ownership, credit and a seat at the design table.

“Make him a co-owner, not a hurdle.”

Most opponents aren't against your project; they're for something your project threatens. Find that thing.

3Real-life analogy the neighbour who owns the access road

You want to build on your land, and the only access road belongs to a neighbour who objected to your plans. You could fight him through lawyers for years. Or you visit, ask what he needs (the road resurfaced, his name on the new sign), agree to share the road and its upkeep, and write it into a deed. Next year he's showing visitors round.

4How it works meet early, ask, share the win, credit, write it down

Meet early, alone. Ines asks for 30 minutes on floor 20 on 16 November, through Elaine. She brings no slides. Her first question: “What would make phase 2 good for Network Assets?”

Listen for the need. Gareth talks for twenty minutes: his team built the meter register; Lodestar's slides show “Lodestar” next to meter data; Network Assets is treated as a feed, not a partner. Underneath: credit and a seat at the design table.

Find the shared win. Ines proposes a meter data service, owned and run by Network Assets, that Lodestar and other systems use, designed jointly for phase 2. It replaces COPYMTR's file drop with something Gareth's team owns. It's also good architecture.

Credit in public, and in writing. From that week, every Lodestar slide showing meter data says “Network Assets meter data service”. The arrangement becomes interface agreement IA-02, signed by Gareth on 30 November. At the December steering, Gareth presents the meter data service himself. Rupert notices.

  1. Meet early, alone, without slides

    Through your counterpart.

  2. Ask what they need

    Then listen for twenty minutes.

  3. Find the shared win

    Ownership of something real.

  4. Give credit in public

    On every slide, in every meeting.

  5. Write it down

    An agreement they sign.

  6. Let them present it

    Their win, their stage.

Vocabulary

Budget holder
The person who controls the money for an area.
Shared win
An outcome good for both sides' goals.
Co-ownership
Giving another party real ownership of part of the solution.
Interface agreement
A written agreement on how two systems and teams connect.
Credit
Public recognition of who did or owns something.
Opponent
Someone working against your project, usually for reasons of their own.

the meter service is now a dependency with an owner who wants it to work.

In plain words: turning a hidden feed into a service owned by the stakeholder who cares most about it improves both the architecture and the politics, and the agreement makes it last.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Avoiding the opponent

    They stay an opponent, with power. Fix → meet early.

  • Going around them

    They escalate again. Fix → through your counterpart, then directly.

  • Flattery

    They see through it. Fix → ask what they need.

  • Keeping the credit

    You win the slide, lose the budget. Fix → give it away.

  • Verbal deals

    Forgotten at the next reorg. Fix → signed, by them.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • An opponent gains budget or authority over something you need.
  • Before a tender they'll influence.
  • A dependency owned by a reluctant team.

Skip it when…

  • The budget holder is already a supporter: keep them informed.
