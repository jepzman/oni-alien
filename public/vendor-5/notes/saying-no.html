How Do We Say No to a Client?

TL;DR

Saying no to a client is part of serving them, if you do it well. Say no to the request, yes to the need behind it, and make an offer they can say yes to. Give the reason in one sentence, and put it in writing. Free work to win future work is the most expensive gift a vendor can give, especially when it compromises a fair procurement.

  • Lives in: email to Elaine, 2 Oct 2028 · CR-021

1The situation The Grid, 2 October 2028

One week into hypercare, Rupert's office asks Elaine to ask Ines: could Wrenfield start designing wave 2 now, in parallel with hypercare, “as goodwill”, at no cost? The phase 2 tender in March could then say “design already under way”.

  • Wave 2 isn't contracted. Phase 2 will be tendered, and Vexmoor will bid.
  • Hypercare has the whole team on 380,000 newly moved customers. Hana is still working weekends.
  • Graham, from Leeds: “Do it. It'll help us win phase 2.”

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Rupert's office

Rupert wants Ascend to look fast. A tender that says “design already under way” sounds like momentum. Asking the current supplier for goodwill work is normal; most say yes, hoping to win.

“Show some goodwill. You want phase 2, don't you?”

Wrenfield Ines

Free design work would take people off hypercare, cost money Wrenfield won't recover, and, worst, give Wrenfield an unfair head start in a tender: Desmond would have to exclude it or share it. A yes would hurt everyone it was meant to help.

“A yes here is bad for hypercare, bad for margin and bad for the tender.”

Goodwill work is never free. Someone pays: your team, your margin, or the fairness of the next procurement.

3Real-life analogy the doctor and the prescription

A patient asks a doctor for antibiotics for a cold. A good doctor says no to the prescription and yes to the need (feeling better, getting back to work), explains why in a sentence, and offers what will actually help. The patient leaves without what they asked for, and better looked after.

4How it works no to the request, yes to the need, an offer

Find the need. Ines asks Elaine one question: “What does Rupert need the tender to show?” Answer: that phase 2 is ready to start in May, not in October.

Say no to the request, with the reason in one sentence. Her email to Elaine: “We can't start wave 2 design during hypercare, or for free: it would take people off 380,000 newly moved customers, and it would give us an advantage in the phase 2 tender that Desmond would have to exclude.”

Say yes to the need, with an offer. “What we can do: a two-week planning sprint in early December, after hypercare ends, paid from the change pool (£42k), producing a wave 2 readiness pack (data profile for wave 2's districts, lessons from wave 1, a migration timeline) that MetroVolt owns and gives to every bidder.”

Desmond is delighted: a readiness pack for all bidders makes the tender stronger and fairer. Rupert approves CR-021. Graham isn't delighted; Ines explains that the pack goes to Vexmoor too, which is exactly why it doesn't disqualify Wrenfield.

  1. Ask what the need is

    Behind the request.

  2. Say no to the request

    Clearly, without hedging.

  3. Give the reason in one sentence

    About them, not you.

  4. Make an offer that meets the need

    Specific, priced, dated.

  5. Put it in writing

    One short email.

  6. Check the offer is fair to others

    Especially near a procurement.

Vocabulary

No, but
Refusing a request while offering something that meets the need.
Goodwill work
Free work offered to please a client, often hoping for future business.
Unfair advantage
Knowledge or work one bidder has that others don't.
Readiness pack
Material given to all bidders so they start equal.
Need versus request
What someone wants underneath what they ask for.
Exclusion
Removing a bidder from a procurement because of a conflict or advantage.

5What the contract says why free work would have been a problem

Lodestar SOW · §7 and the phase 2 procurement rulesOct 2028
no, but

7.6 No Change is binding unless recorded in the Change Register and approved under this clause.

free work outside the register isn't goodwill. it's an untracked change.

Phase 2 ITT (draft), 3.2 Information provided to one bidder about the requirement shall be provided to all bidders.

Desmond's rule. the readiness pack follows it.

In plain words: free work near a procurement can create an unfair advantage the client must remove. Offering paid work that produces something every bidder receives keeps the tender fair and your bid eligible.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • A tired yes

    Free work, burned team, procurement risk. Fix → no to the request, yes to the need.

  • A bare no

    The client feels refused. Fix → an offer that meets the need.

  • Reasons about you

    “We're too busy.” Fix → reasons about them: their customers, their tender.

  • A verbal no

    Forgotten, or misquoted. Fix → one short email.

  • Ignoring fairness

    Your bid gets excluded. Fix → something every bidder receives.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Requests for free or out-of-scope work.
  • Requests during critical phases.
  • Anything near a procurement.

Skip it when…

  • The request is small, in scope and harmless: just do it, and record it.
