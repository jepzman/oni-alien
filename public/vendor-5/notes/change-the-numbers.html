What If the Client Asks Us to Change the Numbers?

TL;DR

Sooner or later a client will ask you to make the numbers say something they don't. Refuse the misstatement, clearly and privately, the same day. Offer an accurate way to say what they need to say; there's almost always one. Put both in writing. Don't sign anything untrue, whatever it costs the next contract. Most people who ask this are under pressure, not dishonest, and take the accurate version when it's offered.

1The situation The Grid, floor 27, 16 January 2029

Rupert is preparing the Ascend board paper for February. On Tuesday 16 January he asks Ines to sign the Lodestar benefits section, which says: “Lodestar has realised £1.9m a year of mainframe savings.”

  • £1.9m a year is MAVIS's total mainframe cost, saved only when all four waves are done (about 2031).
  • Wave 1 retired about 16 % of the batch load; realised savings so far are about £210k a year.
  • Phase 2's decision is Rupert's. Ines's signature would be under a number she knows is false.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Rupert

Rupert's Ascend targets are aggressive, and the board wants savings now. “£1.9m a year” is a real number; he's telling himself the tense doesn't matter. He wants the supplier's signature because it makes the claim look independent.

“It's going to be true. Why not say it now?”

Wrenfield Ines

Refusing a sponsor who decides phase 2, two months before the tender, could cost Wrenfield £9m. Signing would put Wrenfield's name under a false statement to a regulated company's board, and the board would find out by 2030. Ines decides in about a minute. The rest is how.

“There's no version of this where I sign a false number.”

The ask is rarely “lie for me”. It's “say it a bit more positively”, from someone under pressure. The answer is the same: the true version, said well.

3Real-life analogy the surveyor's valuation

A seller asks the surveyor to value the house “as it will be” after the planned extension. A good surveyor says no to the false figure, and offers the true one: “Current value £320,000; estimated £380,000 once the extension is built.” Both numbers are useful to a buyer. Only one of them can carry her signature today.

4How it works refuse, offer, write it down, don't sign

Refuse, privately, the same day. Ines asks Rupert for ten minutes that afternoon, alone. “I can't sign that sentence: £1.9m is what all four waves will save; wave 1 has realised about £210k a year. If the board reads it as realised, it will be wrong.”

Offer an accurate version that still serves him. “Lodestar is on track to remove MAVIS's mainframe cost of £1.9m a year when all waves complete; wave 1 has already realised about £210k a year and retired 16 % of the batch load.” It's a good story: it shows momentum and a big prize. Ines also offers the calculation, sourced, for the appendix.

Write it down. A short email to Rupert at 17:12, copied to Elaine: the original sentence, why Wrenfield can't sign it, the accurate wording, the calculation attached.

Don't sign anything untrue, and know your escalation. If Rupert had insisted, Ines would have told Graham the same day, cited the accuracy clause (§14.3), and declined to sign, whatever it did to phase 2. She didn't need to. Rupert replied at 19:40: “Use your wording.” The February board paper is accurate.

  1. Decide quickly that you won't

    The how is harder than the whether.

  2. Refuse privately, the same day

    No audience, no delay.

  3. Offer an accurate version

    That still meets their need.

  4. Write it down

    Original, reason, alternative, sources.

  5. Don't sign anything untrue

    Whatever it costs.

  6. Know your escalation

    Your own leadership, the contract's accuracy clause, advice if needed.

Vocabulary

Misstatement
A statement that is false or misleading.
Realised benefit
A benefit already achieved, not forecast.
Forecast benefit
A benefit expected in future, with assumptions.
Sign-off
A formal confirmation that something is correct.
Integrity
Doing what's right when it costs you.
Escalation of an ethical concern
Taking a refusal or concern to people with authority, in writing.

5What the contract says the clause behind the refusal

Lodestar SOW · §14.3signed 30 June 2027
refused

14.3 Each Party shall ensure information it provides for the other's governance is accurate.

a signature under £1.9m “realised” would breach this clause, and something much older than it.

Wrenfield code of conduct, 2.1 We do not sign, certify or present information we know or suspect to be inaccurate.

Graham's own code. I'd have reminded him, if needed.

In plain words: an accuracy clause in the contract gives you a neutral, professional reason to refuse, so the conversation is about the clause and not about anyone's honesty.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Signing to keep the peace

    Your name under a false number. Fix → don't sign.

  • Refusing in public

    The sponsor loses face; the fight starts. Fix → privately, same day.

  • Refusing without an alternative

    They have to write something. Fix → offer an accurate version.

  • Not writing it down

    Later, nobody remembers you refused. Fix → an email with sources.

  • Waiting

    The paper goes to print. Fix → the same day.

10When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Anyone asks you to state, sign or present something untrue.
  • Benefits, progress or test results are being “rounded up”.
  • Your signature is wanted to make a claim look independent.

Skip it when…

  • Never skip it.
