Aggregates & Rollups

TL;DR

When dashboards keep summing billions of rows to show the same daily and monthly totals, pre-compute them at coarser grain: sales by shop, category and day; then by region and month. Additive measures (sums, counts) roll up safely; distinct counts need sketches such as HyperLogLog. Rebuild only the periods that changed, and let queries use the smallest table that can answer.

  • Family: serving reports
  • Also called: rollups, summary tables, cubes, pre-aggregations
  • Lives in: materialized views, ClickHouse, Druid, Cube
  • Pairs with: materialized views, fact table types

1The problem why do we even need this?

The executive dashboard sums 2 billion order lines every time it opens. It takes 40 seconds; on Monday at 09:00 three hundred managers open it at once, queries queue, and the warehouse bill spikes. The answers are the same daily totals as an hour ago.

2Real-life analogy till Z-reports

At closing, every till prints a Z-report: the day's totals. Head office adds up thirty Z-reports, not every receipt, and still keeps the receipts in case someone asks about one sale.

3How it solves it coarser tables, built from finer ones

Choose grains from the questions: most dashboards ask by day, shop and category, or by month and region. Build each aggregate from the level below it, incrementally by day, and rebuild the days a batch touched (late data). Sums and counts roll up; averages must be stored as sum and count; distinct counts (unique customers) don't add up, so store a HyperLogLog sketch per row and merge sketches.

Point dashboards at the right level, or let the BI tool or metrics layer pick it automatically (“aggregate awareness”).

  1. Find the slow, frequent queries

    Group them by the dimensions and grain they ask for.

  2. Design a few aggregates

    Usually daily and monthly, at the dimensions dashboards use.

  3. Store additive parts

    Sums and counts; sum and count for averages; sketches for distinct counts.

  4. Refresh incrementally

    Rebuild the days touched by each batch, idempotently.

  5. Route queries

    Point dashboards at aggregates, or use aggregate awareness; test totals against the fact.

agg_sales_daily.sqlgeneric SQL · BigQuery-style HLL
-- rebuild only the days the latest batch touched
DELETE FROM agg_sales_daily WHERE order_date IN (SELECT d FROM touched_days);
INSERT INTO agg_sales_daily
SELECT order_date, shop_key, category,
       sum(amount_net)                       AS revenue,
       count(*)                              AS lines,
       count(DISTINCT order_id)              AS orders,
       HLL_COUNT.INIT(customer_key)          AS customers_sketch   -- mergeable
FROM   fct_sales JOIN dim_product USING (product_key)
WHERE  order_date IN (SELECT d FROM touched_days)
GROUP  BY 1, 2, 3;

-- monthly, from daily: sums add, sketches merge
SELECT date_trunc(order_date, MONTH) AS month, category,
       sum(revenue) AS revenue,
       HLL_COUNT.MERGE(customers_sketch) AS unique_customers
FROM   agg_sales_daily GROUP BY 1, 2;

tools: materialized views (BigQuery, Snowflake, Redshift, PostgreSQL), ClickHouse projections and AggregatingMergeTree, Apache Druid and Pinot rollup at ingestion, Cube pre-aggregations, Looker aggregate awareness, Power BI aggregations, HyperLogLog functions in most warehouses.

4Gains vs losses nothing is free

What we gain

  • Fast dashboards: thousands of rows instead of billions.
  • Lower cost: scan once per load, not per view.
  • Predictable load on Monday mornings.
  • Detail kept for drill-down.

What we lose

  • More tables to keep consistent with the fact.
  • Fixed grains: a new dimension means a new aggregate.
  • Distinct counts become approximate (sketches).
  • Refresh lag between fact and aggregate.

6Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Averaging averages

    Monthly average order value is the mean of daily averages. Fix → store revenue and orders; divide at query time.

  • Summing distinct counts

    Unique customers per day summed for the month count regulars many times. Fix → store sketches and merge them, or count from the fact.

  • Full rebuild every hour

    The aggregate rereads two billion rows each time. Fix → rebuild only touched days.

  • Aggregate drift

    A fix to the fact is never reflected in the aggregate. Fix → build aggregates in the same DAG after the fact, and test totals match.

7When to use it (and when not)

Use it when…

  • Dashboards that many people open often.
  • Fixed, common grains (day, month, shop, region).
  • Very large fact tables.
  • Cost or latency limits on the warehouse.

Skip it when…

  • Ad-hoc analysis that needs any dimension.
  • Small facts that scan in a second anyway.
  • Exact distinct counts that can't be approximate.

8Self-check quiz

Why can't you sum daily unique customers to get monthly ones?

Customers who bought on several days would be counted several times; use sketches or count from the fact.

How should an aggregate store averages?

As the sum and the count, so averages at any level are computed correctly.

What is aggregate awareness?

A tool picking the smallest aggregate table that can answer a query.

How do aggregates handle late data?

Rebuild the days a batch touched, the same as the fact.

what would you do?The regional dashboard needs “unique customers per region per month”, and the fact has 2 billion rows. What do you store in the daily aggregate?

A HyperLogLog sketch of customer keys per shop per day, merged to region and month at query time (or in a monthly aggregate). It's small, mergeable, and accurate to about 1 %, which is usually fine for a dashboard; an exact count can come from the fact when needed.

try it

Time your slowest dashboard query. Build a daily aggregate for its dimensions, point the query at it, and time it again. Then check that the totals match the fact for a few days.
