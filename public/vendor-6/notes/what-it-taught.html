What Did the Vendor Side Teach Me?

TL;DR

Lessons blur fast once the next project starts. At the end of a long engagement, write down what you learned while it's fresh, as rules you'd give your younger self: short, specific, each tied to a moment you can remember. Then share them with someone starting out. This note is Ines's list: ten rules from two and a half years on the client's floor.

1The situation Floor 19, 13 June 2029

Ines spent fifteen years in-house at Aldermoor Water before joining Wrenfield. She has now spent two and a half years on the other side of the table: about 470 late notes since February 2027.

  • Yesterday MetroVolt published its Ascend 2030 memo: “30 % more output with 20 % fewer people”. The lifts have talked of nothing else.
  • The phase 2 decision is due in two days.
  • Lottie asks: “If you could tell yourself one thing in 2027, what would it be?” Ines can't answer in one.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt the in-house Ines

The architect Ines used to be thought suppliers were slow, expensive and always selling. She wrote tenders full of “absorb” and fixed prices, and thought change requests were a trick.

“Why can't they just deliver what we asked for?”

Wrenfield the vendor Ines

The architect Ines is now knows what's behind the price, the change request and the status colour. She also knows which suspicions were right: some suppliers do sell first and think later. Vivian did, once.

“Both sides were right about each other. Some of the time.”

Anyone who has worked both sides of the table makes a better client and a better supplier.

3Real-life analogy the travel journal

Travellers who keep a journal remember the trip; the others remember the photos. The best entries are written on the last evening, before the flight home: not what you saw, but what you'd tell a friend going next year. Pack light. Learn ten words. The cheap hotel near the station isn't worth it. That page is the one that gets photocopied.

4How it works ten rules, each from a moment

  1. Read the contract before the code. (Vol. 2: the SOW clauses that later decided every argument.)
  2. Bad news early, in writing. (Vol. 3: the 7.8 % data surprise; Elaine's quote.)
  3. Price what you know; say what you don't. (Vol. 1: the assumptions page; vol. 6: £9.4m.)
  4. Every promise goes in a register. (Vol. 1: Vivian's dinner; 47 of 47.)
  5. Find the person who really knows. There's always a Bert. (Vol. 3: 64 pages of Bert's book.)
  6. The client's politics are weather: read them, don't make them. (Vol. 5: Priya, Rupert, the lift.)
  7. Never change the numbers. (Vol. 5: £210k, not £1.9m.)
  8. Your pace is your team's ceiling. (Vol. 6: Hana, and the 23:30 notes.)
  9. Decide what you won't accept before you want the deal. (Vol. 6: “absorb”, twice.)
  10. Leave the floor as if you'll be back. (Vol. 6: 31 letters.)

She gives Lottie a copy and tapes another inside the notebook's back cover. Lottie's question gets an answer after all: “Number eight. I learned it last.”

  1. Write it at the end, while it's fresh

    Before the next project.

  2. Make each lesson a rule

    Short enough to remember.

  3. Tie each rule to a moment

    So it's believable.

  4. Keep it to about ten

    Nobody reads fifty.

  5. Share it with someone starting out

    That's where it's useful.

  6. Reread it at the next start

    And add one.

5What the contract says the clause I'd write if I were the client again

Ines's ideal SOW · §1 (a wish)13 Jun 2029
not in any contract

1.1 The Supplier shall absorb all risks … The Parties shall share known risks openly, price them honestly, and tell each other bad news early.

no lawyer would sign it. both teams would work better under it.

1.2 Each Party shall treat the other's people as it would wish its own to be treated.

Elaine did this without a clause.

In plain words: the best contracts don't create trust; they make it cheaper to keep. The rest is people.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Not writing it down

    It blurs in a month. Fix → tonight.

  • Fifty lessons

    None remembered. Fix → ten.

  • Abstract lessons

    Nobody believes them. Fix → tie each to a moment.

  • Keeping it to yourself

    Wasted. Fix → share it.

  • Leaving out the uncomfortable one

    It's the useful one. Fix → write it first.
