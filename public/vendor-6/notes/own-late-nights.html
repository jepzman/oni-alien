What About My Own 23:00 Notes?

TL;DR

A team's pace is set by its leader, whatever the leader says. If you work until midnight, your team learns that's the job. Notice your own signs with the same honesty you'd give anyone else, say them out loud to someone, set limits you'll actually keep and make them visible, and delegate what others can carry. You can't ask your team to rest while they watch you not resting.

  • Family: the cost
  • Lives in: this notebook (the late notes' times)
  • Pairs with: stress on a vendor team, sustainable pace

1The situation Leeds, Sunday 21 January 2029

On a Sunday in Leeds, Ines reads back through her own notebook. Since February 2027 she has written about 400 late notes, the ones at the end of every note in this book.

  • The times have crept: around 21:00 in early 2027, 22:30 by mid-2028, 23:30 in December.
  • Eighteen months of four hotel nights a week near The Grid.
  • She missed her sister's birthday in November for the hypercare review.
  • Eleven days ago she told Hana to rest.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Elaine

Elaine has noticed Ines's emails arriving at 23:00 and has started replying at 23:05. Neither of them asked for that. Clients copy the pace of the vendor's lead as surely as the vendor's team does.

“If she's working at eleven, I'd better be too.”

Wrenfield Ines

Ines can see Hana's signs clearly and her own not at all, until she reads them back in her own handwriting. The team watches what she does, not what she says. Graham calls her “unstoppable” as a compliment.

“I've been teaching the team that this is normal.”

The most dangerous stress on a team is the leader's, because everyone copies it and nobody feels able to point it out.

3Real-life analogy the oxygen mask

The safety card on a plane says: put on your own oxygen mask before helping others. Not because you matter more, but because if you pass out, you can't help anyone. A leader running on empty helps nobody for long, and teaches everyone that running on empty is the job.

4How it works notice, say it, set visible limits, delegate

Notice, honestly. Ines writes down her own signs as if they were Hana's: late-note times, hotel nights, the missed birthday, sleep. Worked-to-booked ratio: 1.4 for eighteen months.

Say it aloud. On Monday 22 January she tells Callum, then Graham: “I've been working at a pace I'd never accept for anyone on my team. I'm changing it, and I need your help to keep it changed.” Graham's reply, after a pause: “I've been calling that unstoppable. Sorry.”

Set visible limits. Three hotel nights a week, not four. Fridays in Leeds. Phone off at 21:00, except severity 1. The notebook closes at 22:00. She tells Elaine, who laughs and stops replying at 23:05.

Delegate. Callum leads the phase 2 commercial work; Bilal leads the data estimates; Lottie runs the joint lessons session. Ines keeps the architecture and the client.

The late notes in the rest of this volume are written before 22:00. Mostly.

  1. Read your own signs as if they were someone else's

    Times, nights, missed things.

  2. Say them to someone who'll hold you to it

    Your deputy and your boss.

  3. Set limits you can keep

    Specific: nights, times, days.

  4. Make them visible

    Tell the client and the team.

  5. Delegate real work

    Not just tasks: decisions.

  6. Check back in a month

    Did the limits hold?

5What the contract says what the contract didn't require

Lodestar SOW · Schedule 9 and Wrenfield working-time policyJan 2029
never required

Sch. 9, 4.1 During warranty, the Supplier shall respond to Severity 1 defects within 1 hour …

severity 1 only. nothing about 23:00 emails on a Tuesday.

Wrenfield policy 3.2 Staff should not regularly work more than 48 hours a week, averaged over 17 weeks.

the UK limit. I'd been over it, on average, for 18 months.

In plain words: very little of a lead's overwork is required by the contract. It's habit, fear and example. Check what's actually promised; most of the rest can stop.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Seeing others' signs, not your own

    You're the last to know. Fix → write yours down as if they were theirs.

  • Private limits

    Nobody helps you keep them. Fix → tell the team and the client.

  • Delegating tasks, not decisions

    You're still the bottleneck. Fix → hand over decisions.

  • Calling overwork commitment

    Everyone copies it. Fix → call it what it is.

  • Waiting for the project to end

    It doesn't end; the next one starts. Fix → change now.

What did the late-note times show?

They crept from about 21:00 in 2027 to 23:30 in December 2028.

Why does a leader's pace matter so much?

The team, and even the client, copy it.

What limits did Ines set?

Three hotel nights, Fridays in Leeds, phone off at 21:00, notebook closed at 22:00.

What did she delegate?

Phase 2 commercial work to Callum, data estimates to Bilal, the lessons session to Lottie.

Who did she tell, and why?

Callum, Graham, Elaine and the team, so they'd help keep the limits.

what would you do?Two weeks later, a severity 3 defect arrives at 21:30 and Ines feels the urge to look. What does she do?

Leave it: severity 3 waits for morning under the warranty terms. Phone off. The limit only works if it holds when it's tempting.

try it

Look back at your own last month: when did you stop working each day? How many weekends? Write your signs as if they were a colleague's. Pick one limit, tell two people, and keep it for a month.

remember!!

Put your own mask on first. Your pace is your team's ceiling.
