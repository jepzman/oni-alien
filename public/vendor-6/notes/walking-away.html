When Should We Walk Away?

TL;DR

The time to decide what you won't accept is before you want the deal. Write walk-away conditions early (margin floor, liability, risk you won't absorb, a pace you won't break), test every new demand against them calmly, offer real alternatives, and if the deal still fails, leave politely, in writing, with the door open. Walking away from the wrong deal is part of winning the right ones.

1The situation Leeds, 31 May 2029

On Thursday 24 May, Desmond sends both bidders a best and final offer request. It carries Rupert's office's fingerprints:

  • Reduce the price by 12 %.
  • The supplier shall “absorb all risk arising from the quality of customer data”.
  • Unlimited liability for errors in migrated data.

“Absorb” is the word Ines had removed from draft v0.3 of the first contract in June 2027. Replies are due Friday 1 June. Graham badly wants phase 2.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt Rupert's office and Desmond

Rupert's office wants a cheaper, lower-risk phase 2 for MetroVolt, and perhaps a test of which bidder will bend furthest. Desmond has to put the same request to both bidders and record the answers. Elaine wasn't consulted on the wording.

“Let's see who wants it most.”

Wrenfield Ines, Callum and Graham

At 12 % off, the margin is about 4 %. Unlimited liability for data errors, on 1.9 million accounts already known to be messy, could sink an 80-person firm. Wrenfield needs phase 2. It needs to still exist more.

“We decided this in February, when we were calm.”

Demands at the last minute are a test of nerve. Conditions written in calm weather are how you pass it.

3Real-life analogy the house you'd love to buy

Sensible house-buyers decide their top price before the viewing, because after you've seen the garden you'll want to pay anything. When the seller's agent rings at the last minute asking for £30,000 more and the buyer to pay for the new roof, they check the number they wrote down, make a fair counter-offer, and are ready to say no and keep looking. Sometimes the seller comes back. Sometimes they don't, and that was the right house to lose.

4How it works conditions in calm weather, a calm test, alternatives, a polite letter

Conditions set in calm weather. On 14 February, before the bid, Ines, Callum and Graham wrote four walk-away conditions and all three signed them: margin no lower than 15 %; liability capped at the contract value; Wrenfield never absorbs data-quality risk alone; a plan at 85 % of capacity.

A calm test. On 30 May they test the request against them, one line each. It fails three of four.

Real alternatives. The reply offers what Wrenfield can do: 4 % off, by moving the business self-service portal into wave 4 and reusing wave 1's reconciliation tools; a shared data-quality pot (£400k, drawn down jointly, with the rules from CR-007); liability capped at the contract value, as in wave 1.

Ready to leave, politely. The last paragraph: “We would rather withdraw than accept unlimited liability or absorb data-quality risk alone. We would be sorry to do so; wave 1 has been the best work our firm has done, and we'd hand it over carefully whoever delivers phase 2.”

Graham reads it twice in Leeds on Thursday 31 May, says “Send it. I mean it.”, and signs it. Submitted Friday at 10:15. Desmond's reply on 4 June: “Received. Noted.” Nothing else. They don't know what Vexmoor answered.

  1. Write walk-away conditions early

    Before you want the deal; signed by the boss.

  2. Test each demand calmly

    One line each: pass or fail.

  3. Offer real alternatives

    What you can do, priced.

  4. Say clearly what you won't do

    And why.

  5. Be ready to withdraw

    Politely, in writing.

  6. Leave the door open

    Offer a careful handover either way.

Vocabulary

Walk-away conditions
Terms agreed in advance that would make you decline a deal.
BATNA
Best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what you'll do if this fails.
BAFO
Best and final offer, requested late in a procurement.
Unlimited liability
No cap on what the supplier might owe for failures.
Risk transfer
Moving a risk from one party to another by contract.
Winner's curse
Winning by accepting terms that make the deal a loss.

unlimited liability is a bet-the-firm clause. we're 80 people.

In plain words: late requests to absorb risk or remove liability caps are common in BAFOs. Answer with a redline, a priced alternative and a calm statement of what you won't sign.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • No conditions in advance

    Fear negotiates for you. Fix → write them in calm weather.

  • Conditions only you know

    The boss overrules you at 17:00. Fix → signed by the boss.

  • Just saying no

    Looks like sulking. Fix → alternatives, priced.

  • Threatening to withdraw

    Sounds like a bluff. Fix → a polite statement, and mean it.

  • Burning the bridge

    Wave 1 still needs you. Fix → offer a careful handover.

10When to use it (and when not)

Skip it when…
