How Do We Notice Stress on a Vendor Team?

TL;DR

Vendor teams on fixed-price work hide stress well, because admitting it feels like letting the client down. Watch the quiet signs: hours, tone, silence, missed weekends, “fine”. Ask directly and privately, with time to listen. Then change the work, not just the mood: leave, hours, rotation. A team member who burns out on your account is your failure, not theirs.

1The situation The Grid, January 2029

Hana Sato has been Lodestar's tech lead since the bid. She worked nineteen hours on wave 1 night. In December she cried in the stairwell and said she was fine. In January:

  • Her code commits arrive at 01:00, three nights a week.
  • She's been sharp with Lottie twice in a week, which she has never been.
  • She eats lunch at her desk every day and hasn't had a weekend off since August.
  • Every “how are you?” gets “fine”.

2Both sides of the table the same moment, seen twice

MetroVolt MetroVolt

MetroVolt sees Hana as Lodestar's best engineer: calm, brilliant, always available. Elaine has noticed she looks tired. Nobody at MetroVolt sees it as their place to say anything; Hana works for Wrenfield.

“She's the one who always fixes it.”

Wrenfield Ines

Ines has known for weeks and done nothing beyond worry. Hana is named in the contract as key personnel, the phase 2 bid is in eight weeks, and Hana is the best engineer Wrenfield has. None of that matters more than Hana.

“I've been treating her “fine” as an answer. It's a symptom.”

Burnout isn't a personal weakness; it's what sustained overload does to anyone. On a vendor team it's usually the most committed person who goes first.

3Real-life analogy the warning lights on a long drive

On a long drive, nobody wants to stop. The car keeps going while small warning lights come on: oil low, a tyre soft. A good driver doesn't wait for the engine to seize. She pulls over, checks, and changes something: oil, a tyre, a rest. Ignoring small lights is how a long journey ends at the side of the road.

4How it works watch, ask, listen, change the work

Watch the quiet signs. Ines looks at what's visible without prying: the timesheet strip (Hana's worked-to-booked ratio is 1.5 for five months), commit times, who she eats with, how she speaks to people.

Ask directly, privately, with time. On Wednesday 10 January, in a booked room, with an hour: “You've worked every weekend since August, and I've let it happen. I'm worried about you. How are you, really?”

Listen without fixing. Hana talks for forty minutes. She isn't sleeping. She feels she's the only one who understands the back-feed and the MetroBus adapters. She's afraid of letting the team down before phase 2.

Change the work, not just the mood. They agree, with Hana leading: two weeks' leave from 15 January, phone off; then reduced hours for a week; then she rotates off Lodestar on 5 February (next note but two) to a smaller client in Leeds. Elaine agrees to the key-personnel change the same day. Hana asks one thing: to be asked, not told, about phase 2.

Check on everyone else. Ines has the same private conversation with each of the team that week. Oskar is tired but fine. Lottie says she's fine and means it. Callum admits he's not sleeping either.

  1. Watch the quiet signs

    Hours, tone, silence, weekends.

  2. Ask directly and privately

    With time; name what you've seen.

  3. Listen without fixing

    Let them describe it.

  4. Change the work

    Leave, hours, rotation; agreed with them.

  5. Deal with the contract side

    Key personnel, client consent.

  6. Check on everyone

    The quiet ones too.

Vocabulary

Burnout
Exhaustion, cynicism and reduced effectiveness from prolonged work stress.
Presenteeism
Working while unwell or exhausted, at reduced effectiveness.
Key-person risk
Dependence on one person; it increases their load.
Worked-to-booked ratio
Hours actually worked divided by hours booked.
Check-in
A short private conversation about how someone is.
Duty of care
An employer's responsibility for its people's health.

5What the contract says the key-personnel clause, used kindly

Lodestar SOW · §6.3signed 30 June 2027
consent given

6.3 The Supplier shall not remove or replace Key Personnel without MetroVolt's prior written consent, not to be unreasonably withheld.

Elaine consented within an hour. “Of course. Look after her.”

6.4 Replacement Key Personnel shall have equivalent skills and a handover of at least five Working Days.

Tariq: a week's handover, then Bilal and Ines behind him.

In plain words: key-personnel clauses can make overloaded people feel trapped. The clause allows replacement with consent; ask early, and most clients agree when wellbeing is the reason.

9Common pitfalls the usual ways this goes wrong

  • Accepting “fine”

    The signs say otherwise. Fix → ask directly, with time.

  • Changing the mood, not the work

    Pizza doesn't fix overload. Fix → leave, hours, rotation.

  • Deciding for them

    They feel punished. Fix → agree it, with them leading.

  • Only checking on the obvious one

    Others hide it better. Fix → everyone.

  • Waiting for a quiet moment

    There isn't one. Fix → now.

11Self-check quiz

Name three quiet signs Ines noticed.

01:00 commits, being sharp with Lottie, no weekends since August (also lunch at her desk, “fine”).

How did Ines start the conversation?

Directly, privately, naming what she'd seen and her own part in it.

What changed in Hana's work?

Two weeks' leave, reduced hours, then rotation off Lodestar on 5 February.

How did the contract allow it?

Key personnel can be replaced with MetroVolt's consent (§6.3); Elaine consented the same day.

Why check on everyone?

Others may be hiding it better; Callum was not sleeping either.

what would you do?Graham says: “Can't Hana just push through until the bid is in?” What does Ines say?

No: she's already past the point where pushing through is safe. The bid can be written without her; she can't be replaced if she burns out. Wellbeing first, and Elaine has already agreed.

try it

Pick one person on your team who's always “fine”. Look at what you can see: hours, weekends, tone. If you're worried, book an hour and ask directly. What could change in their work, not just their mood?

remember!!

“Fine” is not an answer. Watch the quiet signs, ask directly, and change the work.
